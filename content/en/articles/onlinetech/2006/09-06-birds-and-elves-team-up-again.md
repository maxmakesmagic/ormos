I briefly touched upon the deck last week, mistakenly attributing it to the player (guys18) who posted a Premier Event Top 8 finish with the deck then. The deck was actually designed by Ken Ishimaru, who finished in the Top 8 of the Japanese Nationals with his creation, posting a 6-3-1 Standard record overall. The goal of the deck is to accelerate into a turn 2 Hypnotic Specter or Ohran Viper with the help of Birds of Paradise and Llanowar Elves. When this deck wins the die roll and gets out a turn 2 Specter or Viper, a control deck will have basically lost right there. Counterspells can't touch it yet and a Wrath of God is too slow. That is the main strength of the deck.

The deck can also easily win even without those ridiculous unbeatable starts. For example, turn 2 Dark Confidant, turn 3 Hypnotic Specter, turn 4 Giant Solifuge is a fine start as well. A control deck has to deal with every single one of those guys immediately, otherwise they're in trouble. A control player cannot allow a Dark Confidant of Hypnotic Specter to live for more than one turn, otherwise the card advantage they provide will be too much to overcome. Every single one of the creatures in the deck – apart from the Birds and Elves – is a major threat on its own and that's a great thing. And while a control player is busy dealing with your Confidants, Specters, and Vipers, you can usually sneak through some points with a Genju of the Cedars or Giant Solifuge. That can put your opponent down to a low life total, at which point Demonfire finishes the job.

Against other creature decks, the plan is to go for the long game and win the Umezawa's Jitte war. Most creature mirror matches are ultimately decided by the powerful equipment. Usually, the player that manages to keep an Umezawa's Jitte in play will win in the end. Since this deck has a bunch of card drawers in Ohran Viper and Dark Confidant, you should draw more cards (hence, more Umezawa's Jittes) than your opponent and as a result, you should be able to win the game eventually.

Why the snow-covered lands, you might ask? No clue. Perhaps they were in there to trick the opponent, perhaps making him believe you play Skred or Scrying Sheets. But they can also backfire. In case your opponent plays Rimefeather Owl (or perhaps Zombie Musher or a stray Ronom Serpent…), you're going to hit yourself in the head for playing snow-covered basics.

The sideboard allows you to tweak the deck to your liking. Carven Caryatid comes in against fast creature decks, Deathmark is very cheap answer to Rumbling Slum or Patagia Viper, Distress is a fine choice against control or combo decks, Indrik Stomphowler comes in against Snakes to destroy Umezawa's Jitte or Coat of Arms, Crime // Punishment is an all-round answer to anything, and the singleton Giant Solifuge comes in against control decks.

I think the deck is a decent choice. It has a game plan (turn 2 Hypnotic Specter or Ohran Viper) and it is well-equipped to execute it. However, after playing the deck a bunch of games, I have found two problems. First, the only plays 22 lands, and combined with the easily killed Elves and Birds, you will run into a mana screw quite often. Or color screw, because the deck doesn't have that many red sources for Demonfire. Second, the deck performs significantly worse against control decks after sideboard. Once your opponent puts in Last Gasp and assorted cheap removal, he can answer your turn 2 Hypnotic Specter quickly and easily, thwarting your game plan. If you can live with those defects, the deck is a good choice. And you can't really argue with the results. The deck came from out of nowhere and took first place right away. That can only mean one thing: the deck just *has* to be solid.

Akira Asahara is the Enduring Ideal master. He has posted good results with Enduring Ideal variants, culminating in a Top 8 appearance at last year's World Championships. I think everyone who like the epic card should trust his builds, since he knows the deck inside out. Once Enduring Ideal fans online saw the innovative design he played at the Japanese Nationals, they quickly copied it and took it to the Premier Events. The main innovation of this deck is the addition of the trio of Counterbalance, Scrying Sheets, and Sensei's Divining Top. Counterbalance and Scrying Sheets become powerhouses once you can control the top cards of your deck. Counterbalance Ideal is not the only deck that plays those cards. Last week, many players showed up with Blue/White, Blue/Black, of Blue/White/Black control decks, featuring a lot of countermagic, Sensei's Divining Top, Counterbalance and usually Scrying Sheets as well. The [Black/Blue/White Counter Top control deck](https://web.archive.org/web/20060906000000/http://boards1.wizards.com/showpost.php?p=9953858&postcount=15) is probably the best and it took the number 3 popularity slot this week. The Blue/White and Black/Blue variant just dropped a color for better mana consistency. 

But I digress, so let's return the discussion to Akira Asahara's Enduring Ideal deck. His deck does not fool around with typical control elements such as countermagic. The deck has a different game plan. It works like this. In the early game, you dig for an Enduring Ideal with Sensei's Divining Top, Scrying Sheets, and Court Hussar. Wrath of God takes out an opposing assault along the way. Hopefully, you can accelerate into a turn 5-6 Enduring Ideal with Coldsteel Heart or Azorius Signet and then the fun begins. What you search out then depends on the situation, but a Zur's Weirding is often a good start, since that allows you to control your opponent's draws. Or you can go for the combo of Dovescape and Meishin, the Mind Cage, effectively shutting down your opponent's spells. The kill mechanism is Form of the Dragon. Now you might be wondering why Counterbalance is so good in this deck. I saved the best for the last: even though Enduring Ideal prevents you from playing spells, it does not prevent you from countering every card your opponent plays with Counterbalance and Sensei's Divining Top. Counterbalance's effect still works under the Epic restriction!

Just like normal Heartbeat combo, this deck goes off via Heartbeat of Spring, Early Harvest, and then Weird Harvest. But the combo, however, is not to get 4 Drift of Phantasms and a Maga, Traitor to Mortals with Weird Harvest. This deck is not planning to transmute multiple Drift of Phantasms for Early Harvest in order to get up to 23 mana. Instead, furnacebaby's version aims to get 3 different creatures off of Weird Harvest: Myojin of Life's Web, Sky Hussar, and Kiki-Jiki, Mirror Breaker. The combo then works like this: first you play Myojin of Life's Web, pop his ability, and put Kiki-Jiki, Mirror Breaker and Sky Hussar in play. Kiki-Jiki then copies Hussar, which in turn untaps Kiki-Jiki, which then makes another Hussar copy, and so on. The end result is a million hasty 4/3 flyers, which will then gladly attack for the kill. The advantage of this version is that the combo requires less mana; only 9 (the casting cost of Myojin of Life's Web), instead of 23 (the casting cost of a lethal Invoke the Firemind). The deck still runs Heartbeat of Spring and Early Harvest, but it doesn't necessarily need them to go off. According to furnacebaby, the combo is easier and faster to set up and therefore his deck has a better matchup versus aggro than regular Heartbeat because of that. 

The drawback is that the combo is easier to disrupt. A Shock or Umezawa's Jitte with two counters kills Kiki-Jiki, Mirror Breaker in a pinch and then you cannot complete the combo anymore. I don't even want to mention how a mere Fog can thwart your plans. Indrik Stomphowler and Dosan the Falling Leaf out of the sideboard can help to foil their disruption, but it's still an ugly aspect of the deck. Another downside of the deck is that you run a bunch of dead cards. You are not happy to see Kiki-Jiki, Mirror Breaker or Sky Hussar in your opening hand, whereas a regular Heartbeat deck would have a playable Drift of Phantasms in those slots. 

My verdict on the deck, you ask? My initial impression is that it is not better than regular Heartbeat combo. In my opinion, the big advantage of this version – you need only 9 mana to play your kill card instead of 23 – is not that significant. When regular Heartbeat goes off and plays Weird Harvest for a couple Drift of Phantasms, it usually never runs into trouble assembling enough mana. On the other hand, the downsides of this version – easier to disrupt and “dead” cards – are huge in comparison. Even then, this deck does have one thing going for it: when do you *ever* get to play Myojin of Life's Web in a competitive tournament? Indeed.

### ***Magic** Online* III news

Last week, ***Magic** Online* III was showcased at the PAX convention. Andy of the cardbot team and “mtgotraders” interviewed Justin Ziran and got to see how ***Magic** Online* III looks like. It's better to watch a video than 1000 screenshots, so if you're interested you can [check it out here](https://web.archive.org/web/20060906000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://video.google.com/videoplay?docid=-1927224494708510229&origin=mtgcom_daily_fk4). The audio is of poor quality, but you can find [a transcript of the interview here](https://web.archive.org/web/20060906000000/http://boards1.wizards.com/showthread.php?t=693015). I had seen the game earlier this year at the E3 convention during the **Magic** Invitational and the initial impression I had there has stuck: the game looks very nice. I like how the game still uses a spreadsheet mode, which is comfortably familiar to what works well in ***Magic** Online* 2 right now, but now with an easier, and better-looking layout. MTGO III will feature a flexible tourney system, an in-game store (no more separate logins), and a more stable server. 

The estimated release for MTGO III is now the first or second quarter of 2007. The slight delay is partly caused by the development team that wanted to review the duel screen again, based on the feedback of the usability tests. They want to make sure they get everything right and I think it will be worth the wait. The beta is scheduled to start in January or February. If everything goes according to plan, I can get more detailed information and answers to follow-up questions from Justin Ziran next week. So be on the lookout for more news on ***Magic** Online* III next week, here at Online Tech.
