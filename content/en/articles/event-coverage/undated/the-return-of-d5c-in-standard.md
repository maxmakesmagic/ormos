Once upon a time, there was a block called Rath Cycle. You may not have been playing competitively during Rath Cycle, so I will give you the *Reader's Digest* version of what seemed to be happening when it was released.

In the summer of 1997, David Price slipped his monored pantaloons on two-fourths of the way when he introduced the U.S. National Championships to Lava Hounds. Everyone else seemed to be excited about the restored legality of Swords to Plowshares and Necropotence, but Dave nevertheless posted a 6-0 record in the (then) most difficult Standard tournament in the world with what seemed like a rather underpowered deck. While Deadguy Red was a good call for a tournament where one could expect to get that four life right back, or where the opponent was playing Necropotence against a monored deck, the day was eventually taken by Justin Gary's u-r control deck and the most impressive overall weekend was posted, not surprisingly, by Jon Finkel, who won 1997's midnight U.S. Open and eventually advanced to the Nationals Top 8 with a land-light Kjeldoran Outpost deck.

One King of Beatdown's Day 1 notwithstanding, Standard was all about Thawing Glaciers, Force of Will, and creature removal.

Rath Cycle's opening set, *Tempest*, was set in direct opposition to the slowed-down environment. Price's deck was bolstered by Mogg Fanatic, Jackal Pup, and Fireslinger. True control decks in the style of the Weissman Fortress were eliminated from the metagame for months. Blue players themselves went to creatures. The beatdown decks had become quite quick, and had a late game damage source in the form of Cursed Scroll, so sitting behind a wall of permission, playing a strategy based around a four mana sorcery, and milking the final few life points had become a lot less viable. Running blockers, bounce, and the man who did both - Tradewind Rider - seemed a more attractive option. Even Wrath of God lost a lot of potency, as some decks could answer that with Living Death. In terms of free spells, there was no Force of Will, but there certainly was Fireblast.

It was heaven for the beatdown mage... if heaven is a burning pit of volcanic ash, that is.

Perhaps more dangerous to control decks than any of *Tempest's* other beatdown-friendly cards was Wasteland. Turn-1 Jackal Pup, turn-2 Wasteland was the bane of all non-monochromatic players.

Beatdown ruled for months.

In 1997-1998, beatdown's rule was not like Fires of Yavimaya being played by all JSS kids last year, or Wild Mongrel/madness decks that are good today. The threats of the Fires of Yavimaya decks forced high mana counts, making them prone to mid-game mana flood or slow early development in the absence of Birds of Paradise. The heyday of Dave Price's deck involved turn-1 2-power creatures; drawing land in the midgame was less likely as it had a much lower mana count, and even then, excess mountains would just fuel Fireblast and make Cursed Scroll more consistent.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i> The D5C deck took an extreme look at card advantage.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Combo (most notably Cadaverous Bloom) was good, but inconsistent. Necropotence was consistent, but not always good.

Permanents-based blue decks revolving around Propaganda and various efficient creatures such as Steel Golem or Man-O'-War saw some play, but could not, for the most part, race the ultra-fast red decks. Andrew Cuneo's Draw-Go deck with Argivian Restoration, Capsize, and 14 permission spells (this was considered a lot of permission spells) was built soon after *Tempest*, but would not reach the mainstream until just before U.S. Regionals.

So Mike Donais's five-color blue deck (Donais five-color, or D5C for short) was rather out of place when it hit mainstream recognition with the release of *Stronghold*.

The D5C deck took an extreme look at card advantage. It built upon the low land / cantrips theory that had been pioneered by California-based players such as Alan Comer for about one year. Its success hinged on the statement that "it is odd that the best control cards in *Stronghold* are all green."

The D5C deck sported the most skewed design until the Necropotence combination decks that appeared in Extended **Magic** one and three years later. It played literally no dedicated win conditions. It could sometimes kill the opponent with Fireball, whose other purpose was creature removal; it played no "real" non-land permanents. As we mentioned, other control decks were generally permanent-heavy, and were vulnerable to Disenchant. Beatdown decks played with Cursed Scroll, and a variety of decks ran Nevinyrral's Disk. Artifact removal was quite good in the environment, but was a dead draw against D5C. While Wall of Blossoms appears to be a non-land permanent, the D5C deck considered it a cantrip that smoothed mana draws, that just happened to be able to block Jackal Pup. If the opponent were to use a spell on Wall of Blossoms, D5C would be quite fine with this, and thankful for the advantage in time and cards.

The primary path to victory, one that has been echoed in the hands of the designers of D5C's descendents Brian Schneider, Jason Zila, and others, was Gaea's Blessing recursion. Schneider, in particular, wagged his finger at those who played routes to victory beyond Gaea's Blessing. They loosened up lists and didn't themselves draw cards. Gaea's Blessing would keep the deck moving, and would make sure that D5C's library was always juiced with permission for opponents’ threats.

The general incentive to a deck like D5C over, say, a monoblue control deck is that with more colors, a player has access to more efficient branches of specialization. Sure, blue is the best at drawing cards and countering spells, but it is rather annoying when one's Nevinyrral's Disk is hit with Disenchant, isn't it? While powerful, it comes into play tapped, and is therefore unreliable against some decks. Blue decks can't gain life very effectively, and sometimes find themselves in trouble against burn decks, even when the game seems stabilized. On the other hand, Wall of Blossoms staves off just about anybody as quickly as turn two, Wrath of God can handle the regeneration on that pesky River Boa, and just one Gerrard's Wisdom is like a Counterspell for a whole fistful of Shocks.

D5C, like all decks, has issues with certain matchups, and is much worse than a monoblue deck against Wasteland. On the other hand, its ability to draw into more land with 11+ 1-2 mana cantrips, some of which can block, help counter aggressive Wasteland decks a great deal. While it may be at a disadvantage against a permission deck with more lands and more counterspells, D5C has strength in its ability to Disenchant Steel Golem and totally disregard Nevinyrral's Disk. Whispers of the Muse on both sides provide the card advantage, but barring Rainbow Efreet (a card that was all but forgotten by non-CMU players until the World Championships of that year), D5C has the best card in the matchup in Gaea's Blessing... which is ultimately any of its best cards.

Walamies's innovation was to include Call of the Herd where his predecessors had Wall of Blossoms. Call of the Herd serves much the same function - an early blocker that can potentially generate card advantage - but in some matchups, its ability to go on the offense early advances it from good to ingenious. Call of the Herd's synergy with Intuition is also quite excellent. The absence of Wall of Blossoms, in terms of speed, both in handling early beatdown and smoothing out mana, handled effectively by 1-mana white instants like Swords to Plowshares and Tithe.

While there have obviously been other two-and three-color control decks since 1997, the barrier to D5C-like decks in Standard - decks heavily focused on card advantage for its own sake, forcing dead cards from the opponent, and playing the most spells from each color (as opposed to splashing red or white in a deck designed to Probe away Nether Spirit or add red removal to Upheaval-Psychatog) - has primarily been the absence of adequate dual lands. While any D5C deck will sport an abundance of nonbasics, without Reflecting Pool available, most players would just take too much damage from City of Brass and *Ice Age*-style pain lands to make the decks worthwhile. Few three-color control decks outside *Invasion* Block Constructed ever sported 8+ come-into-play-tapped duals.

On the other hand, the new *Onslaught* sacrifice lands give us a great new opportunity to explore a deck like D5C. Unlike Domain decks, which had a similar sort of outlook, we can build D5C base-blue rather than base-green, and our lands, rather than spells, will do the mana-fixing, allowing us to play more lands, and ironically, more spells as well.

The main thematic problem I see with the deck is the presence of actual non-land permanents. I think that *Odyssey* Block will grandfather Mirari's Wake and Solitary Confinement into Standard (quite possibly with Mobilization kill), and that many decks will have no choice but to either start artifact and enchantment destruction, or be able to Cunning Wish for these sorts of cards. Against at least some opponents, this will potentially generate the kind of dead card advantage that Mike Donais enjoyed in 1997-1998, even if creatures cards are required.

Call of the Herd seems like a good solution to Wild Mongrel to me. He soaks up a lot of damage and will often pull a couple of cards out of the opponent's hand. With Donais's original disdain for his early game green blocker, we don't care if Call of the Herd dies... he almost always sets up a better Wrath of God. Against other control decks, Call is obviously quite good, allowing the D4C 2002 deck to play aggro-control for a few turns or make the opponent use "real" cards on its own incidentals.

I'm not sure if Possessed Aven is the right choice in terms of a Morphling substitute. I originally tried Quicksilver Dragon, but Possessed Aven is two mana cheaper and still has 80% of the punch. At the same time, he can block with Wonder in the opponent's graveyard much more quickly, and, at threshold, manhandles two of the three best creatures in Standard (Merfolk Looter and Psychatog), at least until we figure out which *Onslaught* creatures take the opening left by Flametongue Kavu. Possessed Aven also helps to justify the black portion of the deck. On the other hand, it might just be better to run Nantuko Monastery. Unlike the Wake decks, D4C 2002 can run Nantuko Monastery and not mana burn every turn. It is an efficient attacker, an efficient blocker, and completely immune to permission. It even taps for mana the turn it comes into play! On the downside, the mana it produces is colorless, and the deck, as it stands, is four colors.

I thought a lot about including black over just a three-color deck, and have come to the conclusion that its inclusion at this point is justified. For one thing, Randy Buehler assures me that children at Friday Night **Magic** will show us a viable Elf deck, and I think there is probably something to be said for a monoblue tribal deck based around the saucy mana curve of Hapless Researcher, Voidmage Prodigy, and Patron Wizard. One-mana Goblins, along with the Fireslinger of the new age, Sparksmith, may also show their warty and wrinkled heads at a Standard tournament near you.

Mise well kill them all and all the ones the opponent draws from now on with one spell, am I right?

The non-Engineered Plague part of the sideboard could probably use some tuning. Opportunity might be better than a sideboarded Flash of Insight. If monoblack control and Pirates! are as popular in Standard as they have been in OBC, a full compliment of Composts might be in order. Compost may also prove necessary in racing Oversold Cemetery. I originally had two Hibernations, but with access to Wrath of God and Aura Graft, the only green permanent that I could think of that I couldn't handle more effectively with one of the above was Mirari's Wake, and I would much rather Wish for Ray of Revelation against Mirari's Wake than Hibernation any day.

It's quite possible that an updated Wake deck will just be a much better Standard control deck than a low permanents 4-color deck. Beyond the raw power of Mirari's Wake, an enchantment-laden deck will have the ability to dump dead cards with Compulsion, which can give it an edge against other control decks. Nevertheless, I thought it would be fun to try to resurrect this kind of strategy for Standard.

The one thing that I am fairly sure of is that Krosan Tusker is a good man. In this sort of deck, you will almost never cast him. His cycling ability seems to me like "taking the small pile" on Fact or Fiction, except that it costs only three mana and cannot be countered by conventional permission. The last time I saw a card I liked this much that seemed to be janky but also seemed to have a place in a good control deck… that card was Vicious Hunger.
