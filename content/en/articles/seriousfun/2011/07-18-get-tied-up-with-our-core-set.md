Yes, you read the little window decal on the front page image correctly: it's Core Set Week. Is it shocking that an exclamation point was missing at the end of the previous sentence? Since I get a little *excited* about most things **Magic** related, am I unexcited about Core Set Week?

Quite the contrary, actually.

Talking about the "most basic" sets in **Magic** is a different kind of excitement for me. It's quiet, soft, and caring. It has a loud voice from time to time, but it's more about kind words shared around a small table. The Core Set is where **Magic** comes home, and it's where I go to introduce friends to the game.

![](http://media.wizards.com/images/magic/daily/sf/sf152_sanctuary.jpg)  

A Legacy of Infamy

Core sets *used* to be rather boring. Other than a few key reprints that haven't been seen in some time, it was a little harder to be excited about a set composed only of old cards with a fresh printing. So they, you know, *fixed that*.

Core sets are now full of the juicy things that make **Magic** memorable, and they're one of the best ways to share the game. Whether it's checking out awesome mythic rares, learning the process of Sealed or Draft, or putting together the start of a new deck, sharing the things that are amazing about our game should be a core part of our efforts.

How many friends and acquaintances do you know who *don't* play **Magic**?

![](http://media.wizards.com/images/magic/daily/sf/sf152_ponder.jpg)  

The "simple stuff" of **Magic** is surprisingly complex, but core sets are built to handle that complexity in a package that matches **Magic** as a whole. There are three huge reasons to use a core set to show off **Magic**:

> *   It has familiarity, with a fresh take.
> *   The colors shine at more than what they do best.
> *   Shenanigans are cooked right into the set.

I'm not going to explain all the "simple" stuff, because that's what *you* should be doing for friends when sharing **Magic**. My goal is to make that process a lot easier.

Pedagogy Ain't Easy

When you're already familiar with something complex, it's really easy to brush over the details. For example, do you know how to cast a spell? I mean, *really* know? Most of us have the right idea but are missing the actual details.

And that's okay.

Sharing **Magic** doesn't require being a rules advisor or judge; it requires three virtues:

> *   Patience
> *   Forethought
> *   Humility

Our familiarity with the game can be deadly to new players. We need to hold ourselves above petty things that can sour the experience.

You must be *patient* with your friends. Whether they're coming from [**Duels of the Planeswalkers 2012**](http://duelsoftheplaneswalkers.com/) or some fresh pack of cards, it's time that you're going to need the most. **Magic** is complex, so learning it naturally has a curve. If your friend already has some rules knowledge, start by finding out what they know. Find out what they don't know. Find out what they're uncomfortable with. And most importantly, let them drive the discussion.

![](http://media.wizards.com/images/magic/daily/sf/sf152_pride.jpg)  

It isn't the patience to play **Magic** for hours that I'm talking about. (I'm sure most of us can already do just that.) You have to be patient as your friend explores **Magic** in his or her own way. That's why I try to keep simpler decks and core set booster packs handy: I need to convey the essence of what they're looking for without overloading everything. In that context, [Shock](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Shock) can be the better card than [Burst Lightning](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Burst+Lightning) or [Galvanic Blast](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Galvanic+Blast).

You should know your friends better than I do, and each player wants to learn in different ways. That's where the *forethought* to bring what they want comes into play. Each color, each card, has different flavor and game play. Matching interests and decks up to your friends desires is something you want to do. Just like movies and other games, what your friend likes could be dramatically different from what you do.

![](http://media.wizards.com/images/magic/daily/sf/sf152_foresee.jpg)  

It doesn't make much sense to only show the things you like. I'm a fan of attacking with creatures and making tokens, but some of my friends really enjoy fidgeting with artifacts and enchantments while casting [Cancel](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Cancel) and [Fog](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Fog). **Magic** is made up of more than aggressive dudes, and it's up to you to plan ahead for the contingencies where your friends don't enjoy exactly the same thing as you.

Part of what makes core sets great is that they include facets of every color. Red has creatures and burn spells, but also land destruction and spell copying. Blue has countermagic and "draw a card" effects, but also flying creatures and creature copying. ***Magic** 2012* in particular has dualism in blue: you have the aggressive phantasmal creatures, with their [Lord of the Unreal](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Lord+of+the+Unreal), on one side and the usual [Cancel](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Cancel) and [Azure Mage](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Azure+Mage)-like cards on the other. Colors shine brightest when it's up to each card to show itself off.

Of course, just because a color *can* do something doesn't mean it will do it well. That's where the final piece of the puzzle fits: *humility*. **Magic** is a game, and winning games is often pretty fun. It's isn't just "some" players who like to win; every player is genuinely working towards victory.

![](http://media.wizards.com/images/magic/daily/sf/sf152_favor.jpg)  

How each of make choices during our games is open to a lot of discussion. What's important when sharing **Magic** isn't to play it "right" but to play it "fun." It might make sense to take your [**Friday Night Magic**](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://archive.wizards.com/Magic/TCG/Events.aspx?x=events/magic/fnm) battle-ready Standard deck and play against your friend's Intro Deck, but that doesn't mean it would be fun for either of you. You shouldn't necessarily throw games and intentionally lose, either, as that can cause more harm than help in the long run.

It takes a bigger player to choose decks that can allow either one to win. The *Duel Decks* series, two Intro Decks, two Sealed decks, or any other myriad of ways to level the playing field is in your, *and* your friend's, best interests. One of those myriad of ways is to build your own themed decks custom tailored to share among friends.

Every card here has appeared in a recent core set (with two critical exceptions). It's a bundle of mechanical values that describe black in broad strokes:

> *   Drains life and kills creatures
> *   Brings back things from the graveyard
> *   Plays small, annoying dudes as well as big, hungry ones

But it also features a few things that black has in smaller doses:

> *   [Diabolic Tutor](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Diabolic+Tutor) is the current "tutor" effect.
> *   [Crypt of Agadeem](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Crypt+of+Agadeem) hints at more interaction with the graveyard than just getting stuff back.
> *   [Mortis Dogs](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Mortis+Dogs) is definitely black but looks very different thanks to the Phyrexian watermark.
> *   [Sorin's Vengeance](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Sorin%27s+Vengeance) kills players dead.

Here's a story that highlights what I'm talking about. At the recent ***Magic** 2012* Prerelease I attended I had a pretty fun red and white deck: [Serra Angel](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Serra+Angel), [Volcanic Dragon](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Volcanic+Dragon), [Aegis Angel](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Aegis+Angel), and a [Sun Titan](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Sun+Titan) topped out my bevy of bodies. I was battling hard against a newer player who happened to be wielding an [Inferno Titan](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Inferno+Titan). When the inevitable, clichéd clash of Titans happened, I managed to come out on top thanks to my [Aegis Angel](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Aegis+Angel). The game looked over: my opponent had no cards in hand, just lands in play, and 11 life—the exact amount of damage I had through creatures on the table.

With his final draw step came a squint, some reading, a cackling of glee, a tapping of seven lands, and a windmill slam of [Sorin's Vengeance](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Sorin%27s+Vengeance). *Pow!* Right in the kisser. I took 10 damage, putting me at -2. Game over.

[![](http://gatherer.wizards.com/Handlers/Image.ashx?type=card&name=Sorin%27s+Vengeance)](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Sorin%27s+Vengeance)  

It hurt more than it should when that happened. After fighting his Titan for three games (yes, that was also a clichéd "third game of the round" story) I had already believed I had won. But it's those tiniest of tiny chances that make it all the more interesting. With just his one draw step, he went from zero to hero in a game he left grinning.

*That* experience is what you need humility for. It happens in **Magic**, and the more you accept it the more your friends will enjoy playing it.

The spells on the order of [Sorin's Vengeance](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Sorin%27s+Vengeance) let great things like that game happen. Just because you're switching decks doesn't mean the more experienced player won't still have an advantage. Including cards like that in the decks you share will help create those moments of surprise. *Shenanigans* are baked into these core sets to ensure they happen:

Effects like these can completely change a game. You *and* your friend need to make this things happen, and let them happen when they do. And it doesn't mean just whatever big spell you play should definitely happen: just as blasting me with [Sorin's Vengeance](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Sorin%27s+Vengeance) made my opponent the happiest player there, it may be the quiet [Cancel](https://web.archive.org/web/20110718000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Cancel) held for the whole game that makes your friend smile. *It happens*.
