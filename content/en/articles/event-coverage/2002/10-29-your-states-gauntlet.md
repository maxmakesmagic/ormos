This coming weekend we will be able to once again participate in the various State Championship tournaments (or, as they are now called, the 2002 **Magic** State/Province/Territory/Island Champs). Though many players lament the lack of impact that the State Championships play with regards to "feeding" premiere events, this series of tournaments has historically had quite an impact on the development of the Standard metagame for the following year. States is generally the first opportunity we have to see the newest set in action, just as a previous block disappears from Standard-legal play.

Two years ago, States gave us a sneak peek at the Fires of Yavimaya and Rebel decks that would dominate the field at Kai Budde's Chicago (though Kamiel Cornelissen's innovative design would not be revealed until he himself battled to the PT finals). Oddly enough, while Day 3 competitors like Jon Sonne and Brian Kibler managed to take home their respective State Championships crowns in 2001. The eventually dominant archetype of the last 12 months - Psychatog - made very little noise at last year's string of events.

Very likely, we will see a number of interesting and impressive designs across the 2002 State Championships. Brand spanking new rares like Future Sight and Visara the Dreadful are screaming to be added to the next generation of power decks...

But before you set out to develop the next Deep Dog or the Red Zone, it will probably be helpful to figure out what kind of opposition you will be facing at this year's events. Though the field at the beginning of a Constructed format is generally much more open than at any other point, we nevertheless have access to several resources that can suggest the weapon of choice brought to the table by your average opponent.

### The Beloved Blaze is Bargain Priced

It has been quite a few years since monored beatdown unified the Standard field as it did when Fireblast and Cursed Scroll were simultaneously legal, and even when the deck made a brief return in the summer of 2000, it wasn't taken quite seriously. *Masques* Block gave beatdown players a beefy 5/5 option for four mana that out-shined most or all of red's available creatures, and for the most part, the *Invasion* block suggested that all the colors should be friendly, rather than striking out by their monored selves. *Odyssey* block made red weenie/burn attacks difficult by printing Call of the Herd as an efficient defender.

Despite Price's poor performance during the Standard leg of the Invitational, I believe that a deck like his will be typical of the goblin decks you will see at States. For one thing, the King of Beatdown is beloved, venerated even, by almost all young mages. For another, the goblin deck is *cheap*. While some more flashy strategies are certainly available, we cannot ignore the fact that given a relatively short space of time, it will have been difficult for the average player to acquire the rare cards that are necessary for some of the other decks we will discuss. Finally, the goblin deck can just *win*. If the opponent stumbles, he'll probably fall all the way down the stairs.

### The Grandfather Strategy

Almost every dominant deck from almost every block format has eventually "grown up" and represented in a wider field. Rebels went from numerical dominance in New York to winning a Standard Pro Tour over a year later. Blue Skies went from a *Masques* Block deck of choice one World Championships to a dominant Standard deck the following one. The Urza's Block-based artifact strategy premiered in 1999, won a Pro Tour, went on to give Kai Budde his groundbreaking World Championship, confirmed the return of Jon Finkel, and cemented the Great One as Player of the Year... Three years after its introduction, Urza's Block's artifacts and Tinker threaten the upcoming Extended Pro Tour.

While Compost and Naturalize give the u-g madness deck a great deal of play against some of the most powerful opponents in the field, testing has proved the "mirror" match against other u-g decks to remain mostly about Wonder and Aether Burst. In short, if one u-g deck gets Wonder into its graveyard and the other doesn't, the latter deck will be at a significant disadvantage, making the mirror very draw dependent. On the other hand, Zvi Mowshowitz points out that while this is true, the u-g deck gives its player a number of subtle opportunities to misplay, meaning that over time, the superior player will win more games.

Unfortunately, most **Magic** matches last only 2-3 games.

Probably the most consistent deck of the *Odyssey* Block was black control. Gary Wise posted a perfect record with it at the World Championships and Valentin Moskovich took the archetype from zero byes to a Grand Prix victory. Black control exploited the skewed power of *Torment* and did not suffer from the mana inconsistencies of any of the polychromatic archetypes. It was forced down from its slightly-less-than-half-of-the-field position by the development of aggressive Braids decks and the overwhelming dead card advantage generated by matches against the creature-poor Mirari's Wake builds.

However, if there is one thing that you can bank on, it is that most of your opponents at States will be playing with creatures. Tons of cheap creature kill accompanied by powerful cards and mana acceleration should do well against these decks, especially if you don't have to worry about color-screw.

None of the players at the 2002 Invitational chose to go with black control in Standard, but an updated version of the deck in Standard was recently posted by Sean McKeown at Neutral Ground.

The four-color Burning Wish version, played by Brian Kibler, is a brute force deck that wins based on the fact that its cards are much more powerful than the average opponent's cards. The Cunning Wish version, piloted to 2-1 by Invitational Champion Jens Thoren, plays many of the same cards, but could not be more different from the Kibler version. While Kibler's deck is about mana acceleration in the early game, and brute force spell power once its board has been developed, Thoren's deck is an elegant machine. The three-color build wants to get not only Mirari's Wake, but Mirari itself into play, in order to take total control of the game. Using Cunning Wish and Mirari, this deck can generate infinite card drawing over time. Once a Cunning Wish has been removed from the game, the Mirari-Cunning Wish engine allows this deck to retrieve another Wish and another instant. That instant can be Moment's Peace, locking out creature damage, a permission spell of some sort, or Elephant Ambush.

Thoren's deck does not use its Mirari's Wake to ram an aggressive strategy down the opponent's throat. Instead, it creates an army of elephants over time. The elephants can of course defend, but also make for a quick clock given numbers and Mirari's Wake's secondary ability.

Once the proper cards are in play, Thoren's deck is impervious to combat damage, will have access to the ability to counter any number of threats, destroy any type of annoying permanent, and can overrun its opponent with huge creatures. All of that being said, if we can use *Odyssey* Block as a barometer, this deck will not be particularly popular at States, being both rare-intensive and absurdly difficult to play.

### Last Year, but Updated

Besides u-g madness, the two deck archetypes most likely to graduate from 2001-2002 to the 2002 State Championships are the undisputed King of Standard (Psychatog), and crown prince of tapping tokens (Opposition). Both decks suffer at the loss of *Invasion* Block staples like Fact or Fiction and Flametongue Kavu, but both also survived to impressive records at the Invitational.

Dave Humpherys, fresh off a Masters Series final and World Championships Top 8 with the archetype, surprised no one with his 2-1 showing with Psychatog in Seattle.

Humphery's deck is probably the single most interesting build of all the decks we are looking at today. Future Sight can win a game on hitting the table. It turbo-charges a deck with a lot of cheap cards, and both rewards and ensures land drops in a very Necropotence-like way. While Predict is good with Psychatog and great with Memory Lapse, it is absurd with Future Sight. If a less desirable spell (say, a redundant Future Sight) is sitting on top, it can whisk it away. At the very least, Predict becomes and Inspiration for 2 mana.

All of that being said, this Psychatog deck is still set up with the classic Upheaval/Tog kill that we have seen over and over since PT-San Diego. It may be a bit flashier or more confusing along the way, but most games will be won when there are next to no permanents on the board.

The "other" deck from last year's metagame was not based on *Invasion* or even *Odyssey* Block cards, but a *Seventh Edition* enchantment. Not surprisingly, that *Seventh Edition* remains in Standard implies that there will also be successful Opposition decks. Strangely, though, the Opposition decks that showed up at the 2002 Invitational, are a lot different than their u-g or r-u-g ancestors.

Nassif's offense is a death by a thousand cuts. It is a difficult deck to pigeonhole, and an equally difficult deck to counter with one's sideboard. Nassif could ostensibly take out his out-of-place Oppositions against some decks, stranding an opponent's Naturalizes. At the same time, the deck's access to three different colors of answers allows it the ability to smash huge creatures, disrupt combination decks, or destroy artifacts and enchantments.

And, of course, it can tap down the opponent's blockers, untap, and smash his face with elephant tokens.

An equally unusual update is the Punisher deck that Tomi Walamies introduced at Worlds with a perfect record in *Odyssey* Block. The Punisher deck, specializing in the defeat of u-g opponents packing Roar of the Wurm, is one of the metagame's biggest beneficiaries given the wider field. Battle Screech and Opposition are a natural combination, and Walamies's deck "breaks" Opposition in a much more focused manner than Nassif's. *Onslaught* adds the extremely efficient Whipcorder to the Punisher weenie army for even more creature-tapping power. In the hands of a skilled player, Weathered Wayfarer allows the Punisher Opposition deck to draw tons of extra cards.

Obviously, if it has fewer lands in play than the opponent, the Punisher deck can use Weathered Wayfarer the way the card was intended. However, even with the same number of lands in play, Weathered Wayfarer can draw cards… as long as one of the lands is a Flooded Strand. Walamies can sacrifice Flooded Strand to search through his library, and then respond by activating his nomad cleric. With the Flooded Strand's ability still on the stack, the Punisher deck would temporarily have fewer lands in play than the opponent, allowing the use of the Wayfarer. While drawing extra cards is always nice, in this case, the cards are still just lands... but Walamies still has a trick or two up his sleeve! By selecting then cycling Secluded Steppe, this deck can tap two mana and a 1/1 creature to just "draw a card”.

The Punisher Opposition deck is set up very efficiently against a deck like u-g madness. U-g has few long game answers to Spurnmage Advocate, which can simultaneously rob the opponent of threshold, kill his best attacker, and throw Roar of the Wurm back into his hand, where it costs ![6 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/2d/12/2d12c8318f11a9188ce9fdd831b87ed97536df32e65841004ce583d399c4b454.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif) rather than ![3 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/7f/5d/7f5d2f1e55eaa9d19a9a23027b63bfbb5b39dbf8642160dda1a477a03df23a6a.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif). It also has solid game against the Wake decks. Against the Burning Wish version, it can attempt to survive a Firecat Blitz with Prismatic Strands, and against both versions, it can break up machinery with Circular Logic and Disenchant. As a straightforward aggro deck, Punisher Opposition can also just go for the quick offense afforded by its efficient attackers, Battle Screech, and Divine Sacrament. This deck has many ways to win the game, and more still to rob the opponent of the ability to do so.

Last, but by no means least, in the Opposition family is the classic u-g build. Possibly the version of Opposition you are most likely to face at States, the u-g version is epitomized by Olivier Ruel's 3-0 deck from the Invitational.

The u-g Opposition deck is extremely solid, if unexciting. Its long-term plan is to set up creatures and use Opposition to restrict the opponent's ability to play spells. Opposition is particularly good in this version because it has so many ways to generate creature advantage. Ruel's deck plays with Call of the Herd. It runs Squirrel Nest. It turns a late draw Birds of Paradise into an Icy Manipulator. It runs even more card advantage creature generation in the sideboard.

Against other creature decks, this one is particularly well set up. Though it starts the Opposition strategy, Ruel can morph into a Deep Dog deck with Quiet Speculation and Roar of the Wurm out of the sideboard. Ravenous Baloth is a natural addition… aggressive decks will be hard-pressed to penetrate this one's efficient creatures, life gain ability, mana acceleration, and potential to race.

I was somewhat surprised by the omissions in Ruel's sideboard. He did not include Naturalize or Static Orb. Naturalize, while probably not a main deck card, is probably this deck's most efficient answer to an opposing Mirari, Mirari's Wake, or Opposition. While most Opposition players do not start Static Orb currently, it seems that against some opponents, the ability to not just slow down, but completely lock the opponent's board is a valuable option.

### Toby Wachter's Favorite Deck

I would be surprised if Toby himself didn't bring the Battle to States. At one point, I would have doubted if any one else would choose this deck, but since Billy Jensen's impressive performance last spring in Milwaukee, and especially Jonathan Magic's perfect 3-0 record with the deck, Battle of Wits seems like a viable choice.

Part of the allure of Battle is that it can just tap five mana to win the game. Part of it is that no matter how many threats the opponent can present, Battle will almost always have plenty of answers lurking somewhere in the 200-odd cards of its library. Part of the allure - and a big part it is - is that Battle decks don't need to be tuned in quite the same way as other Standard decks. While most decks need to be honed down to 60 lands and spells, Battle of Wits players can play with all of their favorite cards! While a black control deck may not be able to figure out the right balance of copies between Blackmail and Duress, Battle of Wits can play four copies of each. With Battle, if you like a card, you don't have to cut it just because something else seems more appropriate the day of the tournament.

Jon's reason for playing Battle was just that it was the most fun. He didn't lose a match.

Have fun yourselves!
