While I think it is a good idea to be familiar with these decks, you should still take this overview with a grain of salt. Despite the fact that we are in the middle of an established environment, Regionals is the kind of tournament where anything and everything can come out of the woodwork. In 1999, in the three-deck metagame of Necropotence, Survival of the Fittest and Sligh, I played against a Yawgmoth's Will-Tinker deck, Commander Greven il-Vec and a Tradewind Prison on the way to the Top 8 and proceeded to face Enchantress combo once I got there. In 2000, I never faced a single Accelerated Blue deck or a Replenish deck until the elimination rounds. While figuring out deck matchups can be very important, knowing your own deck and being flexible in the face of an unknown opponent should be priority one.

This is the version of Slide that I would consider archetypical. Has it won any championships or made Top 8 in any of the Nationals Qualifiers around the world? Nope. Why bother testing against this version? Zvi Mowshowitz posted it... it is just a couple of cards off of the deck that Zvi ran at the Chicago Gateway.

*Outlook*

Slide is extremely powerful against creature decks. Barring an unchecked turn 3 Elephant Guide while going second, it will generally take r-g beats without breaking a sweat. Even with the optimal Elephant Guide draw, Slide is capable of winning on the strength of its vast board control cards, stalling the fattest men indefinitely while picking off the weenies and setting up the big spell.

U-g has a better time of it, but is ultimately at a disadvantage. U-g's main weapons are Circular Logic, Ray of Revelation and careful management of extra cards in hand. It has long been my opinion that Slide has wave after wave of trump cards in the u-g matchup, specifically Astral Slide, Wrath of God (or the equivalent) and finally Exalted Angel. U-g has to have the Ray to sanction the Astral Slide or it will generally just lose. After that, it has to weather the Wraths. After that, if it is not wholly overwhelmed, u-g still has to keep from losing to a 4/5 flyer with Spirit Link built in. In my opinion, it is more important to use Circular Logic to counter "random morph creature" than even Wrath of God. While u-g can eventually overcome the card advantage of Wrath of God with Quiet Speculation or Deep Analysis, it may not be able to keep up with the persistent development offered by the white permanents. Sideboarded games are a different story, with u-g packing up to four Rays, but ultimately, a wise Slide player should know that u-g can only win with creature beatdown and should mete out answers aware of the fact that there is no haste and no burn.

Slide is best against decks like goblins and beasts. Removal coupled with card drawing and life gain makes for a potent combination. Repeated Teroh's Faithful and Renewed Faith life gain marginalize burn while Exalted Angel mops up the opponent's life total.

And Slide won Pro Tour-Venice.

Before you sign up for the Astral Slide brigade this Saturday, keep in mind that as strong as Slide is against creature decks, it is equally disadvantaged in many of the control matchups. Its current incarnation can do next to nothing about Psychatog's nine mana combo kill (other than gaining enough life to keep out of a lethal Psychatog strike), and the matchup against Mirari's Wake is next to hopeless. Basically, the Slide vs. Wake matchup is a mirror match where neither deck can kill the other quickly, but the Wake deck can draw infinite cards and can play with a limitless library while the Slide deck has no access to permission or relevant card advantage. As good as the Slide permanents are against creatures, they pale in comparison to Mirari, Mirari's Wake and even Compulsion. 

The matchup against monoblack control is not a decisive loss for Slide, but black has two weapons that are extremely effective, especially given the Slide deck's strategy of filling its graveyard with cycling cards. Slide has no counters. There is nothing much it can do about turn 4 Diabolic Tutor, turn 5 Mind Sludge. A Haunting Echoes follow up will generally seal the game at any point. For Slide to win, it will need to get the enchantments down early and milk them for all they are worth. Black can't remove enchantments, so a turn 2 Lightning Rift can do a lot of damage over a relatively slow control matchup. Even with Composts out of Zvi’s side, though, my money is on swamp.

*Modifications*

One of Zvi's main innovations was to splash green, but not to go all out with Krosan Tusker and a heavier green component. The first good Astral Slide decks I saw had Burning Wish sideboards and could win the mirror with Cleansing Meditation, but Zvi answers this strategy with a real sideboard and multiple Rays of Revelation, which are just as good, if not better, over a three game match.

That being said, Jeff Cunningham said most of Zvi's sideboard is “a collection of the cards that don't make the cut in draft decks”. While Auramancer and the like certainly have a place, were I to run Slide this weekend, I would approach the deck a bit differently.

Assuming I chose Slide because it could dominate creature decks, I would overload my sideboard with anti-blue spells. Wrath of God is fairly terrible against Psychatog and Wake and doesn't cycle... I'm pretty sure I'd have four copies of Boil in my board. A well-placed Boil when the opponent taps out for Concentrate or Deep Analysis, even if it only takes out two islands, will give a Slide deck some breathing room against a nine mana combination kill.

The other card I'd like to see is Nantuko Monastery in the main. Astral Slide is one of the few non-blue decks that can establish threshold quickly. There is nothing as impressive as clearing the board with a Wrath of God, followed by sending a 4/4 first strike creature the same turn.

*Tricks*

Lethal damage on the stack? Try cycling Renewed Faith for two life and a card. The best players probably won't let you get away with this, but this play can't be countered, and even heavily advantaged Psychatog players may find themselves with not enough left to win should you buy two measly life.

This was the "secret tech" (or at least the forgotten deck) at Neutral Ground... up until Jelger Wiegersma posted a second place finish at Dutch Nationals that bought him multiple front page articles here on the *Sideboard*.

For those who don't know, the goal of this version of Mirari's Wake is to assemble a position including Mirari, Mirari's Wake and Cunning Wish. With Mirari in play, you can cast Cunning Wish to get whatever you need - permission, life gain, damage prevention, or an instant elephant - as well as an additional Cunning Wish via the Mirari copy. Mirari's Wake simply gives you enough mana to do this without getting killed in the process.

Over the course of just a few turns, most wise opponents will look at the clock, look at your Cunning Wish engine, and figure out if you will make a mistake bad enough to cost you the game or if it is time to shuffle up for the next one. While the machinery of the Cunning Wake deck can be arduous to assemble, once it is in play, the deck can literally generate infinite life via Renewed Faith recursion, an infinite library with Krosan Reclamation or an infinite army of 4/4 or even 5/5 instant creatures.

*Outlook*

I would categorize Wake as probably the most difficult deck to play properly in the current Standard environment. I have found it to be a real test to get the right cards and the right mana for the first five or so turns of the game. Against pressure, you have to play extremely tight **Magic** in the early game, measuring out the right response cards, knowing when to prevent a paltry two points of damage with Moment's Peace, when to risk the face-down Angel and when to tap out for a big spell. I have found that after the machinery is in place, it doesn't really matter how badly you play, but getting to that position can be difficult. According to my good friend Josh Ravitz: “Wake is clearly the best, but you'll probably make a small mistake and lose to some horrible aggro deck”.

Against a true control deck like Psychatog, your biggest weapon will be Compulsion. Turn 2 Compulsion is going to be one of your main routes to sculpting a hand that will allow you to force through your key permanents. Keep in mind, though, that the average Psychatog deck is going to be more mana efficient than you before Mirari's Wake is in play and will also have more permission. Because Psychatog has had to switch over a lot of its anti-control cards, like Cunning Wish and Duress, over to more creature kill main deck, you will have the Cunning Wish advantage.

The enemy of Mirari's Wake is Upheaval. One of the main incentives to Psychatog over this control deck is the ability to reset the game, no matter how far ahead the opponent is. You can have a full hand, both of your five mana cost Disenchant targets in play, and Cunning Wish in your hand, but if something goes terribly wrong and Upheaval resolves, you may find yourself on the wrong end of an Atog in short order. Kai also commented that Upheaval in u-g decks is also brutal for Wake, given both Rogier Maaten's success in Dutch Nationals and probably his own loss to Ken Ho at the Chicago Masters. Kai says a good number of other smart things about Mirari's Wake here on the Sideboard. You can look at his [in depth analysis of this archetype](/en/articles/event-coverage/2003/02-10-wishing-at-the-masters) - untouched by *Legions* - as well as his commentary on [Dutch Nationals](/en/articles/event-coverage/2003/04-24-standard-tech-for-regionals).

*Outlook*

In my opinion, monoblack control is the most powerful deck in the format. It is very similar to the *Invasion* Block domain decks - or even Tinker decks from Extended - in the raw strength of its individual cards and also mixes the speed of Duress and the early-game defense offered by its spot removal.

So why will this deck be severely underrepresented at U.S. Regionals? Compost costs ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif).

Even given its strength across many matchups, monoblack is severely handicapped by the strongest color hoser in the format. Given the fact that roughly 2/3 of the field has access to this card, even the most confident black mages have to think twice about their chances playing against a personal Howling Mine. Houston Champ Justin Gary respects Compost so much he declared black "DEAD" (yes, all in capital letters) in a recent article on Brainburst.

Does anyone remember Seal of Cleansing? How about Dodecapod? Surely decks that lose to turn-2 breakers like those would never be successful in Constructed tournaments. I think it will be interesting to watch the dance between Compost and monoblack at this year's Regionals. Will black show up at all? Will green players expect it to no show and not run Compost? Will black win even in the face to turn-2 Compost? 

*Strategy*

Winning with monoblack is going to go hand in hand with having a good plan against Compost. Leaving in all the copies of Duress in game 2 is a good start. Karsten also has 4 Cabal Therapies, though in the two matchups he had against Compost-enabled green decks covered on the *Sideboard*, it doesn't look like he brought them in for turn 1 redundancy.

Visara is a strong anti-Compost card, much better than the Volcanic Hammer-vulnerable Eastern Paladin, but it costs 6 mana, opening the door to a lot of potential problems. She is still within the range of two red cards, can't block a creature enchanted with Elephant Guide and is subject to Unsummon. Nope, I wouldn't play with fewer than two main deck, either.

The other scary card besides Compost is going to be Persecute. Despite a game 1 advantage over Psychatog, you can expect the opponent to run two, if not three, Persecutes out of the sideboard. It should go without saying that against a single color deck, Persecute is absolutely devastating... part of the necessity of Nantuko Shade in game 2 comes from having to have something significant in play before you lose your hand.

The neat thing about the Garfield deck is that it will annihilate the creature decks that are supposed to be good against creature decks. Who can deal with Phantom Nishoba and Contested Cliffs at the same time? If, like many seasoned players, you believe Regionals will be primarily a sea of beatdown decks, there are certainly worse choices than beasts, few of which create creatures nearly as large.

If not for recent developments in the blue two-thirds of the Big Three, I would definitely have run this archetype (a version a little slower than Gary's, but not quite as slow as the truly giant-sized Garfield deck). When Kai's anti-control Concentrate deck was the default Psychatog build, turn 3 Phantom Centaur looked pretty impressive to me, and a good deal faster than Upheaval. But these days, where even the u-g decks can make you pick up your ridiculously expensive blocker for just 1 mana, I am not so brave.

*Outlook*

The Big Three matchups for beasts tend to be strong, with a lot of the play coming from the specific templating of the opposing deck. The advantage is tremendous against r-g, with losses coming primarily on the back of Elephant Guide. U-g opponents without Unsummon are the kind of players you want to see all day, but those with Unsummon are not so willing victims; multiple Unsummons will pull this favorable matchup uncomfortably below the 50% mark. As I mentioned before, my version of beasts runs 4 Phantom Centaur main, which is a big help against Psychatog. Basically, a resolved Phantom Centaur is a four turn clock at most and runs right by Doctor Teeth. 

On the other hand, Astral Slide is a difficult matchup. Game one is next to impossible, with beasts winning primarily when the opponent's second white mana for Wrath of God comes from a Secluded Steppe and on turn 5. Monoblack control is a toss up and can go either way. Beasts is a deceptively card advantageous deck and can outlast monoblack's anti-creature suite with surprising resilience due to flashback and Genesis.

### Reanimator

This explosive and exciting archetype from the Chicago Gateway didn't even make an entrance in the main tournament. Likewise, Reanimator was a deck we tested early, but the players most interested in it ended up dropping the archetype soon after. Keep in mind I heard things like "that deck never wins" from Gerrard Fabiano, who had tested only Tight Sight (with 4 main deck Krosan Reclamations) against it and our early testing cycle involved decks with 4 Withered Wretch maindeck... before the proliferation of r-g beatdown and their pesky Grim Lavamancers.

That being said, Reanimator is a classic rulebreaker deck. It has raw power exhibited by the most expensive creatures from every color and pays the mana cost of none of them. Its ability to play out a fast Phantom Nishoba while defending with Smother makes it phenomenal against r-g and Visara the Dreadful with haste is positively dreadful for u-g.

The central strategy of Reanimator is to put the proper threat in your graveyard and then pay a discount cost via Stitch Together, Zombify or Doomed Necromancer to get that threat into play. Ideally, you will want to set up Anger with Buried Alive, so that your giant monster can attack or otherwise act immediately. Haste is a key advantage to Visara the Dreadful against a creature beatdown, as well as Arcanis the Omnipotent, who makes a rare Constructed appearance against the Astral Slide mage.

You may have noticed that this deck should have difficulty casting most of its threats directly from hand. Though it ideally leverages Buried Alive and Entomb to select specific cards from the library, Scott's version also has options like dropping creatures via Sickening Dreams, Last Rites or even Gigapede.

Reanimator theoretically lost the most of any archetype with the printing of *Legions* - turn 2 Withered Wretch can end the game by itself - but with few players running that card, it also makes one of the biggest gains: Akroma, Angel of Wrath. Akroma's best asset may be her ability to play both offense and defense, but the fact that she has haste even without Anger online makes her an invaluable racer in tight games.

Reanimator has great game against r-g beatdown, Astral Slide and u-g madness in game one, though Andrew Cuneo said he doesn't think that it can hang with Compost + Aether Burst (or probably Unsummon) in sideboarded games. This archetype has a tough time with Psychatog, which matches its card selection with card advantage and can selectively counter its reanimation spells while ignoring most of the manipulation. Monoblack control is a complete slugfest, with both decks tossing extremely powerful spells at one another, both hoping to dodge the Compost pairing.

Like its predecessor in Extended, today's Reanimator is highly vulnerable to anti-"big turn" strategies. It can spend three cards bringing out a huge threat only to see it die to Innocent Blood the following turn, or have a hand stifled entirely by a single Duress. Though capable of more blatantly impressive early games than any of the Big Three, Reanimator is also more vulnerable to disruption.

Doubtless you have seen other contenders out there, perhaps a Cleric White Weenie or infinite elf deck out of Rob Dougherty, or recall the days when Billy Jensen scored a GP Top 8 with Battle of Wits. The reality of this format is that there are many viable deck archetypes and it is nearly impossible both to test exhaustively against all of them and also pick a deck that will best handle every possible opponent.

My goal here has been to give you a general overview of some of the viable archetypes, the Big Three most importantly. Whether you choose or modify a build by Kai Budde or strike out with your own idea, be sure that you can handle u-g madness, r-g beatdown, and Psychatog, but also be aware that there are a host of choices, both beatdown and control, available outside those fairly stock archetypes.
