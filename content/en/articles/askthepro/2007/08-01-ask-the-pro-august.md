Sleep is one of the most common reasons players make mistakes. Staying focused for so long is tough, especially when it is so hard to fuel your brain with proper food.</p>&#10;<p>The best way to prepare for a tournament is to have everything ready before going to bed. If it is a Constructed event, have your deck sleeved and listed even if you think you may have to change a couple of cards on the next morning. Unfortunately, this is not proper testing. Testing an archetype once is not enough, and the thing is that you might want to try the same archetype in the next draft...which would be the one that really counts.</p>&#10;<p>Don't spend the whole day before the tournament playing <b>Magic</b>. I'm not talking about asking your teammates what they passed and trying do deduce information from that, I'm talking about calling out tricks as your opponent plays them or writing down the relevant contents of your opponent's deck and giving it to your teammates before the next round. Is it encouraged, accepted-but-frowned-upon, or considered cheating?<br> -Etched Oracle<br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hello Etched,</p>&#10;<p>I explained on Tuesday why &quot;team draft&quot; was popular, on Thursday how it worked. To close the &quot;team draft&quot; week, I think it's right to clarify what's allowed and what's tolerated. </p>&#10;<p><b>What's allowed:</b></p>&#10;<p><i>Before the draft</i><br></p><li>You are allowed to set a strategy. Let's say: A, B and C are teaming together, A can tell his teammates to stay away from white as he will force the color whatever happens. It is however a bad idea to do so before the draft as it closes a lot of options to each other players, especially if A ends up not drafting white (because he is cut off, or for any other reason). </li>&#10;<p></p>&#10;<p><i>During the games</i><br></p><li>You can ask your teammates at any time if they saw a particular card. </li>&#10;<p></p>&#10;<p>&#10;</p><li>You can write down all of your opponents' cards so your teammates know exactly what's in their opponents' decks (even though it is not a frequent habit, as it slows down the games). </li>&#10;<p></p>&#10;<p>&#10;</p><li>You can warn your teammate about a particular card you can see coming. For example, A (your teammate) is playing against Z (one of your opponents). Z passed the turn with six lands open. A is about to cast his big spell. Making mistakes happens a lot more when you are playing for fun, and stupid ones can occur because of the lack of concentration. Taping the wrong lands, for example, or committing a totally stupid chump attack come to mind. Except for crucial mistakes (and if noticed soon enough), most mistakes can be undone. Try not to abuse take-backs. </li>&#10;<p></p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;                 <b>August 9, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<p><b>Q</b>: You mentioned &quot;team drafting&quot; a couple of times in Ask The Pro, and I'd like to know exactly how it works. Can you please explain? <br> -Many readers<br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hello everyone,</p>&#10;<p>Six-man team drafts became a popular format, and not only on the Pro Tour. There are no official rules, as it is not a sanctioned format. I'll try to explain here, how it works:</p>&#10;<p><i>1. Gather your team and find opponents.</i><br> Each team should feature three players (four-man teams is an option, but the whole thing is considerably longer if all the games are played—if every one plays each of his opponents. The match being played in an even number of games, it's also possible that it ends up in a draw).</p>&#10;<p>Players can play for a stake. The winning team usually (at least) ends up collecting the cards that were opened during the draft. Keep in mind that it's not allowed to play for money (or any stakes at all) in most countries and tournament sites.</p>&#10;<p><i>2. Everyone sits down.</i><br> Choosing at random, decide who sits where. Each player should be sitting between two of his opponents.</p>&#10;<p><i>3. Open the packs and draft.</i><br> At this point, there should be no talking about the cards themselves, or any information about the cards drafted or the colors you are picking. Draft just like in a regular Booster Draft: pass the boosters to your left for the first and third packs, and to your right for the second.</p>&#10;<p><i>4. Build your decks.</i><br> Each team gathers away from their opponents and builds their decks. At that point, you can share everything that you've seen during the draft. Team drafts often draw crowds of spectators, who want to see what everyone has drafted. To keep things fair, they should not tell the players in the draft what they saw in their opponents' decks. If someone saw, by mistake, the first pick of his neighbour, it is also fairer that he keeps it for himself and doesn't reveal it to his teammates. </p>&#10;<p>When building your deck, you should tell your teammates the relevant cards you passed (avoid mentioning random cards as you will lose their attention). The most important part is to mention tricks (such as removal spells and pump spells) and bombs.</p>&#10;<p><i>Playing the matches</i><br> Each player plays each of his opponents until the match score reaches five for one of the teams. The team with five or more victories wins the draft!</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Is there a way to prove it or at least prevent it?</p>&#10;<p>- Jeremy<br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hello Jeremy,</p>&#10;<p>I'll give you a couple of tips to avoid this kind of problem in the future.</p>&#10;<p>Shuffling manipulations, when they are completed skillfully, are hard to catch for common players. It's also hard to know if your opponent is shuffling legaly or is doing something fishy. In the case you're suspecting something, you should always call the judge right away. He can check the deck being shuffled and see if there's a pattern in the way the cards are ordered. That also means the player in question can be watched going forward, if the judges feel that's necessary. </p>&#10;<p>Another important point: the best way you can prevent card manipulation is to shuffle your opponent's deck everytime he touches it. He is then allowed to cut his deck, and this is sometimes when the cheaters strike. Make sure the cut is random. The option to give the final cut to your deck after your opponent has shuffled it is in the rules but everytime someone cuts his deck after I shuffled, that raises my suspicion. So, if you notice something shady, like a careful look at the deck before cutting, call the judge immediately. </p>&#10;<p>Make sure your opponent does not see your cards when he shuffles your deck, for obvious reasons (the deck can then be stacked, or reveal information your opponent was not supposed to know). The cards should always be facing you or the table.</p>&#10;<p>When you shuffle your opponent's deck, at the beginning of each game use at least one pile shuffle in addition to your riffle shuffles, for two reasons:<br></p><ul>&#10;<li>to know how many cards he's playing</li>&#10;<li>to make sure the sleeves aren't marked.</li>&#10;</ul>&#10;<p></p>&#10;<p>Most of the tips above are habits to take. Cheaters don't act every game. They usually take advantage of less attentive players. As soon as you know how you can be cheated, there is less chance that you will be.</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>
