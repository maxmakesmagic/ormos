<table>&#10;  <tbody><tr>&#10;    <td colspan="2">&#10;      <b>Card of the Day - Friday, July 19, 2013</b>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;  <tr>&#10;    <td colspan="2">&#10;      <div>&#10;        <p>&#10;          </p><div>&#10;            <div>&#10;              <a>&#10;                <img alt="Manaweft Sliver" src="http://gatherer.wizards.com/Handlers/Image.ashx?size=small&amp;type=card&amp;name=Manaweft%20Sliver&amp;options=">&#10;              </a>&#10;            </div>&#10;          </div>&#10;          <br>&#10;          <b>&#10;            <a>Manaweft Sliver</a>&#10;          </b> – <i><b>Magic</b> 2014 Core Set</i> uncommon. You probably know what &quot;mana&quot; means in the context of <b>Magic</b>. But is &quot;weft&quot; an invention of the creative team? Nope! It's <a href="http://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/weft">a weaving term</a> for the yarn or thread carried by the shuttle across the warp. Hmm... lots of jargon there. In other words, it's the yarn or thread that makes up half a weave in cloth. So our little Sliver buddy here (and its Sliver kin) weaves mana together for your benefit! <p></p>&#10;      </div>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Well, it's a plane of the Multiverse, first seen on a card with <i>Planechase</i> (2012 Edition)'s <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20130701000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?multiverseid=226515">Mount Keralia</a>. Oh, and yes, in case you were wondering, there <b>is</b> a tie between Chandra (face of M14) and Regatha. But you'll have to read to find out what! <p></p>&#10;      </div>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

<table>&#10;  <tbody><tr>&#10;    <td colspan="2">&#10;      <b>Card of the Day - Wednesday, July 17, 2013</b>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;  <tr>&#10;    <td colspan="2">&#10;      <div>&#10;        <p>&#10;          </p><div>&#10;            <div>&#10;              <a>&#10;                <img alt="Blood Bairn" src="http://gatherer.wizards.com/Handlers/Image.ashx?size=small&amp;type=card&amp;name=Blood%20Bairn&amp;options=">&#10;              </a>&#10;            </div>&#10;          </div>&#10;          <br>&#10;          <b>&#10;            <a>Blood Bairn</a>&#10;          </b> – <i><b>Magic</b> 2014 Core Set </i> common. Today, this word is chiefly Scottish, and has been considered as such since the early part of the 18th century, but it traces back to Old English (and Old Norse) and has been in use since at least the 12th century.<p></p>&#10;      </div>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

<table>&#10;  <tbody><tr>&#10;    <td colspan="2">&#10;      <b>Card of the Day - Tuesday, July 16, 2013</b>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;  <tr>&#10;    <td colspan="2">&#10;      <div>&#10;        <p>&#10;          </p><div>&#10;            <div>&#10;              <a>&#10;                <img alt="Armored Cancrix" src="http://gatherer.wizards.com/Handlers/Image.ashx?size=small&amp;type=card&amp;name=Armored%20Cancrix&amp;options=">&#10;              </a>&#10;            </div>&#10;          </div>&#10;          <br>&#10;          <b>&#10;            <a>Armored Cancrix</a>&#10;          </b> – <i><b>Magic</b> 2014 Core Set </i> common. So, &quot;Cancrix&quot; is fun because it's not an actual English word but you can see where it came from: the Cancrix is a Crab, which ties in with the constellation <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer_%28constellation%29">Cancer</a>, whose name traces back to the Latin &quot;cancer&quot; (<a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer_%28genus%29">crab</a>), borrowed from the Greek &quot;karkinos&quot; (crab, tumor, constellation), <i>which in turn</i> traces back to the <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language">Proto-Indo-European</a> root &quot;qarq–&quot; (hard). Whew! And &quot;-rix?&quot; In <b>Magic</b>, it's a combining form <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20130701000000/http://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Search/Default.aspx?output=spoiler&amp;method=visual&amp;action=advanced&amp;name=+[rix]+![Matrix]+![Grixis]&amp;subtype=+[%22Beast%22]">previously seen on Beasts</a>. <p></p>&#10;      </div>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

<table>&#10;  <tbody><tr>&#10;    <td colspan="2">&#10;      <b>Card of the Day - Monday, July 15, 2013</b>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;  <tr>&#10;    <td colspan="2">&#10;      <div>&#10;        <p>&#10;          </p><div>&#10;            <div>&#10;              <a>&#10;                <img alt="Seraph of the Sword" src="http://gatherer.wizards.com/Handlers/Image.ashx?size=small&amp;type=card&amp;name=Seraph%20of%20the%20Sword&amp;options=">&#10;              </a>&#10;            </div>&#10;          </div>&#10;          <br>&#10;          <b>&#10;            <a>Seraph of the Sword</a>&#10;          </b> – <i><b>Magic</b> 2014 Core Set </i> rare. The English word &quot;seraph&quot; is an assumed singular version of the Hebrew plural word &quot;seraphim,&quot; which came to English from Latin (via Greek). You probably know (or can guess from the creature type) the seraphim are a choir (or kind) of angel. Our singular word seraph's first known appearance is in Milton's <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise_Lost"><i>Paradise Lost</i></a>, from 1667 (<a href="http://www.dartmouth.edu/~milton/reading_room/pl/book_5/">Book 5</a>, in case you're curious). <p></p>&#10;      </div>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;</tbody></table>
