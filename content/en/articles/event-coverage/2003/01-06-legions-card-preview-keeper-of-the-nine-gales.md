There are a couple of different kinds of Constructed cards that competitive players go for whenever a new expansion set is released. There are the New Basics. These are the cards that we use to replace outmoded predecessors in existing decks. Look back at *Tempest*, the beatdown player's dream set. *Tempest's* most exciting one-drops - Jackal Pup and Mogg Fanatic - both made an immediate splash and fell into this first category, displacing Goblin Digging Team and Goblin Vandal in Dave Price's deck from the 1997 U.S. Nationals. Even when a card seems extremely powerful, or unusually fast, or both, it can "just" be a New Basic. Look at Flametongue Kavu. It was obvious from the start that this card was going to be played in whatever beatdown decks could cast it. Flametongue Kavu simultaneously made the defining card Two-Headed Dragon from Zvi Mowshowitz's Pro Tour-Chicago deck a little too small to be played any longer and took the Dragon's spot as Fires of Yavimaya decks' staple red creature. We often look first to the New Basics cards to make our decks a turn faster, to replace cards that may have rotated out as the new set arrived, or to improve a deck's chances against a field evolving with the same sweep of new options.

But there is another class of brand new Constructed cards as well. These are the cards that the Johnny deck designers of the world seek out when a new set appears. These are the cards that do something a little different, or something new, or something that hasn't been done - or at least done quite so well - in a while. These are the cards that evolve into massive card-drawing engines, or enable previously laughable strategies, or resurrect old favorites. They are the Yawgmoth's Wills, the Entombs and the Astral Slides of the **Magic** world.

In the case of the all-creature themed *Legions*, they might just be Tradewind Riders for a new generation.

Tradewind Rider was, without a doubt, one of the most powerful and defining creatures of its era. Few creatures have seen so much play in so many different kinds of decks... from Adam Katz's Top 4 at the first major event where the card was legal to Billy Jensen's victory at the inaugural Masters, Tradewind Rider has had quite a decorated career. It has locked up boards in combination with Armageddon and Winter Orb, paired with Wall of Blossoms to draw tons of extra cards and played the role of catch-all answer to every kind of permanent-based threat this side of Multani, Maro Sorcerer.

Tradewind's update is no slouch either.

The first, and ultimately most important, point of comparison between the classic and the Keeper of the Nine Gales is the mana cost. For two creatures of similar ability, Keeper of the Nine Gales is a full mana less expensive than Tradewind Rider. A one mana differential in cost is more than enough to separate even vastly less impressive - yet sought-after - cards from those deemed totally unplayable in Constructed **Magic**. Just look at the difference between Carnophage and Filthy Cur or Ironclaw Orcs and Goblin Hero.

When comparing cards that cost four mana with cards that only cost three, we have an even bigger incentive: the Birds of Paradise factor. Green mana acceleration creatures can turn three mana cost spells into two-drops. Going first, this can mean a lot when trying to slide a threat underneath counterspells. A lower overall mana cost will, many times, allow the Keeper of the Nine Gales to go active a full turn sooner than its predecessor... and given the ability to stunt the opponent's mana development, to say this is a "good thing" is an understatement.

The second point of comparison is Tradewind Rider's requirement of two other creatures to operate. Keeper of the Nine Gales also requires two teammates, but they must both be birds. In a lot of ways, this is a big strike against the newcomer. While Tradewind Rider was able to combine with whatever creatures the deck designer decided would be good to play - everything from Quirion Ranger to True Believer - Keeper of the Nine Gales requires a specialized deck to function properly.

The good news is that one of the classic Tradewind strategies - splashing Tradewind Rider in a green deck - is a good place to start with Keeper of the Nine Gales. Four copies of Birds of Paradise is a given in this style of deck; as we've said, Birds of Paradise is perfect to accelerate out the Keeper and that card's presence as a bird in the deck goes a long way in powering up the Keeper's bounce ability. The downside is that a deck with only Birds of Paradise and Keeper of the Nine Gales is not likely to draw enough birds to get the ball rolling with any regularity. Valuable slots that might once have been occupied by much more powerful creatures will have to be devoted to Sage Owls and Screaming Seahawks in order to keep the Keeper happy. Then again, Sage Owls will help you to draw your Keepers and Screaming Seahawk will make sure you have enough birds to operate, so it ain't terrible, just mostly bad.

We might also see monoblue decks based around Keeper of the Nine Gales alongside the birds that have already proven to be Constructed-quality. Both Thieving Magpie and Possessed Aven have shown up in competitive designs in the past, and there is no reason why they couldn't spearhead a new strategy with the coming of *Legions*. My only concern here is that with such expensive companions, the monoblue Keeper of the Nine Gales deck may be a bit slow to handle ultra-quick offenses led by cards like Wild Mongrel that have defined both Standard and Extended play of late.

Nevertheless, I think a more common strategy than either will be to run Keeper of the Nine Gales as a compliment to the white token-generating cards. Keeper of the Nine Gales has a great deal of synergy with Quiet Speculation and Battle Screech. Moving to Extended, it is also a natural combination with Ordered Migration. The important thing to note here is that while this new card is powerful, while it is valuably cheaper than its predecessor, Keeper of the Nine Gales is the kind of card that has to have a deck tailored *around* it, while Tradewind Rider could be played in combination with any number of different creatures, in a variety of potentially less focused decks.

The third point of comparison is size. Tradewind Rider was a busty 1/4 and could block most beatdown creatures comfortably. Keeper of the Nine Gales is a lot smaller, weighing in at 1/2. While Tradewind Rider was never played primarily for his ability to block, Keeper of the Nine Gales's smaller size is, nevertheless, something to think about when preparing your new decks for any expected metagame.

All of that being said, I think Keeper of the Nine Gales is an exciting new creature that will do a lot of good for Constructed **Magic**. Possibly its biggest contribution will be in forcing deck builders to look at spells that might not normally make the cut in their designs. In much the same way that slackers like Donate or High Tide have contributed admirably to the best strategies of their respective eras, we might see competitive decks featuring Lieutenant Kirtar or Mystic Familiar. Even Cloudchaser Eagle might just graduate from the sideboard. For a reusable ability as powerful as "return target permanent to its owner’s hand," concessions like these don't seem that large at all.
