As I write this, I am traveling back to my hometown on Cape Cod to celebrate the wedding of a friend. It's a happy occasion that I don't get to attend too often – most of my other friends are either already married or have sworn off the ritual. So this may be the last one for several years. The topic's on my mind, and it makes sense to bend an off-theme week column this way.

While there are certainly cultural differences in the specifics of weddings around the world, there are also many striking similarities. Weddings are a sort of universal language, and there's an awful lot of common understanding of the rituals and meanings behind most wedding ceremonies. Because of that, I thought that deck formats based on weddings might be a pleasant diversion. 

Before we get started, some disclaimers:

![](http://www.wizards.com/global/images/mtgcom_daily_aa229_picMain_en.jpg)
*   Your wedding, real or planned, may have operated differently from the formats I'm suggesting today. In fact, I certainly hope so! I don't need to hear the details.
*   Your marriage, real or imaginary, may involve a love that does not necessarily break down easily into **Magic** terms. Again, I certainly hope so! And again, you need not share this fact with me. Having been in love with the same woman for the last 19 years (and married for 13 of them), I am fully versed in the wonderful and strange language of love, and how fundamentally different it is from, say, the way a Serra Angel blocks a Shivan Dragon.
*   There are no doubt several kinds of marriage rituals, all around the world, that have intriguing details that I will not cover today. The fact that I do not mention them is not an attempt to judge their validity – merely an attempt to keep the article brief and readable.
*   I am not going to remember your favorite **Magic** card with a card title, artwork, or flavor text with connotations (or denotations) of marriage. Start getting over it now.
*   For those of you who either (a) never want to get married or (b) have been through a marriage that didn't work out, I'm sure you can all accept that the topic can still be intellectually interesting, if not necessarily emotionally satisfying to you right now. Carry on as best you can.

### New Format #1: Life Partners

*Basic idea*: Do we come out of the womb married? No, we do not. Why then, must we always start a team format with a predetermined teammate? Best to let fate – or a heavy bribe – decide your destiny. 

*How it works*: Start a typical chaos game. (It doesn't have to be an even number of players.) Players may play the following ability:

> *Tap all lands you control: Choose target player. You bid any amount of life and/or cards and/or permanents. That player may choose to TEAM with you. If that player does, pay that amount of life and/or cards and/or permanents. That player gains that much life, and/or draws that many cards, and/or puts that many 1/1 green snake tokens into play. You may attempt to TEAM only once per turn, and only whenever you could play a sorcery.*

"TEAM" means what you'd expect: that you and that player now define victory (or defeat) together. You both win the game when all other players lose, and/or when one of you achieves an absolute victory condition (e.g., Test of Endurance). Your life totals and turns remain separate. You may not attack each other. For purposes of cards like Troll Ascetic or Ravenous Rats, you are no longer opponents.

If you have to set more common sense restrictions, please do so. I would recommend the overall rule: when one player loses, the teammate loses as well. That ought to take care of most work-arounds.

You may only team with a single player over the course of a game. (No polygamy! This isn't an HBO series, and you're definitely no Bill Paxton.) You may not "un-team". It's all about commitment, baby.

*Strategic implications*: This is a highly "political" format, in the sense many players use the term (incorrectly, I might add). Put another way, you will see much chasing of the players in desirable board positions. This formalizes an arrangement already present in some groups: rather than a bunch of players coming together in loose associations for a turn or two and then stabbing each other in the back, you forge real, meaningful team relationships. Don't you feel more mature already?

*Derivatives*: If you have six or more players, allow teams of three to form. Slow-forming teams add an interesting layer of complexity to multiplayer **Magic**, since choosing your teammate is as important as the other deckbuilding and play choices you make.

You can also, if you like, choose to allow "divorce", which (for strategic balance reasons, and not because I'm trying to be funny) should take a full turn to take effect. A long "cool-off" period will reduce the temptation or ability to stab someone in the back suddenly and trade them in for a new partner. I like the idea of committing better, because it forces certain tactical considerations that you don't need to make if you're just in it for the short term.

### New Format #2: Unity Deck

*Basic idea*: In some marriage traditions, the main celebrants take two already lit candles and light a new candle together. (By the way, if you do this in your own real wedding ceremony, make sure you "pre-light" the single candle before the ceremony starts. Pre-lit candles are easier to light than new candles, as my wife and I found out the hard way. Man, waiting for that sucker to light was the longest 30 seconds of my life.)

So why not do the same thing with decks?

*How it works*: An even number of players starts a chaos game with whatever decks they like, each separated into two piles: land and non-land. At the beginning of the game, create random pairs of players. Each pair shuffles their two piles of land cards together, and their two piles of non-land cards together. (It is okay if a non-land pile has more than four copies of a given card.) Then, each player draws three cards from their shared land pile and four cards from their shared non-land pile to get an opening hand. No mulligans.

Players still take individual turns – but during the draw step, that player must choose to draw from the team's land or non-land pile.

The pair is a team. "TEAM" here would mean the same thing as it did before – just common sense teaming.

*Strategic implications*: The random selection of pairs is probably necessary to ensure a minimum of attempts to "break" the format with complementary decks/strategies. Sure, you'll still see more Aether Bursts than you'd probably like – but it shouldn't knock the evening too far out of balance.

*Derivatives*: ensure deck diversity by assigning each player a two-color "guild" (or mono-color) before the night begins. Or, ensure deck redundancy by forcing everyone into the *same* guild or color.

In addition to deck decisions, you can play around with the teaming function. Maybe it's enough that the decks are together: why must the players be? Let them attack each other with commonly drawn weapons.

### New Format #3: Children

*Basic idea*: Not every marriage results in children; but the vast majority do. Why shouldn't your **Magic** format try for a bundle of joy?

*How it works*: You need at least six players. (You will need at least three teams of two players each.) Only four players start the game, paired up into two teams of "parents". The parent teams start play normally. You can go with the new, sanctioned Two-Headed Giant format (shared turns and 40 life), or you can simply do individuals in turn who act as TEAMS. Whatever you like.

At a given threshold (see ideas for thresholds below), one of the two teams becomes PREGNANT. At that point, two more players enter the turn order begin a "parallel game". These two players do not interact with each other or the other players for three rounds. (They're too busy gestating!)

At the end of the third round, the players are "born" as teammates of the parent team. Obviously, the team that did *not* give birth now has a tougher task. Of course, if there are more players waiting in the wings, then other child-teams may be born… if new thresholds are met!

If a parent team dies, the child team may continue. If a child team dies, a parent team may continue.

Possible thresholds:

*   At the beginning of a player's upkeep, if they have less than five individual life (or less than ten shared life), they become pregnant.
*   *\[Allowable after four or more turns.\]* At the beginning of a player's upkeep, if they have exactly seven cards in hand, they may reveal their hand. If they have no land cards in hand, and less than two land cards in play, they become pregnant.
*   *\[Allowable after four or more turns.\]* At the beginning of a player's upkeep, if they have no non-land permanents in play, they may reveal their hand. If they have no non-land cards in hand, they become pregnant.
*   Each team starts with a warning counter. Whenever combat damage lethal to an opponent would resolve, or whenever a spell or ability that would win the game is played, the controller of that damage, spell, or ability turns in their warning counter and the damage/spell/ability is prevented/countered. The other team becomes pregnant.

You can also come up with your own ideas.

*Strategic implications*: Obviously, whoever gets the child team first will have a distinct advantage. The thresholds should be set so that the team getting the child has probably taken a bit of a beating first.

*Derivatives*: This format is easily modified to have normal multiplayer games "re-involve" players who get killed early. They can become embryos, ready to re-enter the game if certain conditions are met. How and why this happens are matters best left to individual groups.

### "That's Easy to Break!"

One more disclaimer. If you're reading this article and feel a format is too easy to "break", then you can certainly modify it. But allow for the possibility that I see the same path to breakage, and still believe many readers would benefit from trying the format as written. Not all players and groups are going to see the same things you do. And you will not see everything that someone even more experienced in alternate formats – say, me – may bring to a format that seems disarmingly "easy".
