### 

I went to my first baseball game in 1978, a night time affair between my beloved Toronto Blue Jays and the far-better-than-they-are-now Milwaukee Brewers, which the hometown boys lost 5-3. That night, amid the screams of fans, the beer-soaked ground showered repeatedly in peanut shells, basking in the steady glow of the towering lights at Exhibition Stadium, I began a love affair with America’s Pastime which has survived hiccups and strikes to this day.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>Pro sports provide us with the greatest dramas of all…</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Since that game, I’ve watched countless contests over twenty-five seasons, each filled with moments upon moments of the purest form of drama I have ever known: the chase of the fly ball, the timeless confrontation between pitcher and hitter, the ball that curves as it approaches the foul pole… in baseball, there’s always something to take your breath away. With apologies to luminaries like Joe Carter and Mookie Wilson though, nothing has come close to Kirk Gibson’s home run.

It was the first game of the 1988 World Series. The all-powerful Oakland A’s, a team of eight-foot-tall powerhouse hitters and pitchers with arms of iron, were leading the comparatively meek Los Angeles Dodgers 4-3, as well they should have been. Oakland’s best hitter, Jose Canseco had hit a grand slam early in the game off LA’s only hope Orel Hershiser. Now, the best relief pitcher in history, Dennis Eckersley was on the mound for Oakland, needing one last out with the tying run on first base.

With his pitcher due up, LA Manager Tommy Lasorda decided now was the time to use Kirk Gibson as a pinch hitter. Gibson, LA’s best player and emotional leader in the regular season, was playing on borrowed time. The man had been playing on bad knees for over a decade and now it had finally caught up to him. They so crippled him that he couldn’t be asked to take the field that night. Walking up to the plate, Gibson looked like he’d lost his wheelchair.

Eckersley quickly got the count to 0-2, but Gibson wouldn’t give up without a fight, fouling off two of the next five pitches in getting the count full. The next pitch would decide everything. He looked truly awful trying to swing, as the strength generally comes from the legs, a luxury he didn’t have. Eckersley threw one final pitch and Gibson swung, almost entirely from the wrists and hit the ball deep to right field and over the fence for an impossible game-ending home run. The moment was so devastating for Oakland that, despite being the better team, they lost the series 4-1, but the rest was all elementary. When Gibson hit that ball, time stopped forever.

Now, this was fifteen years ago and I still remember the TV screen. Mickey Hatcher running around like a rooster with the head cut off, Eckersley slowly walking back to the dugout, Gibson’s arms raised as he practically crawled around the bases. Pro sports provide us with the greatest dramas of all, because there’s so little and so much at stake all at once and once in a while, Kirk Gibson hits that home run and dreams really do come true.

While not televised to half a billion homes around the world, the Pro Tour has had its moments. With the prize curve far steeper than the contract structure of Major League Baseball, the same pride is on the line, but with it the possibility of a new path, a new future and a lot of money that the competitors don’t always have before that pivotal match. What follows is a look at some of the Pro Tour’s most memorable moments, be they successes or failures, in Pro Tour history. They are our legacy:

### “Do You Have Any Fast Effects?”

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/b5/8a/b58ab4c26a39de5cec210c6bd453124c2ff605abbbbdcf489aa6068f285631d5.jpg"><br><i>Borer made a… grave mistake.</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Possessed of as thorough a natural instinct for the game as anyone in its history, Terry Borer routinely showed up to major events and thrived without having seen the cards before, so when Wizards decided to have a sealed deck Pro Tour with the never-before-seen *Mirage* set, things were bound to go well for the Canadian. They did, until the Quarterfinals, when Borer and the rest of the world came to a fuller understanding of the rules of the game.

Taking place in the Pro Tour’s first season and only three years after **Magic’s** inception, when today’s game still faces tough-to-answer rules disputes, there was bound to be some lack of clarification. Borer, facing Darwin Kastle in the quarters, had three creatures to Darwin’s two with Grave Servitude in hand and Kastle on low enough life that any of the creatures, instantly enchanted by Servitude, would be enough to kill the YMG big man, essentially locking up the game and match. Borer attacked, Darwin blocked and Terry asked his no infamous question: “do you have any effects?”

At the time, the rules of priority were not well understood by just about anyone but level five judge Tom Wylie, but when Tom heard Terry’s fateful question then saw him attempt to cast the Servitude, he was forced to interject, informing Borer that he no longer had the opportunity to cast spells before damage. Darwin survived the attack, swung back with all his men and won the game and match, advancing all the way to the finals before losing to German Frank Adler. From then on, people fully understood the importance of that question.

### Justice’s Double Consult

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/95/6e/956ec21791ceb7d34ece848d4f27a3aab9478c4790e1c636c1817c460c9d8357.jpg"><br><i>Unfortunately for Justice, his burly protector was in hand.</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

You’d think that playing at World Championships with an uncastable Sleight of Mind in your deck would be costly, but Tom Champheng, the 1996 World Champion proved otherwise. Playing White Weenie in a sea of Necro, the Australian played a Sleight to go with his many protection from black creatures, but made what should have been a fatal mistake in recording his decklist when he forgot to add Adarkar Wastes to it, leaving the blue card limp. Fortunately, Champheng was able to make his way to the Finals, where he faced off against reigning American Champion and the world’s best player at the time, Mark Justice.

Justice was playing Necro just like everyone else, but evidently doing so better than the rest of them until he made a fatal error in the tournament’s final game to cost himself the championship. The mistake came when Champheng threatened to wrestle control of the game from Mark by casting Armageddon. Justice stopped and thought over his options for a moment before casting Demonic Consultation, then cast another one in response, naming Dark Ritual for each immediately. The result of the two Consults took Mark so deep into his deck that he didn’t have enough time left to cast his one remaining threat, Ishan’s Shade and attack enough times to get the win before decking himself. If he’d waited on naming the target for the second, he could have chosen swamp, the most plentiful card in his deck, in order to cut his losses, cast the Shade a few turns later and potentially won. He eventually would return to the finals at Paris, where he again lost, this time to Mike Long.

### LA Riots

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTTOK01/833.jpg)

Before the final of the second PT-LA, Mark Rosewater held one of the funnier interviews of all time, asking Tommi Hovi a number of lengthy and detailed questions to which he repeatedly received one word answers. This was being viewed by virtually the entire Pro Tour on a very large screen at the site and by the end, the crowd was in hysterics, with Rosewater begging to be pulled off the floor. Then the mood changed.

Facing American David Mills, Hovi could only watch as his finals opponent got disqualified from the tournament for receiving his third warning of the tournament for casting spells then tapping lands, illegal at the time. Mills disqualification shocked the mostly American crowd which, led by Mark Justice and Mills’s teammate Mike Long, ran amok, with yells of protest and light physical abuse to the premises. Mills’s penalty was eventually downgraded to match loss, allowing him to keep his second place prize, but the final, the only one in PT history to go uncompleted, was awarded to Hovi, who would eventually go on to become the first player to win two Pro Tour finals. Mills would become the first to lose two.

### “Best Rats Ever”

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/79/1f/791ff633146acc44c39db17eabafe873e705ed4c9348b49b29854a17be1efd7e.jpg"><br><i>We told you they were ravenous.</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Mike Pustilnik has won multiple Grand Prix and a Masters, but without a doubt, his crowning achievement came in the form of his victory at 2001 PT-LA. Playing in the final of the *Invasion* Block Rochester Draft PT, Mikey squared off against the Pro Tour’s newest superstar, Kamiel Cornelissen, who had just finished second at PT-Chicago and then drafted the best deck in this Top 8. Everyone agreed Kamiel should win.

In this, the final chapter of the **Magic**-Queen Mary story, the first four games split down the middle. Kamiel grabbed the advantage early on in game five, getting Mikey P down to six life before a kicked Dismantling Blow took care of the Power Armor that had been wrecking him. That started turning things Mikey’s way.

He played a one mana tapper and Shackled Kamiel’s Kavu Climber, then Exluded Serpentine Kavu before Kamiel cast Tribal Flames on the Apprentice, but the real nail in the coffin came on Mikey’s next turn. With one card in Kamiel’s hand, Pustilnik untapped, drew and cast Ravenous Rats, forcing Cornelissen to discard Rith, the Awakener. The soft-spoken Mikey P uttered “best Rats ever” and the crowd exploded. A few turns later, the title was his.

### Kai Topdecks Again

I’ve lost track of the number of Sundays Kai Budde has survived, but I think he’d happily tell you his luckiest trip through those three match maulings came at PT-New Orleans. Playing the latest in a number of Trix incarnations, Kai, along with teammate Ben Klauser, had made yet another Top 8.

In the Quarterfinals, facing off with Darwin Kastle, Budde, facing death in two turns with no cards in hand pulled off the impossible, drawing Illusions of Grandeur then Donate off the top of his library among other memorable draws. In the Semis, it was much of the same, facing off against another YMGer Dave Humphreys, in a match that suggested the opening hand was only window dressing, but it was in the tournament’s final game against Tomi Walamies that he made his ultimate top deck, one that was long overdue.

With the first four games split between the two Europeans, Kai cast a main-phase Intuition for three Accumulated Knowledges to take advantage of Tomi’s tapping out, meaning the Finn could cast Call of the Herd. The token started attacking for three a turn as Kai drew three cards, then four, but kept on drawing land after land. He was able to hold Tomi’s other spells in check, but the pesky elephant, joined by a flashed back partner, was able to get Kai down to four. At the end of Tomi’s turn, Kai cast Stroke of Genius for three more cards, again, no solutions and untapped. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/40/b3/40b3cca43c57dd01cef837a9cc894192b2f689c19cc87f29db4bcd19292d570c.jpg)

Kai drew Morphling. With six lands and Sapphire Medallion, he was able to play it and block one of the two rushing elephants, leaving himself at one life. Morphling attacked, then attacked again, then after Tomi Fact or Fictioned, Kai, countered the Finn’s own Morphling. Two attacks later, Tomi was at a loss for life and had no alternative but to extend the hand.

Those are my favorites. If you think I missed one, post it in the forums, that should make for some interesting reading and if I think it bears mentioning, maybe I’ll post it in next week’s Wise Words, which I’ll be writing from Pittsburgh, though only as a one week, pre-PT visitor.

Before I close shop this week, *Sideboard.com* received a number of emails about a joke I threw into last week’s column about the voters in the poll mentioning there having to be American. Among pro players, there’s always a lot of trash talking and ribbing and among it is the attitude from American players that they’re the best and the response from those of us from outside those borders is often to try to keep them in check.

In case they needed clarifying, those comments were in no way meant to be anything but similarly lighthearted ribbing and while they may have been a mistake, I felt I could poke that fun because of the fact that **Magic** transcends those political boundaries that often fight to keep us apart. Canadian, German, American, we’re all gamers and I like to think that on some level, that means we can get casual once in a while. If my attitude offended anyone, I apologize for the offense taken: just know that underneath my sarcasm, I’m still the same guy who tried to move to the U.S. three weeks ago, so I obviously couldn’t think too poorly of your country.

Have a good week.
