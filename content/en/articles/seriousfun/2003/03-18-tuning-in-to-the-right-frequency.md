### Why casual players should care about card advantage

First, a pre-emptive strike to the "how could you forget…" crowd:

*   Howling Mine
*   Horn of Greed
*   Prosperity
*   Viseling
*   arbitrarily large X combos with Stroke of Genius or precursor or derivative

Lovely. Now, I'd like to get on with the article I'd prefer to write.

### CARD DRAWING: IT'S NOT JUST FOR SPIKES ANYMORE

It doesn't happen very often, but every once in a while I'll get an email from a reader with a bone to pick with me. (Amazingly enough, not everyone agrees with me all the time. Personally, I think most of these readers suffer from sloppy leftover antagonism based in Rosewater's column the day before, but I can't prove that.) Typically, the beef goes something like this:

> Why do you insist upon things like 60 cards, and four copies of cards, and a smooth mana curve in casual duel and multiplayer decks? Isn't **Magic** supposed to be about fun? I like my 293-card monstrosity that never gets anything out before turn eight. I take it wherever I go, including the shower. Only a sadistic fascist like you would tell me to dismantle my sweet-smellin' cardboard baby.

Okay, I made the shower and baby part up. But there's a veiled hint of hurt in some of these emails that I would betray a community thriving on inefficiency. Big, huge, inconsistent decks that make you wait for 20 turns before they do anything are "fun." They "never play the same way twice." They "usually die, except for one time when I got all six pieces of the combo in place!"

It is not my place to define fun for other people. But I'll throw my definition out there, and you tell me whether or not I'm close to yours. To me, fun in **Magic** is getting to showcase your deck's tricks. Those decks can be geared toward a win, or they can be geared toward just being a nuisance, or they can be geared toward helping everyone but yourself, or whatever. But the point is, *you get to show people how clever you are*. How could that not be the point, in a game that blends creativity and strategy? There's nothing to be ashamed of here.

So if your goal is showing the table what your deck can do – generate a 130/130 trampler by turn four, or put several pieces together in a combo, or set up a balanced board position that is ready to handle most multiplayer threats – then you ought to care how often you can do it. You ought to care about frequency.

![Howling Mine](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/0b/e10bf7952d823bf84eedc64600e52e258235e55b90d4b7739bf80f28de4cf872.jpg)

And there are two keys to frequency: card drawing, and card advantage. The two concepts are tightly related, but not identical. Casual **Magic** players should care about both.

You know what? I think I will take a closer look at that Howling Mine.

### DRAWING VS. ADVANTAGE

Just about every casual player worth his or her salt has at least one Howling Mine – and what they have, they have in decks. It's probably the most frequently played card on multiplayer tables, barring basic land. And it's a heck of a card – you can use it to try to gain the table's trust, or accelerate to your key cards, or put everyone in an uncomfortable position (e.g., Iron Maiden), or animate the inner Grizzly Bear with Karn, Silver Golem. Whatever works for you. It's great card drawing.

But it's not card advantage.

That's because everyone's drawing, not just you. Card advantage is when the cards you draw and use are more effective than the cards your opponents use. This can happen one of two basic ways:

*   you draw more often than your opponents; or
*   you kill off more than one enemy card with a single card

The second option is interesting, especially in multiplayer environments: one for one cards like Terror or Counterspell just aren't good enough, so much of the time. But that's for another week. Here in card drawing week, we're going to look at that first method of card advantage, and how we can make it happen.

You've essentially got three options. First, you can play a series of cantrips. These tend to be instants or sorceries that have a primary effect, and then add the second effect of "draw a card." (Think Zap.)

Second, you can play permanents that have activated (Treasure Trove) or triggered (Mercadian Atlas) abilities to give you card-drawing options. These tend to be more desirable in group play, since repeated use is an excellent quality in an environment where you have to slog through multiple enemies.

Third, you can take advantage of *Onslaught*'s new cycling cards that give you an effect as you draw a new card. Since the cycling effect is uncounterable, this is very attractive in those groups that have heavy numbers of blue mages.

Not very good against flyers, perhaps. But my point is illustration – you can substitute the solutions you need. For example, I put a single copy of Predator, Flagship in there to give a glimpse of the other side of card advantage – repeatable destruction.

Speaking of repeatable destruction. To close out, here's a variation on a Words of War deck I have. Honest salesmanship: the similar deck I currently use has mixed results, mainly because it can't keep a creature on the board and our play group has a heavy emphasis on creature decks nowadays. The mana you get from Thran Turbine *can* be used to pay for Well of Knowledge, since your draw step is part of your upkeep (this became true with *Sixth Edition*). This makes for a pretty good repeatable situation, if you have Words of War out. Most players are already familiar with the synergy between the Words and Arcanis. Add Pursuit of Knowledge, Mindless Automaton, and Urza's Blueprints for draws without mana cost, and you can build yourself a nice, cheap series of Shocks to clean off the board, finish off opponents, or whatever.
