With rotations made by Eighth Edition, at least among the established archetypes, the winner in Standard appears to be u-g. On the most basic level, u-g's enemies are severely weakened. Losing Duress and Counterspell are severe disincentives to playing monoblack control and Psychatog. Sure, those two decks didn't beat u-g with those particular cards - Duress might have been good at clearing the way for Visara the Dreadful, but Duress itself wasn't the problem - but these Eighth Edition changes mean that fewer players will opt to play the decks that are most dangerous for a u-g opponent.

Moreover, r-g isn't badly hurt at all. While r-g loses Llanowar Elves and Karplusan Forest, it gets off pretty easily. R-g can either replace Llanowar Elves with Birds of Paradise (making the heavy colored mana requirements of Phantom Centaur, Violent Eruption, and potentially Blistering Firecat easier to manage), or just up the land count to 24 in order to incorporate Shivan Oasis over Karplusan Forest. When you factor in that r-g is quite a winnable matchup for u-g, and that more people will be flocking to the team of Wild Mongrel, Elephant Guide, and Volcanic Hammer, the environment seems pretty good for the u-g mage. That's right. U-g just has to remember to pack his Unsummons, and he should be good to go in tournaments from Berlin to Atlanta.

But which u-g mage exactly is to benefit from Eighth Edition?

All of them.

### U-G Madness

One of the curious things about u-g is that it is such a diverse archetype. When we think of u-g in Standard, we generally think of u-g madness, as typified by the deck most loudly advocated by Jeff Cunningham and company. Nicolai Herzog recently won this year's European Championships with a very Cunningham-flavored (if modified) u-g listing, which prompted Sideboard's Week in Reviewer to remind everyone that he is "a sheer genius".

U-g Madness is the archetype that comes to mind when we think of the broken potential of Wild Mongrel, but also an archetype that sometimes folds to an overload of creature kill spells. The Cunningham touch is the removal of Merfolk Looter - a onetime universal 4-of - for the Quiet Speculation package. This is the type of u-g madness deck that will come out of Eighth Edition... after all, there will be no Merfolk Looter available.

Besides getting a lift from environmental factors, the departure of its enemies and the increased numerosity of its prey, u-g madness is one of the few blue decks that benefits from the Eighth Edition Core Set changes. While most blue mages are still shaking their fists at the sky, defiant at the loss of 10-year veteran Counterspell, u-g madness is quite happy with the arrival of its replacement: Mana Leak.

I predict that the u-g madness archetype will go through another basic build shift, as it did from a deceptively controlling eight counter deck to a Wonder beatdown deck, and then later when Cunningham replaced Merfolk Looter with Aquamoeba and Quiet Speculation. We are already seeing the migration of Phantom Centaurs into the main deck four mana slot when they once didn't even make the sideboard. Players like Kai Budde himself are lamenting the erratic opening hands possible when a deck designed to dump cards into its graveyard doesn't draw Careful Study or Wild Mongrel. At the same time, cards like Withered Wretch in the black beatdown decks are becoming more popular. Quiet Speculation, once a guaranteed AncestralRecall stapled to a Demonic Tutor for two mana, can now prove a liability. Roar of the Wurm is a terrible cast on seven mana, and not particularly superior to Phantom Centaur on four mana. With less space devoted directly to the graveyard, slots for Mana Leak suddenly open up.

In a perfect world, u-g threshold with Mana Leak and bounce seems a formidable choice to me. It has low mana requirements and can get in the face of almost any opponent. Much of the viability of u-g threshold rests with red. If red rises as much as we expect with Eighth Edition, few strong players will go the zombie route. Graveborn Muse is just a lot less impressive when the other guy has Volcanic Hammer. In a world with lots of mountains and few swamps, u-g threshold is a better deck than u-g madness. Its men are bigger faster, it can do more when under the gun in the early turns of the game, and it has access to Worship out of the sideboard thanks to Flooded Strand and Windswept Heath. Few are the Sparksmiths willing to dance with Nimble Mongoose.

For all of these reasons, u-g threshold should be considered a roguish choice in the expected metagame. Choosing it over u-g madness will likely be done only when specific twists in the environment change the incentives to specific cards in these two related archetypes.

U-g flashback has arguably the most raw power of all the u-g decks. Who can realistically beat a draw that opens with a perfect Careful Study, follows up with a deceptively annoying Catalyst Stone, and then ends turn three up a Deep Analysis and a Roar of the Wurm? Even the less dramatic draws often yield a full array of Wurm tokens in play in the first few turns of the game. On top of Careful Study and Mental Note, Quiet Speculation itself is quite a solid threshold enabler for Grizzly Fate, which is one of the reasons that Rob Dougherty added the versatile Werebear to the offensive lineup of his version. More than one opponent has been caught with insufficient mana available for his Moment's Peace or Firebolt because of the flashback mage's Catalyst Stone.

For all its big spell potential, u-g flashback can be even more clunky in terms of uneven draws than u-g madness. While it sometimes outclasses Deranged Hermit, there is no getting around the fact that Grizzly Fate costs five mana the first time around. Capable of dominating any archetype with bomb after bomb, the fact remains that the deck can be raced by a tempo-oriented opponent (Zvi Mowshowitz's Threshold deck [took out](/en/events/2002/worlds02/round-15-zvi-mowshowitz-vs-steve-omahoney-schwartz) Steve's ostensibly more powerful deck at Worlds on the back of Aether Burst and Standstill).

Due to its hybridized nature, u-g flashback is even more vulnerable to Withered Wretch than the other two main branches of the u-g color combination. As [I warned about](/en/articles/event-coverage/2003/02-03-dissecting-withered-wretch) black's second-best bear several months ago, much of the potential diversity of the u-g decks has been stamped out. What might be the most powerful version of the deck can have its plan disrupted either on the Quiet Speculation front, losing its main avenue for card advantage, and also on the threshold front, yielding less powerful Grizzly Fates and Werebears. In this sense, "Quiet Roar" reminds me a great deal of 2000-era Tinker decks. It is an overwhelmingly powerful assemblage of mana and expensive threats with a proactive plan designed to utilize specific synergies (rather than one built with specific metagame solutions in mind), but one that is at the same time vulnerable to a fast two mana hoser played by a minority of decks. U-g flashback is the kind of deck that appeals most to the profile of a player planning to overwhelm the other guy by "doing broken things" than figuring out how specific cards in his deck will interact with specific cards in the opponent's deck; for all its potential shortcomings, I don't think any deck in the upcoming environment can look quite as slick or powerful as u-g flashback when it gets its bustiest draw.

In that vein, Mana Leak fits into the u-g flashback plan almost exactly as it did in suicide brown when Zvi Mowshowitz and the Mogg Squad splashed that permission spell into the historically proactive Tinker deck. With a good draw, Quiet Roar can almost always deploy a fast threat capable of winning the game all by itself in the first three or so turns of the game. Much of the time, it can clutter the entire play area with threats in the first five. Pretty much all this deck wants is to not get hammered with an ill-timed Aether Burst (or, these days it seems, a Patriarch's Bidding). Tsang, Dougherty, and Prosak all ran Envelop in Cleveland, and while that card certainly filled the role, *Odyssey* Block competitors didn't have to worry about countering Smothers, Starstorms, or Exalted Angels. Unlike in a long game with a true control deck, a proactive deck can use Mana Leak as a hard counter a surprising amount of the time because it is generally trying to preserve board position rather than stop oncoming threats.

While I would personally consider u-g flashback as a third option to the other, more popular, u-g decks, it is hard to deny that this powerhouse has a certain something that makes you want to shuffle up the cards and see if you can't iron out the specifics.
