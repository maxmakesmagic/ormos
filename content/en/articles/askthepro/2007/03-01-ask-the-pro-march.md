![](https://i.ormos.uk/a1/c3/a1c32cb4cea706bb480729e50669771d75d76b266de86e82df6e9d8f4d16d6ec.jpg)Wizards of the Coast welcomes Hall of Famer Raphaël Lévy as the featured player here at "Ask the Pro." A fixture on the Pro Tour for the last eight years, Raphaël is uniquely positioned to answer your questions about the life of a professional **Magic** player, give a historical perspective on the game and high-level tournament scene, share stories about travelling the world, and talk about the role **Magic** plays in his life.

Seventh on the lifetime Pro Points list, Raphaël began his Pro Tour career back at Pro Tour-Paris in 1997. He became a regular on the tour starting at the 1998 World Championships. Since then he hasn't missed a Pro Tour, an astounding streak of 49 consecutive events. He was recently honored as a member of the [**Magic** Pro Tour Hall of Fame](/en/articles/event-coverage/undated/tournaments-events-trading-card-game-magic-the-gathering-2)'s 2006 class and was inducted at the 2006 World Championships in Paris. Send your question, along with your name and location, via this email form. Answers will be posted every Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday. 

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>March 24, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: I was recently at a PTQ where I went 5-2 and got ninth place. :( What do you do as a player to overcome the agony of finishing one spot out of a Top 8 or 16?<br> - Thanks, Ro <br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hi Ro, </p>&#10;<p>First of all, to ease your pain, I wanted to let you know that in most French PTQs, some 5-1-1s don't even make the cut. But I know, it usually doesn't help to know that there's always less fortunate than yourself. </p>&#10;<p>Anyways, a lot has been written about your case:</p>&#10;<li>No stone cannot be polished without friction, no man cannot perfect his experiment without test. <i>Confucius</i></li>&#10;<li>Better a patient man than a warrior, a man who controls his temper than one who takes a city. <i>The Bible, Proverbs 14:29</i></li>&#10;<li>Anger leads to hate. Hate leads to suffering. <i>Master Yoda, The Phantom Menace.</i></li>&#10;<p>What it means basically is that it's too bad for you, but there's no need in being angry or agonising. You don't have to throw chairs around or punch a wall. You'll make it soon enough.</p>&#10;<p></p><li>Learn and move on. <i>Raphaël, ATP, March 24, 2007.</i></li>&#10;<p></p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>March 22, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: How much is a Pro Point really worth in terms of dollars, assuming you're going to be at least Level 4 or so. Obviously it depends on a lot of things, but when you pros think of justifying the cost to attend an event, what kind of approximate expected value do you give 1 Pro Point?<br> -Doron Blake<br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hi again Doron,</p>&#10;<p>The answer to your question depends on many different factors:</p>&#10;<p></p><li>The Pro level you want to reach</li>&#10;<li>The Pro level you currently have</li>&#10;<p></p>&#10;<p>First of all, how much are the different levels worth:</p>&#10;<p><b>Level 3: 20 points</b><br> $500 per Pro Tour (4 Pro Tours a year + Worlds) <br> = 5*500 <br> = $2,500</p>&#10;<p><b>Level 4: 30 points</b><br> $1,000 per PT<br> 1 trip per season (estimated at $700)<br> = (5*1,000)+700<br> = $5,700</p>&#10;<p><b>Level 5: 40 points</b><br> $1,500 per PT<br> $250 per GP (expected to attend 12 GPs)<br> All the trips to PTs<br> = (1,500*5)+(12*250)+(5*700)<br> = $14,000</p>&#10;<p><b>Level 6: 50 points</b><br> $2,000 per PT<br> $500 per GP (expected to attend 12 GPs)<br> All the trips to PTs<br> All the hotel on PT locations (estimated at $200)<br> = (2,000*5)+(12*500)+(5*700)+(5*200)<br> = $20,500</p>&#10;<p>The simplest way to calculate how much a Pro Point is worth would be to divide each bonus you get when you level up by how many points you need. For example, up to Level 3, each Pro Point would be worth:<br> $2,500/20<br> = $125</p>&#10;<p>You also have to keep in mind that the first nine points (or first 19 up to Level 3) of each step are worth nothing if you don't get the 10th one (or 20th).</p>&#10;<p>From there, you have to calculate differently and not consider point individually, but as a part of a set of 10. For example, to level up from Level 4 to 5, 10 points are worth $8,300. </p>&#10;<p>When hunting for Pro Points, you will have to calculate how much you have to spend in plane tickets and hotel rooms to capture the 10 points. Thanks to stats you have about yourself, you can know how many points on average you make at each GP stop and see how many you have to play in order to make the 10 points. If the total expenses are superior to the expected value of the level you want to reach, then you may decide not to go. </p>&#10;<p>To sum it up, the Pro Point raises in value at the end of a season, when players have set their objectives and want to get the 10th point that would allow them to level up. As for an example, when I was fighting for Level 5 last season, I considered every point being worth between $800 and $900.</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>March 20, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: Have you or any of the other dedicated pros you know tried to get Wizards to make the Grand Prix circuit a little more user-friendly in terms of travelling? Looking at the schedule, there are five GPs within a month and they're absolutely all over the place. It seems like Wizards might achieve better turnout from you guys if it went something like Kyoto-Singapore-Amsterdam-Massachusetts-Dallas, rather than Dallas-Singapore-Amsterdam-Mass-Kyoto...<br> - Doron Blake <br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hello Doron,</p>&#10;<p>GPs are a lot more important now than what they used to be. One Pro Point for a top 64, three for a top 16 – same as a PT top 200 – and even more for a Top 8, they are mandatory stops if you want to achieve a good season or reach a high level in the Pro Players Club. So basically, Pro players are bound to travel – a lot – to capture PT Points.</p>&#10;<p>Wizards is often trying to make pro players' lives easier and better. The Pro Players Club they set up was a good start. The Pro Lounge sure makes the PTs cozier. But there's a lot more things that could be improved.</p>&#10;<p>Wizards of the Coast has offices around the world, and each region's office is in charge of the local Grand Prix. When you factor in all that goes into putting on an event (booking the site, coordinating schedules, having it not conflict with other major events like PTs or Prereleases, etc.), the offices on each continent can't perfectly coordinate when the GPs happen. That's why the GPs were so close to each other and that some of them take place at the same time (Grand Prix-Strasbourg and Grand Prix-Columbus, for example). The fact that they were all over the place, and not really easily reachable, was out of anybody's control. It was announced, unofficially, that they would work more carefully on the GP schedule next season.</p>&#10;<p>I threw the idea some time ago, to create some kind of official &quot;syndicate&quot; of players, to talk about issues like these. For legal reasons, it's impossible to make such thing. We'll probably manage at some point to set up meetings between the Wizards' staff and us, pro players, to share our ideas and wishes (it already happens unofficially at major events, where we give feedback). I don't know how much influence we can have on organized play, but it could be a good way to improve it. </p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>March 17, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: What's it like playing an opponent who doesn't speak your language, and how do you effectively communicate with them?<br> - Greg Schwartz <br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hi Greg,</p>&#10;<p>Magic is now spread worldwide and it's common to see players who don't speak the same language playing against each other, at any level of competition. The tournament rules ask players to make an effort to make sure their opponents know what's going on. </p>&#10;<p>Even when players don't have a language in common, <b>Magic</b> has an international vocabulary that doesn't require them to speak that much. &quot;Play,&quot; &quot;draw,&quot; &quot;attack,&quot; &quot;block,&quot; &quot;stack,&quot; and &quot;go&quot; are words known by everyone ... and it's mostly all the words you will need to know to have a normal game going. </p>&#10;<p>To avoid problems, you will have to be extra careful when you're playing under these circumstances. Even without words, you can manage to make yourself understood clearly by your opponent by using signs, for example. In the worst case, you can ask a judge who can translate. </p>&#10;<p>Misunderstandings due to the language barrier can lead to strange situations where your opponent takes advantage of the fact that he &quot;didn't understand what was going on&quot; and gets himself a &quot;take back&quot; for a mistake he just made. That's something you can avoid by being super clear.</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>March 15, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: Do the best Limited Pros have an encyclopaedic knowledge of cards in any given set? For example, would a Pro consider <a>Word of Seizing</a> every time <img alt="3 Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7f/5d/7f5d2f1e55eaa9d19a9a23027b63bfbb5b39dbf8642160dda1a477a03df23a6a.gif"><img alt="Red Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif"><img alt="Red Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif"> is left untapped, or do they tend to just think about common tricks?<br> - James S., Glasgow <br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hi James,</p>&#10;<p>When the format is new, pros learn all the common tricks as fast as possible. They are the ones that will see the most plays and that you'll have to play around the most. When you play around a trick your opponent may hold, you have to take in consideration a couple of factors:</p>&#10;<ul>&#10;<p>1. How will the trick he may have affect the game if you run into it<br> 2. What are the chances of him having it </p>&#10;</ul>&#10;<p>Most of the common tricks will have a limited impact on the game. They're often spot pump or removal spells that usually don't provide card advantage naturally (as in: &quot;destroy two creatures&quot;). As they are commons, they are likely to be in your opponent's deck and with some experience, you will be able to play around them all.</p>&#10;<p>It's a different story for rare tricks. Not everyone has the time to practice enough to know them all and what to expect when your opponent has access to any kind of mana. In general, before making a play, you will have to ask yourself &quot;What can my opponent do now?&quot; Try to gather all the information you have to figure it out. The mana open on your opponent's side, the cards you may have passed in the draft, the way he has been playing so far. Most rare tricks are devastating when you find the right window to play them. I'm thinking about &quot;<a>Word of Seizing</a>&quot; or &quot;<a>Draining Whelk</a>&quot; type of game-changing plays. Both can be played around and I know that when I see my opponent passing the turn with cards in hand and <img alt="4 Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/36/29/3629288966daa042decf01d3752789ab6676fddd67c3679fa580cd3baecae158.gif"><img alt="Blue Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif"><img alt="Blue Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif"> open, I'm thinking about <a>Draining Whelk</a>. </p>&#10;<p>After you played a large number of games in a given format and if you've paid enough attention, you'll end up playing around all kind of tricks, common or rare. Sometimes it won't matter as you can't simply play around everything, but having a perfect knowledge of all cards will definitely save you some games. <table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>March 8, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: I was recently at a PTQ where I went 4-1. With one round left I played, lost, went 4-2 overall and came in ninth. After looking at the standings I felt like if I had drawn with my opponent we both would have made Top 8. Could you explain how this works and how newer players can tell if they should draw their last match to make the Top 8? Thanks,<br> -Kruger <br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hi Kruger,</p>&#10;<p>Before playing your last round, you usually have a few minutes to figure what you need to make Top 8. </p>&#10;<p>In a six-round Swiss round tournament, it’s simple math. The undefeated players will be in whether they play or draw. When you are 4-1 and wondering if you can draw, you have to check how many players can reach 13 points or more. To make sure you’re in with a draw, you have to make sure no more than eight players can reach 13 points. Check the standings and the pairings. Keep also in mind that you’re not supposed to wait for other matches to finish to decide to draw. </p>&#10;<p>When there are more than eight players who can reach 13 points, you will have to rely on your tie-breakers. If your loss came early in the tournament, like round 1 or 2, you will probably have bad tie-breakers and be in a position you could draw yourself out of the Top 8. If you picked up your loss later, you’re likely to make it with a draw. Just compare your tie-breakers with the other potential Top 8ers. Tie-breakers depend on the percentage of wins your opponents have. </p>&#10;<p>In the case you have a doubt, it’s always better to play. You’ll feel better if you lose and don’t make Top 8 than if you draw and still not make it. </p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>
