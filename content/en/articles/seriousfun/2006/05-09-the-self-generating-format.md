As Mark Rosewater instructed me when he recruited me to do this column several years ago, Serious Fun is first and foremost a column about "alternate formats". We do spend quite a bit of emphasis on conventional multiplayer formats – and a couple of those formats have even begun to get detailed rules attention from Wizards. But it's not enough to stick around the formats we all know well. I do feel, from time to time, a frequent pull to suggest something more daring in how you play **Magic**.

Among the more popular topics in reader emails I get is a call for "more new ideas for formats." The reader has thought up a format and wants an opinion on it; or has a group that's bored and wants to try something new, but doesn't feel they know enough about alternate formats to make a good one; or they've tried something and it didn't seem to work.

Most of these readers are unfamiliar with the [Serious Fun archives](https://web.archive.org/web/20080617100320/http://www.wizards.com/default.asp?x=mtgcom/columnarchive&column=SeriousFun), wherein I estimate I've come up with at least 50 distinct **Magic** formats. (Actually, it's probably something more like 100, but I don't want to try to count them. Besides, I know someone on the message boards now will do it for me anyway, because human nature's a real pain… right now, someone's thinking, "There's no way he's done 100 formats. I must prove him wrong." They'll try to put it out of their mind, but they won't be able to resist. So they'll finally go to the archives, read through each article, and count up the formats. Then they'll post that newfound information on the boards, claiming I'm wrong – or maybe even right – thereby falling neatly into my trap. Ssssshhhh! Don't say anything! If they know my plan, they won't do it!). Going back to Casual Fridays columns on sites prior to **magicthegathering.com**, the number gets even higher.

Among my favorites:

*   [The Pink Dinosaur column](/en/articles/seriousfun/2005/01-25-be-a-pink-dinosaur), which was just too darn fun to write. I'm *still* waiting for my online Pink Dinosaur avatar.
*   "[At Your Service](/en/articles/seriousfun/2004/06-08-at-your-service)", a fun homage to Myr Servitor.
*   "[Before You Bust Those Packs](/en/articles/seriousfun/2003/08-05-before-you-bust-those-packs)", one of several times where I encouraged readers to do more with their packs than open them and then complain about a bad rare. I still think casual Limited formats is one of the best ways to increase the value you get out of packs, especially if you buy them in boxed quantities. This column had one immediate follow-up, and then one or two revisits over the years.

*   "[Limiting your Bad Format](/en/articles/seriousfun/2002/07-02-limiting-your-bad-format)", which followed up on a column two weeks previous about reveling in bad cards for decks. As **Magic** players, we are fairly unconventional people. We like being occasionally contrary, and the idea of using bad cards simply because they are bad and someone will get angry at us for not using good cards…well, it's appealing.
*   "[Dictionary Formats](/en/articles/seriousfun/2004/03-16-dictionary-formats)" may be my favorite format-based article, for two reasons. First, it led to a format (the Octant format) which our group still uses occasionally to this day. Second, it got great graphics work (as my columns generally do!) from the good people at Wizards – they even came up with a penguin wizard token just for that day. (No, it's not a real token – but you can print/cut/paste it and pretend it is.) Two weeks later, I did more dictionary formats, and then I got scared with the whole idea because I was learning really freaky words that I knew I'd never use. Do you ever worry about making your brain bleed with an overabundance of arcane, technical information? 'Cause I do, after reading a dictionary for about an hour. Hmm. Maybe I've revealed too much.
*   "[Multiplayer from the Masses](/en/articles/seriousfun/2002/03-26-multiplayer-from-the-masses)", one of the very first Serious Fun columns. I did like doing this, but I am less prone to relay reader ideas nowadays than I used to be. Why? For the same reason deck help became impractical: my readers are lovely individuals who can simply crush me with the collective weight of their emails.
*   "[Gently Rapping at Your Door](/en/articles/seriousfun/2003/10-28-gently-rapping-at-your-door)", which had a revisit the following week due to the high and positive reader response to the idea of flavor-based formats.

There are a few other weeks where I was pretty much exclusively devoted to dreaming up (or relaying faithfully) a variety of formats for you to try out. Not to mention, quite a few articles where I mentioned two or three format ideas in passing, while talking about something else.

All this begs the question: *are you guys insatiable locusts, or what? How many of these formats do I have to come up with before you leave me alone?*

It may help if I take a "teach you how to fish" approach to alternate formats. It's really not that hard to come up with your own formats, and you'd be surprised how well your group may receive them. Why don't we go through a creative process and see if you can't try your own hand at this alternate format thing?

I'm basing some of today's column on a past Serious Fun column on building a new format, called [Elevens](/en/articles/seriousfun/2003/11-11-elevens). This column will take a new approach to format-building. It's not that the old one doesn't work – I just think with the archives out there, readers should take the time to read the old stuff, and then I should take the time to think up new stuff.

To do a good job at making a new format – or doing a great many things in life – you need to develop at least four different assets: creativity, humor, analytics, and team ethic. (If you're a big fan of acronyms – and who isn't? – you can remember the CHAT quartet. This is not just a fun English verb – it is also the French word for "cat". While I was in college, I spent a semester abroad in Paris – this was during their bicentennial in 1989. I did not see any cats, which is fine because I don't really care for cats. What does this have to do with **Magic**? Not much; but I bet you'll remember that CHAT thing a bit more easily now.)

### The First Asset: Creativity

![](http://www.wizards.com/global/images/mtgcom_daily_aa227_picMain_en.jpg)

When you make something, how do you make sure it's original? Quite simply, you don't – you recognize that there's very little new under the sun. You become creative by studying what's out there, finding the gaps, and filling them. Artists who develop new ways of looking at the world, authors who start new genres and subgenres, mathematicians who wonder what it would be like to have a game on collectible cards – these are all people who paid attention to the world around them first, and *then* started building. Younger people often wonder "why we have to learn this stuff" – in any class, from history to mathematics to literature. The burden of standing on the shoulders of giants is that the giants keep getting taller. Each generation builds on the knowledge and experience of those before. (Well, the jury's still out on the baby boomers, but there's still hope for them.)

Creativity, you see, isn't about lying back and waiting for stuff to come into your teeny, tiny little head. It's about sitting up and paying attention.

So sit up and look around. What's bugging you about the **Magic** formats your group plays nowadays? What makes every game just like the last? What's missing?

That's where you start. That's where your big format idea will come from. From there, it only gets easier – and harder.

### The Second Asset: Humor

When I refer to humor, I generally mean the self-deprecating sort. This is the asset that ensures you don't take yourself too seriously. You see the foolishness in coming up with a format that requires ten pages of explanation; so you keep it simple. You enjoy laughing, so you insert some quirks into the format. You don't mind losing a game every once in a while in highly embarrassing fashion, so you make sure the format is fair and balanced so everyone has an equal chance to win.

Humor is what's behind all of that. It's what helps you separate out the things you can control from the things you can't, the things that are admirable about you from the things that are less so, the things that you enjoy about your group from the things that drive you crazy – and do all of this without taking it all so personally.

As you build new formats or anything else, remember that no matter what happens to this new format, it's okay to fail. It's okay to tinker. It's okay to succeed wildly, and then try to mess it up all over again. Nothing is sacred – certainly not in a game like **Magic**.

![](http://www.wizards.com/global/images/mtgcom_daily_aa227_pic1_en.jpg)

### The Third Asset: Analytics

As you try out your new format, you will need humor, yes – but you will also need to be serious about details. You will need to learn what is working and what is not. You will need accuracy in your analysis – is the format not working because the people playing are just too tired and uncooperative, or is it because the format makes it too hard to get anything done?

Here are some sample questions you could ask yourself while tweaking a new format:

*   If I asked everyone in our group, what would they say was the number one cool thing about this format? Put another way, what is the one thing we gotta make sure we don't ditch if we change things around?
*   If I asked everyone in this group, what would they say was the number one drag about this format? What's got to go first?
*   What do I remember about the best game under this new format? What was that experience like? How was it different from typical **Magic** games in our group?
*   Do we have to play this new format all the time? What would be the right frequency for this new format, so no one gets sick of it?
*   Never mind the new format for a moment. What are the two or three best things about our play group? Do those two or three things shine through when we play this format? Why or why not?
*   Again, set aside the actual format. What are the one or two things about our play group are troublesome? (For example, is there a huge difference in play skill, or collection sizes, or use of proxies, or attitude toward new players?) Does the new format alleviate these problems – or make them worse? Are the problems we have the sort of thing you can solve with a new **Magic** format?

By substituting a few words, you may find some of these questions helpful beyond **Magic** as well.

### The Fourth Asset: Team Ethic

It sounds trite at first, but the people you play **Magic** with, work with, study with, hang out with – each of these groups is a team. In fact, if you'll indulge this dog owner's point of view, each of those groups is not just a team – it's a *pack*. Each member of the pack (or team) plays a role, sometimes consciously and sometimes not. The team gets along best when everyone knows how they fit in and feels they have what they need to do a good job.

What does this have to do with creating your **Magic** format? Well, you may be the only person in your group to read this article. (Apparently, your role is reading highly intelligent and informative Internet articles. Congratulations.) And you may be the impetus behind a new format for your group. But you can't do it alone. You need to listen and watch your group as they grapple with a new format. Let each member of the group find their voice and contribute to what the group does.

Notice the questions I asked above, especially the first two. I didn't ask you what *you* thought the best or worst parts were to the new format. I asked you what you believed (or learned) the *group* thought the best or worst parts were.

Team ethic shows up in another way. When you build a good format, your group thrives. They enjoy playing. They come up with better and stronger ideas for decks, because they have more energy for it. They play harder and it should become more difficult for you to win. This means the challenge is higher for you, which should be more satisfying.

It's a great thing about teams – the more time you spend strengthening the people around you, the stronger you get as well. Look for formats that do the following:

*   **Level uneven playing fields**. Differences in income (which leads to collection size), experience (play ability), and other obvious factors shouldn't wreck your group's fun. Yes, you'll always have games where you just build decks and play, but sprinkling in formats that force people in comfortable positions to build decks in new and uncomfortable ways helps *everyone* out. (This was the basic principle behind Limited formats, when compared to Constructed formats.) These usually "stronger" players have to adjust to a new reality and the "weaker" players get a shot at winning for once.
*   **Lend themselves to short explanations**. Everyone in your group should be able to explain what the format does in a few short sentences. If you look back at our group's octant format, it boils down to this: you're in a world with territories. You only attack/affect the few territories close to you. Over time, you can expand to affect more players. Everything else about the format is silly little technical details that we readjust every time we play. It doesn't matter – we all know what we're trying to accomplish.

*   **Allow for team play**. If it's possible to come up with a team variant for the format you're building, I highly recommend it. Don't panic if you can't make this happen – some groups always have seven players, and some formats are ingenious enough that teams are unnecessary. But nine times out of ten, the tensions and problems that readers tell me about in their groups are the sort of thing that team play would help with. (Most frequent problem: "ganging up". See the ganging up series from a couple months back in the archives for more on this.)

Bottom line, if your new format gives newer/younger/poorer players a shot in the arm, is easy to understand, and minimizes the chances of arguments over "unfair" play, it has a greater chance of success.

### The Last Asset: Faith In Yourselves

Once you have a format off the ground, you may be tempted to email me with it and ask for my opinion (or better yet, to post it in an article!). Email away – I'd love to hear from you – but realize I am unlikely to comment extensively on the format, or publish it.

Here's why (it goes beyond my time limitations, though those are certainly a factor.): Many readers send me formats (or decks, or other issues in emails) in a search for validation. "Is this format good," they ask. "Also, will my girlfriend ever want to play **Magic** with me? And by the way, does this deck make me look fat?"

Folks, people like me don't have the answers. Not writers, not **Magic** staff, not Pro Tour players, and certainly not the loudest guy at your local card shop. (Seriously. What is his deal?) 

Who knows if the format will work? *You* do, after you and your group try it out. Who knows what changes you need to make? *You* do, after you all talk it over. Who knows what cards will work best in the format against your friends? *You* do, after you work at it hard enough.

The main reason I wrote today's article, on today's topic, is because it is consistently the #1 topic where I see the fragile egos of my readers hanging in the balance. My heart goes out to you. You want validation. But this isn't a parking ramp – this is a creative effort for fun. Have faith in your abilities, and in the company you keep. You'll do great, and you'll enjoy yourselves on the way.
