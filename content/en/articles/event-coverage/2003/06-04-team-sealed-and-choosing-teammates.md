Team play is the perfect hybrid between competitive play and casual play. It combines the tension of competition with the relaxation of playing **Magic** with your friends. You’re not competing with everyone in the room as two people are on your side. Wins are twice as sweet while losses are about half the pain.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptbos02/808.jpg)

That is what makes team play my favorite format. Of course I’ve been on the same team for a long time although our name change from www.star-maker.nl/lap to www.lowlandgames.com might not make that all too transparent. Together with Frank Karsten and Jelger Wiegersma, I’ve played about a tenth of my total amount of tournaments and, as a result, we’re pretty close as a team. When we open our sealed decks, we know what needs to be done and each one of us sorts the cards and lays them out as needed. Communication goes smoothly and we know how to resolve issues if they come up. Those kinds of things are what you want to have in a team. Make sure you know your teammates well – and preferably consider them friends. At the very least, make sure that you are willing to spend a couple of days with your teammates without getting the urge to murder one of them. Friendship is sometimes difficult to find in teams. 

We go pretty far in dividing roles between each other. Frank typically provides us with pen and paper to keep track of life totals, while Jelger and I take turns supplying sleeves, drinks and mascots for the team. While this is of course somewhat nonsensical, those rituals do give us a feeling of being a real team. Rituals can be good – have someone on your team be the one to get drinks.

Of course, this isn’t what team play is all about. Playing **Magic** in teams isn’t about who gets the drinks, isn’t about who supplies the paper, it’s about playing **Magic**. So how do you go about selecting your teammates? Usually you’ll also want your two teammates to be at about the same skill level as you. If you’re teaming with someone way better than you, he is going to see a lot of mistakes you make and give you comments on them. While this is good for a learning experience, it is frustrating both for him and for you. You get corrected a lot and he has to put up with a teammate making mistakes he wouldn’t make. While it always happens that teammates make mistakes, and sometimes those mistakes cost matches, the frequency of mistakes should be about equal over each of the teammates. A good rule of thumb could be that every teammate should have around the same Limited rating. A team with members of 1780/1820/1840 would be a good team while 1920/1740/1780 would be a bad team.

After you find people you like who are of about the same skill level, you should try and see who lives close to you. Practice as a team is crucial and if you live 200 kilometers (approximately 125 miles) apart, it’s rather hard to get together. If you can get your team together often, there are plenty of opportunities to practice team sealed builds and practice your draft signals. Also by generally seeing your teammates a lot, you should be able to know what their preferences are with regards to deck types in Limited. At last year’s Pro Tour, it was clear that I knew the matchup in the middle seat better than Jelger or Frank and as a result I was put in the B seat.

This season however, we don’t really know who is best at which matchup. As this influences seating for the draft part of the tournament, it is important to think about before you enter the tournament. However, since Team Draft is only a small part of the Qualifiers and Grand Prix, the rest of this article won’t be about seating or Team Draft, but rather Team Sealed.

There are a lot of pitfalls when building a Team Sealed deck. First of all, you can build one deck that is too strong, making the other two too weak. This happens more often if there is one good player on the team who wants all the goodies for himself. While an imbalance in power among the three decks is almost always unavoidable, the total amount of power in the final three decks should be maximized. More often than not, this means that each of the three decks is about equally powerful. 

The next pitfall is having people play with cards they don’t like. An example of this was when we played in the Venice Gateway and we told Jelger to play our w-r deck that contained Insurrection. Jelger hates that card with a passion and we lost because he didn’t play his game so that he would just win with Insurrection. Now, it’s easy to put the blame on Jelger for not doing that, but Frank and I were equally guilty of it. We told him to play Insurrection in a deck he didn’t like, and we told him to play that deck. Consequently, Jelger plays decks that match his style of play and has won us more games than lost. 

Finally, if it is possible, you should give the best deck to the weakest player and the worst deck to the best player. That way, you give the better player more opportunity to turn your bad cards into good ones. Very often, it is possible to win with a more mediocre deck by playing differently or by utilizing creatures and spells in a non-conventional way. Of course this could be the topic of an entirely different article. Regardless, by giving the worst player the best deck, and the best player the worst deck, you even out the chances of winning more and overall you will win more team matches than if you give the best player the better deck. In that case, he will probably not drop a single match, but the team result depends on the weaker players with weaker decks. Chances are higher that you’ll lose the entire match that way.

By distributing the decks according to their strength, an extra decision is added to the deck construction part of Team Sealed. You’ll have to estimate which deck is strongest, and who is best with which deck. What distribution for the three decks is going to give your team the best odds of winning a team match? The ideal would be if you manage to build three decks and one of each suits one of the three team members very well. If this doesn’t happen, then you should distribute the decks in such a way that the team has the highest chance of winning the team match each round. If two members are equally skilled in playing the cleric deck but one of the two agrees he is better at the beast deck than the other, that player should be playing the beast deck and the other the cleric deck.

However, all this theory is moot if you misconstruct the sealed deck. At the last GP Trial we played, we had reasonably good decks. We only managed to go a mediocre 3-2 with them, losing to Tom van de Logt’s team with insane cards, and to Maarten Ferguson’s team, but I have no clue why we lost to them. I do think we somewhat misconstructed the decks we were handed and we should have been able to beat both Tom’s team and Maarten’s team had we built them right. Unfortunately, I lost the decks on the way back or I would have discussed the way we built them.

In short however, we built a u-g deck from our pool that had three win conditions: Day of the Dragons, Pemmin’s Aura and Centaur Glade. It was built to survive for a while and then win on one of the three. The major flaw in this plan was that we disregarded fear and flying. We underestimated the amount of evasion that the deck would face, and together with that, its inability to deal with cards in general was a problem. It lost to Decree of Pain, Call to the Grave and almost lost to Silklash Spider. The other decks we built were u-w soldiers and b-r zombie goblins. The u-w deck turned out to be too strong and should have been w-r most likely. That way the u-g deck would turn out to be much stronger itself, while green could have been split up so that we could make a b-g deck with an insane curve.

One of the best types of practice you can get for Team Sealed is by opening one or two decks with a couple of teams and then each taking turns constructing them and writing down what you make of it. Discuss what you built and why you made those choices with the other teams and you should learn a whole lot. Of course, nothing beats playing in a tournament where you can earn some rating while experiencing the full implications of the deck construction choices you made as a team. 

Concluding, I would like to stress one last time how important it is that you don’t punish teammates for making mistakes. The first true frown and degrading comment is the beginning of the end for your team. Especially on the day of the tournament, a crucial mistake will haunt your teammate already, and reminding him or her of it won’t do his concentration much good. Keep in mind to have fun while winning the PTQ alright? You’re there to win that Pro Tour slot together!
