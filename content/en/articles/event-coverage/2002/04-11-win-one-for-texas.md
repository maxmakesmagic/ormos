Once upon a time I wrote rather a well received article called Building Broken Decks volume IV: Lessons from Texas for The Dojo, then edited by Chris Senhouse. While there is no longer any Dojo to visit, you can re-read that gem, thanks to our friends at archive.org [here](https://web.archive.org/web/20020411000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://web.archive.org/web/*/http://www.thedojo.com&origin=sideboard_strategy_sb20020411a). 

You don't really have to do so to benefit from this article; the main points of Lessons from Texas are the exploration of two rules:

### The Rules:

### The Bill Macey Rule

![](https://i.ormos.uk/95/3a/953aaa26f6d1188de23ebcef4844b80fe703f98308387bc929f179d337890751.jpg)

In a new or open format, the most vulnerable point of the metagame is in the newest set. To succeed in a new format, you should play the most powerful cards in the new set, for which opponents, playing established decks, may not be prepared.

### <table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/0b/53/0b53bd077673dc60c268bddc8514049bf4f8d3b483da29f24896e172690a9fa8.jpg"><br><i>Try to stay one step ahead of the competition.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

The Lan D. Ho Rule

Predict the intuitive decks. Figure out the next-level deck that beats the intuitive decks. As you advance throughout the tournament, there will be fewer intuitive decks and more next-level decks (like yours). To succeed, you must therefore build the best version of the next-level deck, capable of beating other next-level decks, including itself.

Bill's 1996 White Weenie deck attacked the metagame head-on. With monoblack Necropotence the defining deck, Macey brought eight maindeck Protection from Black creatures to hammer the opposing life total. Where the Necropotence deck had Contagion and Nevinyrral's Disk for board control, Macey answered with Disenchant and Aeolipile. Not only would he be able to remove his opponent's creatures, they would not be able to do the same to him. The secondary deck of summer 1996 was g-w Armageddon, a deck very much like Bill's, playing for a midgame Armageddon from superior board position. Where g-w had a 4-mana Erhnam Djinn or 5-mana Deadly Insect for it's pre-Armageddon threat spell, Bill answered with superior tempo: the 1-mana Swords to Plowshares was many times as good in his deck, given its lower mana curve, and his significant threats started at 1 and 2 mana... and could go toe-to-toe with even the 4- and 5- mana g-w spells.

But every White Weenie deck could make those claims against those two opponents. What set the Macey deck apart were the three maindeck Kjeldoran Outposts. As good as his deck was against Necropotence, busting through a tournament like the U.S. Open meant having to deal with u-w control as well. While u-w control was in those days notoriously bad against "The Skull" in the hands of non-Jon Finkel magicians, u-w could still play spoiler to midgame creature decks like White Weenie and g-w... unless they presented an unstoppable threat like Kjeldoran Outpost. In a format where u-w had no Political Trickery to answer the Outpost, Macey shored up his last matchup and ran over the slower Millstone clocks of those decks with a permission-proof army of 1/1 attackers.

He may not have "broken" the format (he lacked turn-1 Hypnotic Specter and Necropotence plays and had 0 copies of Hymn to Tourach after all), but Bill may very well have "solved" it.

Contrast The Bill Macey Rule with The Lan D. Ho Rule. While both former AustiKnights approach an uncertain metagame, Bill's assumption of superior information does not mesh 100% with Lan's rule developed three years later, in an age of much more widespread internet information. Both players attacked the metagame with proactive decks, both go for hard-to-answer threats, but Lan goes a step further... once you start hitting the low-numbered tables, you have to start contending with players with their own - often parallel - innovations.

Lan prepared primarily for Sligh, Recur, Hatred, and the other well-known decks. Crumbling Sanctuary, Phyrexian artifacts, and Mishra's Helix played sliver bullet roles across the board, and Masticore beat most creature decks by itself... but like most players of this style, Lan knew if he resolved Tinker he would win regardless of the matchup. Unlike most Tinker players, though, he had 11 permission-proof lands that made sure his undercosted search spell would resolve against Draw-Go and its brethren, and with mirror preparation, he had Hurkyl's Recall in his sideboard, waiting for the unsuspecting Matt Vienneau.

Lan wasn't planning to lose to another monoblue deck. (Ironically enough, he did, in the last round, playing for Top 8, but bad draws happen to everyone, and The Iron Giant nevertheless had the best overall win percentage of any deck at 1999 Pro Tour-Chicago).

All of that being said, with Regionals 2002 approaching, we have yet another open field ahead of us. How will we resolve a Standard defined by San Diego's Psychatog and g-r decks, but complicated by *Torment's* heavy contributions to black board control and the addition of the powerful - and blatantly undercosted - madness mechanic?

We have the bulk of the intuitive decks already; they were recently listed in [The Decks to Beat: Developing Your Playtest Gauntlet for Regionals](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/03-25-the-decks-to-beat-developing-your-playtest-gauntlet-for-regionals). Chad Ellis has even begun for you next-level thinking, having outlined the specific weaknesses that decks in this environment are already showing. Toby Wachter, over and over, has reminded us that 200+ card decks just might be viable too.

The tools are all here on the *Sideboard*... but how will you rush head-on into Regionals 2002?

While Seth Burn, Scott Johns, and of course Zvi Mowshowitz tuned the most efficient Fires deck, Brian's was just a half-step past even the best version of that intuitive archetype... for just one weekend. But it was enough.

You want the Bill Macey Rule in action? Brian's deck made better use of *Invasion* cards than almost anyone else's at that PT-Chicago. He had Blastoderm redundancy in the form of Jade Leech, and he figured out that in a format where Ancient Hydra, Scorching Lava, and Urza's Rage were the best removal spells... he would answer with an enormous evasion creature.

You want the Lan D. Ho Rule? Just as Lan (who incidentally co-developed Brian's sideboard) would have predicted, the best versions of decks like The Red Zone - Chevy Fires specifically - had the exact same innovations (Jade Leeches and Dragons) that Kibler's deck boasted. What they didn't have were maindeck Wax/Wane, which not only made Kibler's already enormous animals win every fight, but also broke up the Fires deck's much-feared "The Fix", answering Saproling Burst with just one mana. Going to Lan's sideboard, the Red Zone got even better in the pseudo-mirror match. Unlike the Tangles of most of his opponent, in Brian's deck, Simoon played one-way Wrath of God against Birds, Elves, and opposing Hydras; Armadillo Cloak, on the other hand, was a one-card combo that almost guaranteed victory.

While both well-built Fires decks and The Red Zone had the same capability to topdeck significant threats post-Wrath of God against control decks, Kibler's incredible sideboard and lack of maindeck enchantments went a long way against Rebels. You may not know the playtesting frustration of drawing The Fix over and over in a pre-Flametongue Kavu Fires deck, while Scott McCord would again and again answer with Disenchant and then go to town with Lin-Sivvi... but then again, at Chicago, Brian would never have to worry about that combination broken by opposing Rebels. If only Kibler knew how to declare creature type properly when casting Tsabo's Decree, or if his Sunday opponent had been anyone but Kai Budde, we may have very well had a different Chicago champion.

So what has this trip down memory lane taught us, with Regionals so very close?

1\. The new set is very important - use it to find hard-to-answer threats.  
 2. Other people will figure out the same stuff you do; don't be surprised to see the mirror.  
 3. In an open field, proactive, threat-based decks are most likely to advance... your opponent has to answer your threats and he might not have the right answer at the right time, or... your opponent's deck might stumble, so finish him before he can get his plan in motion.  
 4. Attacking with giant monsters is fun.

Have fun this weekend all of you!
