What made Solar Flare so successful? It's not just one reason; it's a combination of factors. It runs Wrath of God, the best card in the format by a mile, and decks that play the best cards are usually good. Furthermore, it includes a lot of card advantage, from Compulsive Research to more subtle card advantage vessels such as Azorius Chancery, which makes sure you never run out of gas and soothes the impact of mulligans. Third, the deck gets the best from three colors, while keeping a consistent mana base, thanks to the *Ravnica* Block duals and Signets. Solar Flare also has multiple game plans, from fast starts via turn-four Zombify or Persecute to a solid late game, so you never know what to expect out of the deck. That makes it hard to prepare *against* the deck. Last but not least, Solar Flare doesn't lean on countermagic and therefore doesn't need to keep mana open during the opponent's turn. This reduces the need for instant-speed card drawing and allows Solar Flare to accommodate the best sorcery speed card drawer in the format: Compulsive Research. These reasons for what makes a successful deck might be good to keep in mind, as they might help you in your future deckbuilding endeavors.

I doubt we have seen the last of Solar Flare. I expect it will remain a top tier deck in the new *Time Spiral* Standard, with some adjustments of course.

The major drifts are that:

*   Aggro Loam has fallen out of grace.
*   Mono Green Aggro has also become less popular, because it was a metagame choice against Aggro Loam, and without many Aggro Loam decks in the format Mono Green Aggro loses much of its edge.
*   Blue-White Tron has solidified its place as one of the best decks in the format.
*   Consequently, a lot of players switched to decks that have a good matchup against Blue-White Tron, picking up Boros Deck Wins and Flow Rock for example.

That last point is an interesting one, and I hadn't introduced Boros Deck Wins or Flow Rock in one of my columns yet. You can surely guess what's coming now…

> “I had used Destructive Flow 2 years ago as a sideboard card for my Rock deck. That was when everybody was playing Tooth and Nail or Affinity. The Tooth matchup was bad, so I sideboarded Flows. Since then the format changed, and Destructive Flow became an incredibly good maindeck card. There are just so many matchups where an early Flow is an autowin. The resurgence of Urzatron decks lately makes the card even better; all they have is Repeal usually, otherwise it's game over. I break the symmetry of Destructive Flow by only having basic lands in play on my side of the board. I use saclands to fix my mana, while not giving up my ability to only have basics in play. The saclands also have good synergy with Sensei's Divining Top. Flow Rock gets the Online Tech stamp of approval. The Extended format is going to change a bit with the introduction of *Time Spiral*, but likely advantageously for Flow Rock. People might shy away from playing Psychatog because of Sudden Shock, and Tormod's Crypt might scare away Aggro Loam decks, thereby removing Flow Rock's bad matchups.

### [*Invasion* Block Sealed Deck Tournament VII](/en/articles/feature/2006/10-13-invasiontm-block-ipa-sealed-deck-tournament-vii)

### Online Tech discovers Online Classic

The card pool of online Classic format is unlimited. It uses all of the available online cards; nothing is banned or restricted. Classic enthusiasts praise the large card pool which enables degenerate combos, and it is of course fun to be able to play with every card you own. Online Classic used to be somewhat useless, because it was just Extended without the bannings. However, the format got a big boost last year when *Mirage* and *Visions* were released online. This gave Classic two full sets of powerful extra cards to work with, and now Classic has its own flavor distinct from Extended. It's a nice format, but unfortunately there are no Classic Premier Events, and I have never seen the Classic 8-man Constructed queue fire.

“Dangerlinto,” captain of the [Classic Quarter](https://web.archive.org/web/20061101000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.classicquarter.com/&origin=mtgcom_daily_fk12) filled this void by organizing a biweekly Eternal Struggle Classic Player Run Event. For those of you unfamiliar with Player Run Events, you can find announcements in the [PRE forum](https://web.archive.org/web/20061101000000/http://boards1.wizards.com/forumdisplay.php?f=590). If you want to participate, join the appropriate online chat window – in this case, by typing “/join Classic” – and type “register” there. Pairings are made manually and are announced in that chat window. You set up a match in “Casual Play – Anything Goes,” which your assigned opponent joins, and report the match result afterwards. The tournament setup is almost identical to regular Premier Event tourneys, with Swiss rounds and a Top 8 playoff, but the difference is that the tournament organization of Player Run Events is done by hand, not by the program. It takes a bit longer, and you can't win huge prizes, but there is a relaxed casual fun atmosphere and a wide community that supports these events.

![](http://www.wizards.com/global/images/mtgcom_daily_fk12_picMain_en.jpg)I expected the Classic format would be filled with extremely powerful combo decks, since the insanely combolicious *Mirage* Block cards are legal, including Dark Ritual, Lion's Eye Diamond, and Vampiric Tutor. Here's a snapshot of possible Classic-legal degenerate combos:

*   Get a Worldgorger Dragon in play via Entomb or Careful Study, then reanimate it with Necromancy. The Dragon's come-in-play effect triggers, removing Necromancy. This triggers the leave-play effect of Necromancy, destroying the Dragon. This, in turn, triggers the Dragon's leave-play effect, getting back the Necromancy, which puts the Dragon back in play. And then you repeat the cycle from the start. By tapping your lands in between removing your permanents from the game, you can produce infinite mana and eventually kill with Ambassador Laquatus or Demonfire.
*   Abusing the activated ability of Auriok Salvagers to repeatedly recur Lion's Eye Diamond from the graveyard to create infinite amounts of mana of any color. With infinite mana, you can use recur Chromatic Sphere to draw cards until you find Pyrite Spellbomb, which kills the opponent after 10 Auriok Salvager cycles.
*   Helm of Awakening combined with two Sensei's Divining Top equals infinite spells. If you have Top #1 on top of your deck and Top #2 in play, you tap Top #2 to draw Top #1, then you play Top #1 (for free, thanks to Helm of Awakening), use it to draw Top #2, and so on. You don't actually achieve anything with this except making a huge storm count, so naturally you kill with Brain Freeze.
*   Goblin Charbelcher in combination with just one land in the deck. Instead of using lands, you make mana with cards like Chrome Mox, Lion's Eye Diamond, Dark Ritual, Tinder Wall, etc. and use Vampiric Tutor to fetch Goblin Charbelcher, which then kills your opponent in one easy activation.
*   The last combo deck is quite degenerate, because it can kill on turn one by going something like Dark Ritual, Dark Ritual, Dark Ritual, Chromatic Sphere, Lion's Eye Diamond. Then you play Burning Wish and respond by sacrificing Lion's Eye Diamond. You fetch Mind's Desire, and you have enough mana in your pool to cast it. You will hopefully flip into cards that can keep the engine going and you eventually kill with Tendrils of Agony.

That is just me theorizing which combos are viable. But what decks actually rise to the top of real Classic events? Last weekend, we had a special [PRE Classic tournament](https://web.archive.org/web/20061101000000/http://boards1.wizards.com/showthread.php?t=725104). It was part of UnCon, which is an annual fall blowout of games, contests, seminars, and special chat events hosted by Wizards' online community moderators. It aims to recreate the same atmosphere as a real GenCon convention in cyberspace. Since Wizards sponsored this tournament with *Time Spiral* booster prizes, the turnout was huge. 112 players showed up, so the results should be representative. Ten rounds later, we had these players at the top of the final Standings: 

> 1\. Aylon, Scepter-Chant  
>  2. Ashar, Vial Affinity  
>  3. ArdeMetropolis, Vial Affinity  
>  4. LangeBrandon, Mono Green Aggro

In the Top 8 we also had Flawed Paradigm with Big Red and Hollow0n3 with Cheap Affinity, amongst others. I was actually quite surprised to learn that no degenerate combo decks had risen to the top. Instead, simple Extended decks with updates took the top places. The results of the [Eternal Struggle 1.6](https://web.archive.org/web/20061101000000/http://boards1.wizards.com/showthread.php?t=706084) and [Eternal Struggle 1.7](https://web.archive.org/web/20061101000000/http://boards1.wizards.com/showthread.php?t=714491) Classic tournaments that took place in the last month confirmed the conclusion that online Classic is not dominated by ridiculous turn-one-kill decks at all. Actually, “budget” decks such as Vial Affinity perform well, because they consistently won faster with Disciple of the Vault and Skullclamp before a combo deck could go off.
