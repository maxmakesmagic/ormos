Let's say you've just cracked open your first *Morningtide* booster. Or your fifty-first. Doesn't matter. The important thing is, you open that *Morningtide* booster, inhale the smell of fresh cardstock, and thumb through the goods.

Some people, of course, would flip right to the rare. But not you. (Well, okay, maybe you peeked, but bear with us.) There are fifteen cards in that pack, and you want to look at each of them in turn. Past the delightfully goofy expression on Game-Trail Changeling's big, trampling moose-face, past that awesome flavor text on Rivals' Duel, past the raw efficiency and card advantage of Latchkey Faerie, you see...

Brighthearth Banneret.

You scan it quickly, mentally file it away for that Warrior deck you're thinking about building, start to pass it by... and then you pause.

You're at least broadly familiar with the Bannerets by now. Each one is a two-cost creature with a race and a class that makes your spells that match its race or class ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif) cheaper to play. Simple right? Race, class, maybe slap on a keyword, and you're done!

...But then, there are Elemental Shamans as well as Elemental Warriors, so really this guy could be the "Shaman" Banneret instead, couldn't it? Then the "Treefolk" Banneret would have to be Warriors... but wait, would the "Warrior" Banneret have to be a Treefolk? Why not a Banneret for Elf Warriors? Of course, that would leave Treefolk out in the cold, and Treefolk in Lorwyn are deciduous....

And as long as you're playing armchair designer (and who doesn't, now and then?), why does this one have reinforce? Not that you're complaining—the ability to cash this little fellow in as a combat trick when you draw it late is not to be scoffed at—but couldn't the red Banneret just as easily have haste? Or first strike?

Uh-oh, now you've gone and started *thinking* about it. If Brighthearth Banneret had haste, what would Frogtosser Banneret have? Fear? Or is that too predictable? Maybe it doesn't need a keyword at all. Maybe it could be bigger, or cost less, or....

*Wow*, you think. *There's a lot going on with this one little common.*

Oh, and...

*What does "Banneret" mean, anyway?* *What's a Brighthearth? What's with the flag?*

Every card has a story, and that means five cards have five stories (at least!). In the case of a cycle like *Morningtide*'s Bannerets, those stories are tightly intertwined, weaving in and out of one another's narratives (and Multiverse records), subtly pushing one another this way and that. Some of the same factors affect all of them—but unique concerns influence each of them as well.

Maybe now you're intrigued.

But not so intrigued you forget to check out your rare:

### Look Familiar?

As we said, every card has a story... and every story begins somewhere. The cycle that would become the Bannerets first appeared in the *Morningtide* (then "Butter") design file looking like this:

> CW01\_BTT  
>  Pearl Familiar  
>  1W  
>  Creature - Kithkin Soldier  
>  2/1  
>  First strike  
>  Spells that share a creature type with CARDNAME cost you 1 less to play.
> 
> CU01\_BTT  
>  Sapphire Familiar  
>  1U  
>  Creature - Merfolk Wizard  
>  1/2  
>  Shroud  
>  Spells that share a creature type with CARDNAME cost you 1 less to play.
> 
> CB01\_BTT  
>  Jet Familiar  
>  1B  
>  Creature - Goblin Rogue  
>  1/1  
> Fear  
>  Spells that share a creature type with CARDNAME cost you 1 less to play.
> 
> CR01\_BTT  
>  Ruby Familiar  
>  1R  
>  Creature - Elemental Warrior  
>  1/1  
>  Haste  
>  Spells that share a creature type with CARDNAME cost you 1 less to play.
> 
> CG01\_BTT  
>  Emerald Familiar  
>  1G  
>  Creature - Elf Shaman  
>  2/2  
>  Spells that share a creature type with CARDNAME cost you 1 less to play.

As you can see, while the idea was there, the specifics were almost all different—different stats, different keyword abilities, and in one case a different race. The biggest difference, you'll notice, is that these original race/class "familiars" didn't specify the race and class they made cheaper, instead using the "share a creature type" technology that still appears on the set's kinship cards (among others). The change from "share a creature type" to listing the specific race and class was an early one, and it never changed back. The change was discussed beforehand in Multiverse (the Wizards card information database) by *Morningtide* lead designer Paul Sottosanti:

> ps 10/5: this could also just say "kithkin and soldier spells you play cost 1 less." we think that would be better for timmy, and as written it's better for johnny. we're not entirely sure which we like better.  
>  ps 10/23: switched to that.

Paul explains the decision:

With that decided, there was still a lot left to do. The power, toughness, keywords and other abilities, mana costs, colors, and even races of the five "familiars" were up for grabs. Designer Ken Nagle discusses the constraints at work in designing and developing the cycle:

<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;                 <b>Ken Nagle</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<p><img alt="Ken Nagle" src="https://i.ormos.uk/73/dc/73dc2c209bef80978eea09781f3075d348fa6472480299108af0945ff889dce7.jpg" title="Ken Nagle">From design, we adhered to these rules from <i>Lorwyn</i>:</p>&#10;<p>Kithkin are white and green. <br> Elves are green and black. <br> Goblins are red and black. <br> Treefolk are green, black, and white. <br> Faeries are blue and black. <br> Merfolk are blue and white. <br> Giants are red and white. <br> Elementals are all colors. </p>&#10;<p>Here are the rest of the rules the <i>Morningtide</i> design team followed:</p>&#10;<p>Rogues are mostly blue and black. <br> Warriors are not blue. <br> Shamans are neither white nor blue. <br> Giants already have <a>Stinkdrinker Daredevil</a>. <br> Head Developer Devin Low loves Treefolk. <br> Try to dodge <i>Planeshift</i>'s Familiar cycle—<a>Sunscape Familiar</a>, <a>Stormscape Familiar</a>, <a>Nightscape Familiar</a>, <a>Thunderscape Familiar</a>, <a>Thornscape Familiar</a>.</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Given those constraints and the assumption that there would be five members of the cycle—one in each of the five colors, each of which would reduce the cost of one of the five main *Morningtide* classes—there are a *lot* of potential combinations. Ken refers to the process of assigning races, stats, and keywords to each of the Bannerets as a "logic puzzle," going so far as to write JavaScript code that simulates the process:

*Click each box once for an "O", indicating that your Banneret for the race in that column will have that row's mana cost / power / toughness / ability. Click twice for an "X" to remind yourself which combinations you have eliminated based on the above rules or your other choices. Click a third time to clear. Enter whatever non-cost-reduction abilities you like in the slots provided, and click "Finished" to see what your Banneret design file looks like.*

This is something you often see around **Magic** R&D—and, we'd wager, just about any R&D department: the obviously crazy idea. The design stories on this very web site are full of examples of ideas that seem doomed to fail in retrospect—from [serious consideration of a sixth color](/en/articles/feature/2007/01-30-the-color-purple) to [exploring for treasure](/en/articles/feature/2007/10-08-working-for-peanuts) to Soldiers, Knights, and Warriors being lumped together as "[fighters](/en/articles/feature/2008/02-04-my-first-ever-design-article)."

Then there are the other crazy ideas, like, say... [noncreatures with creature subtypes](/en/articles/feature/2007/10-12-surprising-subtypes). Or putting [a race and a class](/en/articles/makingmagic/2008/01-21-the-amazing-race-class) on the type line of every card that represents a sentient creature. Or [playing creatures face down](/en/articles/uncategorised/2002/09-06-morph-onslaught-s-new-ability). Or reworking the entire rules system from the ground up so the game is easier to learn and easier to remember (yes, we're talking about the oft-cited *Sixth Edition* rules overhaul—various writers on this site have mentioned this before, but we can't stress enough how contentious it was at the time and how necessary it was for everything that came after).

Those ideas were crazy, too. But they were that other kind of crazy—the awesome kind of crazy (or, at the very least, the tough-but-necessary kind of crazy, like an action movie hero). When one set of ideas is printed on glossy cardstock and the other set is relegated to design articles like this one, it's very easy to think of them as fundamentally different. But when it's all just hastily scrawled playtest cards and half-formed theories, there's one best way to tell the difference between good ideas and bad ideas: test, test, test!

Ken's last comment hints at a discussion from the early days of Banneret design—should they reduce "double matches" by ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif) or by ![2 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif)? Although the double reduction is fun, one of the goals of *Morningtide* was to enable both race decks and class decks—not focused race/class decks. A double reduction says "play me with Treefolk Shamans," which wasn't right for the set.

Note also Ken's prophetic first comment about a two-drop 1/3 Treefolk . Once the card hit those stats, it didn't change again. Some of the other members of the cycle had a longer path. Note that once "familiar" was rejected as a final name (more on this later), "Herald" was chosen as a placeholder.

That last comment from Alexis dates from the time when the "Herald" had a "backstab" cost of ![0 Mana](http://wizards.com/images/Symbols/Symbol_0_mana.gif). It was soon given "backstab" ![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif) and a triggered ability that caused each opponent to lose 1 life.

At that point, this fun, simple common had become surprisingly busy and mathematically demanding:

> CB01\_MOR  
>  Stinkdrinker Herald  
>  1B  
>  Creature - Goblin Rogue  
>  1/1  
>  Goblin spells and Rogue spells you play cost 1 less to play.  
>  When CARDNAME comes into play, if its smuggle cost was paid, each opponent loses 1 life.  
>  Smuggle B (If a creature you control that shares a creature type with this dealt combat damage to aplayer this turn, you may play this for its smuggle cost.

(Prowl, as you might have noticed, had many names over the course of design and development—RogueSting, backstab, smuggle, and trespass, and possibly more. By contrast, reinforce and kinship each went directly from initial placeholder (buff and synergy, respectively) to final name in one step. Some mechanics are tougher than others.)

The development team searched for a simpler alternative ability for the card. Fortunately, the color pie for keywords had [recently been rethought](/en/articles/makingmagic/2007/06-18-keyword-play), and that put a new option on the table: haste, now secondarily in black. The dream of throwing down this little Rogue, attacking for 1, and then prowling out another Rogue at an even further reduced price was cool enough for haste to stick.

Meanwhile, Stonybrook Banneret had been given a "Spiketail Hatchling"-type ability that was quickly dismissed as not fun. It switched back to shroud for some time, but there was a feeling that there was enough shroud in *Lorwyn* block already. Islandwalk supported an already-established Merfolk theme, making the decision pretty easy.

Ballyrush Banneret had the simplest time of all—it became a clean, efficient two-mana 2/1 early in development and stayed there.

Then there was Brighthearth Banneret. The design version had haste. The next version had first strike, another red ability, but there was a problem—remember the rule Ken listed above about steering clear of the *Planeshift* Familiars?

### The New Hotness

As the cards' abilities fluctuated, R&D members shuffled up decks of playtest cards for Sealed Deck, Draft, and Constructed to help tune them. Now, you're probably familiar with our playtest cards from other design and development articles. For a very long time, they've looked like this one from *Lorwyn*:

![Lorwyn Playtest Smokebraider](https://i.ormos.uk/48/87/4887b0c1a806fc6871dc10d336dc9c53e5cdbfd6ab073886ed270719bbe6cfbc.jpg "Lorwyn Playtest Smokebraider")

*Morntingtide* playtesters, however, were working with something different—something that looked more like this:

![Morningtide Playtest Bannerets](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/c1/e1c181a449857c5d8996bc89c1b99addcc59eef8a4d3f7c06977051ec5426ad5.jpg "Morningtide Playtest Bannerets")

The new playtest cards—now the R&D standard—were the brainchild (brainchildren?) of then–design intern Alexis Janson. Alexis realized that there was no reason playtest cards couldn't look more like real cards. Drawing on her background in computer science, she found a way to display visual information right on the stickers. This provided a material as well as aesthetic advantage, allowing playtesters to call out issues like "Wow, that's a lot of text" or "The type line says Merfolk, but the art shows a Kithkin" very early in the development process. It also made playtesting easier for those who aren't used to looking at playtest cards—a big help, because people from all over the building occasionally try their hand at Sealed Deck or Draft for sets in development, and not all of them can quickly find the information they need on the old playtest cards. Once developers had done some playtests with Alexis's new stickers, nobody wanted to go back. That said, we still use the old playtest stickers earlier in design when the set's templates, creature types, etc. are still in flux—and when there's no art to display anyway. When we show playtest cards on the site in the future, don't be surprised to see a mix of both kinds.

### Art Attack

Looking at those Banneret playtest cards above, you might notice that they have art. Concurrent with development, the creative team had written art concepts for the cards in the set, and art director Jeremy Jarvis had assigned them to illustrators.

Where possible, it's quite common for members of a cycle to be assigned to a single illustrator in order to capture a unified look and feel. In this case, **Magic** art director Jeremy Jarvis assigned illustrator Ralph Horsley to bring the Bannerets to life. The art descriptions for the cycle were pretty spare, so it would be up to Horsley to concoct the details that would make the illustrations really shine.

### There's No Text Like Creative Text

The cycle's abilities were nearing their final forms. Their art had been finalized. The only thing left was their names and flavor text. Creative writer Doug Beyer sheds light on the process:

<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;                 <b>Doug Beyer</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<p><img alt="Doug Beyer" src="https://i.ormos.uk/36/ec/36ec6013327716582eaca4dccd0e02a5ff23310016e218e4b883d659190f9844.jpg" title="Doug Beyer"><b>Bannerets – Naming</b></p>&#10;<p>It seemed clear that the bannerets had to have a cycled-up naming convention, so you could refer to all of them with one term (like people have been doing throughout this whole article). Some people in R&amp;D were calling them &quot;familiars&quot; because of their mechanical similarities to the <i>Planeshift</i> familiars, but as <i>Lorwyn</i> and <i>Morningtide</i> were strongly tribal sets, the cards were concepted as a cycle of tribal-humanoids instead of random Insects and Kavu. That meant the actual word &quot;familiar&quot; would be too weird here. The placeholder name for them was the &quot;heralds,&quot; but I didn't like how close that sounded to <i>Lorwyn</i>'s eight-card cycle of harbingers. &quot;Herald&quot; is basically synonymous with &quot;harbinger,&quot; and so is basically that whole page of the thesaurus—envoy, courier, forerunner, etc. I wanted to go a new direction, and I wanted to key off the military feel of that banner that was in each card's art description.</p>&#10;<p>In my research I happened upon &quot;banneret.&quot; In English history, a banneret (or &quot;knight banneret,&quot; BAN-er-et or ban-er-ET) is a knight who's been honored for his valor so that he's entitled to lead troops to war under his own banner, rather than under the standard of the local lord. It has the flavor of &quot;leading a bunch of troops into battle,&quot; which I liked with the tribal cost-reduction mechanic. It's also literally the name of a guy who carries a banner—a perfect example of a obscure-but-real term that <b>Magic</b> uses in a new way. </p>&#10;<p>The <i>Morningtide</i> bannerets aren't knights, but they do fill a special role that allows them to carry heraldric banners significant to their respective groups. <a>Bosk Banneret</a> hails from the <a>Murmuring Bosk</a>, a mystical forest in Lorwyn, and supports the cause of both Treefolk and Shamans. <a>Stonybrook Banneret</a> is from the magically adept merrow school known as Stonybrook, and carries a scale-covered banner infused with magic relevant to both Merfolk and Wizards. Ballyrush is a kithkin clachan where soldiers are trained. The Brighthearth is a flamekin group charged with bringing together the flamekin (Elemental humanoids) with Warriors of all races. And Frogtosser is a warren of totally off-their-rocker, freak-brained boggarts who love sneaking around and causing utter chaos.</p>&#10;<p><b>Bannerets – Flavor Text</b></p>&#10;<p>Some <b>Magic</b> cycles have cycled flavor text—flavor text that all take the same form or rhythm, like the <i>Legions</i> invokers, or have subject matter in common, like the bannerets. Cycles can be hard on flavor text; take it from me, it's pretty common for two members of the cycle to sound really cool, and for the rest to fit badly and sound forced. Still, I asked my intrepid writers to give cycled flavor text a shot for their banneret submissions. If worse came to worst, I could just cherry-pick the best pieces for each card and provide more detail on what these guys were about.</p>&#10;<p>It was <a href="/en/articles/feature/2008/01-28-fit-to-be-tide">Elye Alexander</a> who hit upon the idea of describing the creatures' banners themselves, in particular how they were constructed and how this reflected on each tribe's culture. I loved how his submissions for <a>Ballyrush Banneret</a>, <a>Brighthearth Banneret</a>, and <a>Stonybrook Banneret</a> showed off the differences between the tribes' cultures while retaining a similar subject matter across them all. I wrote a piece in a similar vein for <a>Bosk Banneret</a>—and then as the set drew to a close and <a>Frogtosser Banneret</a> gained some space for flavor text, I wrote another one for the boggart member of the cycle as well. </p>&#10;<p>I'm happy with the cycle, as cycles go. The pieces feel similar one another, yet didn't lock the writers into some kind of bad Mad Libs, where the green one and the black one just have &quot;life&quot; and &quot;death&quot; substituted in each other's flavor text. They help explain aspects of the bannerets themselves while supporting the setwide need to give detail on the tribes and the world as a whole. And they have the satisfying &quot;pattern-completion&quot; aspect of a good cycle, where you read two of them and wonder what the other three will be like.</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

These five simple cards took a long path from initial designs to final cards. Next time you open a booster, take a moment to think about the cards inside. Every one of those cards—even the commons—was the subject of debate, scrutiny, and lots of hard work. So take a good look at that common and ask yourself:

*What's this card's story?*
