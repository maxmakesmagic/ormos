The why is often far more important than the what. Answering what you would do in a certain situation doesn't necessarily explain much about your underlying thought process. Two people can come to the exact same conclusion about what to do when presented with a certain set of circumstances, but their reasons for making their decisions could be completely different.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/a8/5a/a85ae0f548e2bbfae85cac60fa55b918f6537ec5eb69ef42405b96fab3ba7142.jpg) 

  

Sometimes when two (or more) players have come to the same decision for different reasons, they can both be correct in their thinking. Maybe one player deduced the correct play by systematically ruling out other possibilities, while the other player determined the correct play because it would lure the opponent into an elaborate multi-turn trap. Or maybe one player deduced the correct play by counting the outs remaining in his or her, while the other player deduced the correct play by trying to minimize the number of cards that the opponent might be able to draw in order to win outright.

But oftentimes, when multiple people have came to the same decision for different reasons, then at least some of the people have come to the decision for the wrong reasons.

There have been countless times when I've made the right play for the wrong reasons. While this might appear to be better than simply making the wrong decision, in many ways it can actually be much worse. Sure making the right play for the wrong reasons will improve your chances of winning the game at hand, but it can also make it quite difficult for you to learn from your mistakes. 

This is why I'm far more concerned with *why* you would make a play than *what* that play might be.

Before I get to my answer, I want to clear up some misconceptions.

### Is Your Opponent Making an Aggressive Attack?

The fact that your opponent attacked with his or her Goblin Shortcutter does not actually reveal any information to you. Your opponent is obligated to attack in this spot. Your opponent knows that Goblin Shortcutter won't be able to block your Surrakar Marauder next turn (and if it will be able to, then things are looking pretty good no matter what), and there are very, very few reasons why your opponent would not want to trade a vanilla 2/1 with your 2/1 than can easily gain evasion.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/34/de/34de14940727183f3a7eb3fdccb0a0047e92ad13487c0e9ac18531e8f8cae02a.jpg) 

  

In pretty much any situation in which your creature isn't going to be able to block next turn, and you wouldn't mind trading, then you should probably attack. (As a corollary, if you have a creature that cannot be blocked that you wouldn't want to block with, then you should probably attack)

 ![Cunning Sparkmage](http://gatherer.wizards.com/Handlers/Image.ashx?size=small&type=card&name=Cunning%20Sparkmage&options=) 

  

Further, there is almost no chance that your opponent has a Cunning Sparkmage here. If he or she had a Cunning Sparkmage, he or she would almost certainly cast it precombat. Your opponent would not attack first and then cast a postcombat Cunning Sparkmage. Pretty much the only situations where your opponent could be holding a Cunning Sparkmage here would be if your opponent is luring you into some sort of elaborate trap (which isn't worth worrying about at this point) or if he or she has a significantly better play to make. These scenarios are quite unlikely and will reveal themselves to you pretty quickly.

### Crybreaker

Do you care if your opponent casts Slaughter Cry here?

![](https://i.ormos.uk/cd/99/cd99531b67cd13739440894a408a2cd6bcde5b261253f760d0796fbde37d6562.jpg) 

  

Actually, yes you do. But you don't actually care enough for it to alter your play.

Here's why.

In many scenarios you are happy if your opponent uses their combat trick on an early turn. It's generally not that big of a deal to trade your two- or three-drop for a good trick; in fact, you are often happy with that exchange. It will prevent your opponent from being able to make a game-changing play several turns later.

But in this situation, where your opponent's trick is quite expensive, and you have a Searing Blaze, you aren't worried about luring the trick out now as to minimize its impact. It's not like your opponent is going to be able to use that Slaughter Cry to off one of your particularly important creatures—aside from your Vampire Nighthawk, your creatures are all pretty similar—and because Slaughter Cry costs three mana, it's not like your opponent is going to be able to cast it along with another spell anytime soon. 

### What Your Hand Tells You

I do not block here. 

Before I get into why I wouldn't block, I need to mention that if my hand and my deck were slightly different, I could very well see myself blocking. The simple fact that Surrakar Marauder is a better creature than Goblin Shortcutter isn't a good enough reason to not block. Not by a long shot. And the fact that you are still at 20 life isn't a good enough justification either. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/mtg/images/daily/li/li83_surrakar.jpg) 

  

If you have a slow hand in a deck with a really good late game and you are presented with this trade, you would be crazy not to take it—even though you are still at 20.

While there are a number of completely legitimate lines of reasoning that explain why you shouldn't block here, the one that seems most relevant to me is the fact that you *know* that you have all of the tools for a very good aggressive start, whereas your opponent might not have what it takes to deal you 20 quickly. If for some reason you knew that your opponent had an equally aggressive draw (say you had cast a Duress or a Peek-type card on your first turn), then you would be completely justified in blocking. 

One player in the forums identified the best case scenario being if your opponent doesn't have any follow-up plays and explained this as a situation in which blocking would turn out very well for you as you would have an enormous advantage. While I agree that the best-case scenario for you in this position is for your opponent not to have any follow-up plays, and I also agree that you would have an enormous advantage if you traded and then began casting formidable creatures for several consecutive turns, it does not follow that it is correct to block if your opponent doesn't have follow-up plays. In fact, if I somehow knew that my opponent didn't have plays to make, then the last thing that I would want to do is block here. Instead I would want to bury my opponent as quickly as possible, so that he or she wouldn't have an opportunity to draw out of this bad position.

### I'll Race Against the Other Racers

Racing against a slow deck and racing against a fast deck are two very different processes. When you are racing against a slow deck, you are generally sprinting to try to beat your opponent before he or she is able to take over with more powerful cards. This is the type of racing that often involves chump-blocking large creatures for several consecutive turns while you peck your opponent down to zero (or until you draw that burn spell to deal the final 3 points of damage). 

![](http://www.wizards.com/mtg/images/daily/li/li83_blaze.jpg) 

  

While you are usually able to focus more on your opponent's life total during the early stages of a game against a slower deck, when you are racing to 20 against another aggressive deck, you need to pay just as much attention to your own life total as you do to your opponent's. Even on turn two.

However, the fact that you will (likely) be able to determine whether or not a trade should take place between your opponent's Goblin Shortcutter and your Surrakar Marauder in the future means that you can comfortably plan on trading some hits now even if you decide you have to make that trade later.

So once you've decided not to trade because you've realized that you have the proper tools in your hand to win a race, what should you plan on doing next turn? (Alternatively, and often more effectively, you would want to roughly outline your subsequent plays before you choose whether or not to block.)

When you are trying to understand the terms of the race that you are engaging in, you must look at both the short- and long-term implications of your potential plays. While you would normally look to make the plays that would knock your opponent down to zero the quickest against a slow deck, mana efficiency is often the most important factor when playing against another quick deck. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/mtg/images/daily/li/li83_stonework.jpg) 

  

So while it might seem more attractive to cast a second Surrakar Marauder next turn to keep the pressure on your opponent, you should strongly consider casting your Stonework Puma here, as it will allow you to fully utilize your mana this turn and will make it easier for you to fully utilize your mana in the future. 

If your opponent does not make a play after knocking you down to 18 (and your attack goes through unmolested), you will want to cast your second Surrakar Marauder. If instead your opponent casts, say, a Goblin Roughrider, then you will want to cast your Stonework Puma with plans to block (under this scenario, your most likely fourth turn will be Searing Blaze + Surrakar Marauder in order to reestablish your position, not Hedron Rover). In fact, if your opponent casts almost any sort of 2+ power creature, you will probably want to cast your Stonework Puma with plans on blocking with it.

If your opponent does not do anything to advance his or her board, you will likely be better off casting your second Surrakar Marauder with plans on casting your Hedron Rover the following turn.

While there are situations in which casting either one of your creatures would be correct, casting a Searing Blaze on your next turn would be a very poor play. Yes, it is the play that provides the greatest immediate advantage in terms of board position and life totals—if you knew for some reason that you were going to have to discard your hand at the end of your next turn, then that's probably the play your would make—but you can't just look at the next turn when determining your plays. You have to look further into the future.

Your Searing Blaze is likely to go up further and further in value with each passing turn, as it will allow you to destroy something quite formidable (possibly knocking off a Shatterskull Giant, or even an Emeria Angel at an insane profit) while still leaving you free to cast another relevant spell on the same turn. Your opponent's Goblin Shortcutter, on the other hand, will still be just a vanilla 2/1.

So, in short, if you are worried about falling far behind in the race, cast your Stonework Puma, leaving yourself the very real option of casting two relevant spells on turn four. If you see yourself getting ahead in the race, capitalize on that advantage by casting a second Surrakar Marauder. Unless your opponent plays something extremely threatening, like a Warren Instigator along with an Adventuring Gear, then you should plan on waiting on your Searing Blaze.
