### World Championship Recap

**The Winners**

World Championships are now over, leaving a staggering amount of interesting data to sift through. The most commonly-asked Worlds-related question this side of the Atlantic so far seems to be “who the heck is this Zink guy?”

![](https://i.ormos.uk/7c/e7/7ce707edb1b164132fda83ffb2f7544bd5dca538d52847dd1e92db7ca772b019.jpg)

Surprisingly little is currently posted about our new World Champion. He is eighteen and he hails from Bochum, Germany. He was introduced to the game around the time *Mirage* was out, and played in tournaments for about a year and a half when he broke through with a Top 8 finish at Grand Prix-Cologne and the German National Title in the same year. Zink followed that up with a fourth place finish at European Championships that year. That was the last the **Magic** community heard of Zink until now. After having such a great year in 2001, he took some time off **Magic** to pursue other interests. He made his return to Pro Tour glory in Venice this year, where he finished 35th overall. 

Zink started out strong with a 5-1 finish on Day 1, playing a Mirari's Wake deck. He followed it with another 5-1 during draft. This meant Zink only needed ten points on Day 3 and he got them using a Reanimator deck, finishing sixth after eighteen rounds of Swiss. Zink had to play an exhausting Top 8 where he battled two mirror matches, but he emerged victorious in the end, his sideboard proving very effective against Okamoto in the finals.

Jin Okamoto, easily one of the best players in Asia, is also a new name to learn for most people reading this column. Known in Japan as “Mr. Tiebreaker”, Okamoto has missed the opportunity to play on Sunday twice now. At the World Championships in Toronto Okamoto finished eleventh, in a five-way tie for seventh place. At 2002 Pro Tour-San Diego, he was the only player with 32 points to miss the Top 8, finishing ninth overall. 

Okamoto's other nickname is “The Last Emperor” as he won the last APAC Championships ever held, in 2001. He also has a pair of Grand Prix Top 8 finishes to his name. Although Okamoto speaks virtually no English, he has earned the respect and admiration of his peers, and is well recognized on the Pro Tour. With this tournament result, he becomes the third Japanese player to earn a finalist finish at a Pro Tour event. Because this is Worlds, he won more money than the others, thereby achieving the highest tournament finish by a player from Asia.

As with Zink and Okamoto, this is also the first Pro Tour Top 8 for Finland's Tuomo Nieminen. His only previous career finish was 2nd place at Grand Prix-Stockholm in 1997, but Nieminen did have a number of money finishes and achieved gravy train status last year. He was thrown off his game by having to spend almost a year serving in the army. He was back in time to compete in Finnish Nationals, and placed second there. Nieminen's Top 4 finish and team Finland's performance was just enough to put him back on the gravy train. He is certainly among the players to watch next season.

Following three Pro Tour Top 8 rookies is a true veteran. Dave Humpherys is one of two members of team Your Move Games – and the only Americans – to make Top 8. One of the game's veterans, Humpherys trophy case features fourth place finishes at Pro Tour-New Orleans, 2002 World Championships, 1999 Pro Tour-New York, and now Worlds in Berlin. He seems to have some kind of special affinity for fourth place. Humpherys is also a Team Pro Tour-Washington champion as part of the YMG trio. Add to that two Grand Prix wins (one individual, one team) and two more Top 4 team finishes, and you have a career anyone would be proud of. Humpherys is currently eighth on the lifetime money winners list, with just over $130,000. 

### The Teams

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/worlds03/a442.jpg)

To no one's surprise, the United States team made the finals once again. Their opponent this time around was Finland. Finland has maintained a solid lead in the team competition throughout most of the tournament. Brazil was also doing very well and it wasn't until the very end of Day 3 that U.S.A. overtook them for the second place in the standings. Both U.S. and Finland continued to do well during team day. Their slots in the finals were locked in before the final round was even played.

In the third place, to everyone's surprise, was Team Malaysia. They went undefeated during Team Day, rising from fourteenth to third place, only five points behind United States. France rounded off the Top 4.

In terms of pride, Justin Gary had the most on the line in the finals. Back in 1997, he led the only United States National Team in the history of Worlds to fail at getting the first place. It was going to be close this time, as both teams drafted very solid decks. In fact, I predicted during coverage that Finns would take it 2-1. Josh Wagener quickly lost his match and both other Americans were down a game. Then Gary evened the score 1-1 as Walls won his match. It was all up to Gary, who pulled a victory against Arho Toikka to secure victory for the defending champion nation.

### The Decks

The World Championships was the first chance for the pros to showcase exactly what they would do with the post-*Eighth Edition* metagame. It appears the answer was Mirari's Wake. It was clearly the best deck in the field, as five of the six undefeated players at the end of the day were running it. However, the player to win Day 1 was France's Sylvain Lauriol with Goblin Bidding. The Japanese-made deck made its first appearance at Grand Prix-Bangkok where it absolutely dominated the metagame. This weekend the goblins were back in force, played among others by Jon Finkel (monored version), Zvi Mowshowitz, and Top 8 finisher Wolfgang Eder. Other popular decks included black-white control (similar to Nassif's build from European Championship) and blue-green madness.

Day 2 featured Rochester Draft and was won by none other than Kai Budde. Budde, who finished 17th overall, favored a white-blue soldier archetype. He managed to draft that once, and a black-red deck another time to go undefeated.

Extended was the most interesting, as each team wondered just how insane everyone else's version of Mind's Desire deck would be. Combined with cards like Intuition, Frantic Search, and Turnabout, it is possible to build consistent Mind's Desire combo deck that would kill on turns 3 or 4. It seems that either the Desire decks were not as good as many believed, or the rest of the field simply over-metagamed against them, but none that I know of achieved a 5-1 or better record. There wasn't a lot of information on this posted during live coverage, so I know of only two undefeated decks on Day 3 – Florent Jeudon (who won the day) with Tinker, and my own Benzo (Reanimator) deck that went 5-0-1. The field was extremely diverse, with goblins, Mind's Desire and Reanimator being some of the most popular archetypes. Several players I spoke to commented that they managed to play all six rounds without facing the same deck twice. That is a sign of a healthy metagame.

### Player of the Year

![](https://i.ormos.uk/d6/f0/d6f0a047ad3c5af1cded49406383a5d4204119fb80fbb58b0de18992d3c399f1.jpg)

Kai Budde holds the title once again, surprising no one. He finished the season with a total of eighty points. Justin Gary and Mattias Jorstedt are tied for second place at 64 points, with Dirk Baberowski following at 58.

Masashi Oiso earned the Rookie of the Year title with 49 points, beating out his closest competitor by a whopping 21 points.

Mark Rosewater was pleasantly surprised to find over 100 participants playing at the Magic trivia show he hosts at every Pro Tour stop. Mark commented that this is the second-largest trivia show he ever held, beating out even the one at the World Championships in Yokohama. The largest one was held at Origins sometime over five years ago.

In a related story, on behalf of *Sideboard* staff, I would like to congratulate Mark and his wife as they are expecting twins in January. Way to go Mark!

Question Mark was not the only super-sized side event at Worlds. The crowds were staggering. One of the PTQs held over the course of the weekend gathered over 400 participants. Tournaments with 100+ players in them were run regularly. Wizards of the Coast appear to have done a great job promoting this event, and it paid off in numbers.

### Battlegrounds

![](http://www.wizards.com/global/images/sideboard_wir_20030827_picMain_en.jpg)

Worlds attendants got their first look at Battlegrounds – an XBOX real-time strategy game based in the world of **Magic: The Gathering**. The demo version present appeared to be an early beta copy, still missing most of the more impressive features of the game. In a nutshell, it is a real-time combat game where you control a Wizard who can conjure creatures (like Elvish Pioneer, Horned Turtle or Deaths-Hand Buzzard) to harass the opponent or defend you from his creatures. You will also have access to a variety of spells that can influence the battlefield. 

The demo failed to truly impress, but again, it was an early beta copy. Those who have access to the game at WOTC offices assure that it is a solid game and should be worth another look once a more detailed demo is released.

Justin Gary played against Arho Toikka during the first round of team competition at worlds this year. It looked grim for the Americans as Gabe Walls had already lost his match and Justin was down a game to Toikka's bomb-laden red-green deck.

Toikka delivered the killing blow when he cast Wave of Indifference, preventing any of Gary's creatures from blocking for the turn and attacked with his team of giant monsters. There wasn't much Gary's r-b-u deck could do about it and the Finn could already smell victory… except that Gary used his Skirk Alarmist to unmorph a Frontline Strategist! There was no white mana in Gary's deck but, as his draft did not go well, he was forced to play with an off-color morph. The Strategist's Fog effect prevented Toikka's forces from winning the game and Gary was able to attack back on the following turn to win. Gary ended up winning his match and U.S. drew against Finland overall that round.

Raphael Levy's u-g deck met Christopher Gibbons's Sneak Attack build in the second round of Day 3 at Worlds. Levy knew it did not bode well for him when he Sneak Attack hit play. He attacked with Wild Mongrel and left three mana open to summon an Arrogant blocker if need arose. His opponent tapped one red mana and put into play… Phage, the Untouchable.

Gibbons was very disappointed to find out that the combo did not work. In fact, since Phage came into play without being cast, he lost the game. Gibbons actually checked the validity of the combo with a judge in South Africa, where he is from, and the judge incorrectly told him that this would work. 

Moral of the story – always double-check any weird combo tricks you might try to pull with the more experienced judges at the Pro Tour before you submit the deck list.

He was winning on the table as his Wild Mongrel and Roar of the Wurm squared off against an Arrogant Wurm. There were no cards in either player's hand, and no Wonders in either graveyard. This was the fifth of the extra turns and Lo Moro had but one draw to find a way to kill his opponent, who was at four life.

Lo Moro drew his card and accepted a draw, throwing down a… Careful Study! Once his teammates and friends rushed to the table chiding him for his mistake, they revealed the top two cards of his library. Sure enough, there was Deep Analysis there. 

Lo Moro could have won the game by flipping either Deep Analysis (which he could then use to draw two cards and discard them to Mongrel) or Wonder over the top two cards of his library. 

Moral of the story – never give up until every last option is exhausted.
