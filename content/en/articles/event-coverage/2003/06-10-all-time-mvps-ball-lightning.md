Last week’s Sligh poll was very close. I received more email from Fireblast supporters than for either Ball Lightning or Jackal Pup. When I think back to the golden age of Sligh, right around *Tempest*, the card I immediately think of is Fireblast. While Jackal Pup softened up my opponents and Ball Lightning put them into Fireblast range, it was Fireblast that so often ended games. But, in the end, Ball Lightning edged out Fireblast for this week’s MVP. This can be attributed to a number of factors. For one, Ball Lightning had a longer window to impress people, being Standard-legal for two core sets, as opposed to Fireblast’s life of a single block. Ball Lightning is much splashier than Fireblast, being a rare creature with a huge effect on the game. It’s more damage at a “lower cost”. Well, we’ll delve more into Ball Lightning in the next few paragraphs. For everyone who wrote in about Fireblast and Jackal Pup, don’t worry – they’ll be back on the ballot before too long.

### What’s a Ball Lightning?

Six damage for ![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif). Ball Lightning is one of the quickest, most efficient ways to deal damage to your opponent. A 6/1 package for three mana seems too good to be true. And it is. Ball Lightning’s main drawback is that it is a temporary creature. Your opponent can always just opt to take six, putting you back a card. In most decks, this alone would be enough of a drawback to leave Ball Lightning out. I mean, nobody plays Lava Axe. There’s also the very daunting casting cost. Triple red is difficult to pull off and this is a card you absolutely want to cast early, fating Ball Lightning almost exclusively to very focused monored designs.

On the surface, Ball Lightning is a simple card. Its effect on individual games makes it much more complex. A 6/1 without trample would have very little effect against any deck with creatures, even with haste. Trample really makes the difference on Ball Lightning. Consider that the rules of trample have changed significantly since *The Dark*. When Fifth Edition was released, the rules of trample (and many other rules) were reworked. Before Fifth Edition, a player was forced to assign lethal damage to only a single blocker before being allowed to spill damage over to his or her opponent. This allowed Ball Lightning to barrel through the opposition much easier than under current rules, which stipulate that you must assign lethal damage to each blocker before assigning any damage to your opponent. This weakened trample overall and especially on Ball Lightning, as a large blocker or multiple small blockers could prevent damage from getting through, which is the whole point of the card.

Released in 1994, Ball Lightning was not a power card at first. Though players noticed its high damage-to-cost ratio and generally thought of it as a cool card, it was severely hampered by the metagame at *The Dark’s* release. Though Ball Lightning packs a powerful punch, it was exceedingly easy to deal with in 1994. Virtually every deck was playing some cheap spot removal, like Lightning Bolt or Swords to Plowshares, and cards like Icy Manipulator and Maze of Ith - which was released in the same set - offered players very effective ways of eliminating the Ball Lightning, sometimes without even costing them a card. Additionally, most control decks were plugged into the life gain provided by Ivory Tower. When played properly, the Tower would earn its caster 2-3 life points per turn, rapidly erasing the effect of Ball Lightning. In short, Ball Lightning was unleashed upon the metagame – and the metagame was prepared.

In 1996, a new deck emerged: Sligh. At first, it was a clunky-looking red deck that won through mana optimization, some card advantage and an incredible aggressive redundancy. No Ball Lightnings. While the deck had concepts that were way ahead of its time, red was unfortunately sidled with a suboptimal set of creatures (Paul Sligh’s original decklist, found [here](https://web.archive.org/web/20030610000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://classicdojo.org/history/sligh2.html&origin=sideboard_feature_20030610a), uses Dwarven Traders and Goblins of the Flarg, which are not exactly Jackal Pups and Mogg Fanatics). 

It wasn’t until 1997 that Sligh started making the transition from a strange utility/beatdown deck into a full-fledged offensive machine. Dave Price, who would later pilot a monored *Tempest* deck to victory at Pro Tour-Los Angeles, brought this deck to Nationals:

It would become known as Deadguy Red. Price played on Team Deadguy, hence the name, but the title was very apt. Taking the haste approach to the extreme, Price’s deck features a full compliment of Ball Lightnings, complimented by two Viashino Sandstalkers in the same slot for redundancy and adding Lava Hounds in the four slot for additional haste beats. The brute force approach won Price kudos (and an undefeated record in the Standard portion of U.S. Nationals) and showcased the power of fleeting haste creatures, most notably Ball Lightning. 

When *Tempest* arrived with Jackal Pup, Mogg Fanatic, Fireslinger and Cursed Scroll, Sligh took its final leap from its utilitarian past into the realm of pure beats. Replacing the weaker creatures with these powerful new guys, Sligh was able to place several powerful threats around Ball Lightning, forcing opponents to deal with a wide variety of cheap, dangerous damage sources. At the same time, colors other than red were going through a drought of good, cheap removal spells. The result was breathtaking. Turn four kills were not uncommon at all – and players fought frantically against the tide of damage, trying to avoid “Fireblast range”, which clocked in a four, eight and sometimes even twelve life. With the threat of Fireblast as a finisher, players could ill afford to take six from a Ball Lightning. And with those same players desperately trying to deal with Sligh’s other dangerous creatures, Ball Lightning started slipping through the cracks more and more often. 

### Why Was It Great?

<table><tbody><tr><td><table><tbody><tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/65/b4/65b44cec90bda938bbb278a79acba1f455a38a19b714807bea68c253b77de30d.jpg"></td><td></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/dd/0a/dd0a617788694ee0e82eed58b0746ab9dbdbdb6e709ee5390dcfb32556a6990e.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/96/f5/96f5d53e3ef958055c4fdf5bf835be99797bd18449a68a882c96ab3801bae612.jpg"></td><td><a><img alt="Phyrexian Negator" src="https://i.ormos.uk/cf/ac/cfacd63a626da4b47b958dc616a15d7ab28175164d3268957cbd0afd137f18fd.jpg"></a></td><td></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/a0/a9/a0a91e69ef087e17e689f758224146196f0a29fad934eb2639d2fb73d4cf3c75.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c0/e2/c0e24bff2fa206011911cb4b5cb20b4559cb82301a77e757b6e86de3c1ae659a.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/fa/ab/faab219b94a5d6a8405ff77aefe6f815ba2bba52fc447652a633eec85e227836.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table><br><i>Black’s Ball Lightning</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Nowhere in the game of **Magic** will you find a creature that reduces your opponent’s life total faster than Ball Lightning. Six damage on the third turn puts it into a very elite class of creatures, most of which have more serious drawbacks than dying at the end of the turn. Phyrexian Negator is comparable in its ability to deal quick damage, but it virtually requires a Dark Ritual to get going early enough to be effective, and then it can strand you in a terrible situation. Even the threat of Ball Lightning can reshape an entire game, as a player hovering around six in a close game can not risk tapping out or dropping their defense in the face of the devastating effect of a Ball Lightning-led burst of damage. Sometimes, even that didn’t matter. In the face of games that go turn 1 Jackal Pup, turn 2 Pup, Fanatic, turn 3 Ball Lightning, turn 4 damage spells (or another Ball Lightning!), Fireblast, Fireblast, very few decks can survive. Even in those decks, survival often required the player to draw a specific card, like Gerrard’s Wisdom, and be able to play it on the fourth turn (while playing first), or face near-certain death.

### The Big Picture

<table><tbody><tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/65/b4/65b44cec90bda938bbb278a79acba1f455a38a19b714807bea68c253b77de30d.jpg"></td><td></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/dd/0a/dd0a617788694ee0e82eed58b0746ab9dbdbdb6e709ee5390dcfb32556a6990e.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/96/f5/96f5d53e3ef958055c4fdf5bf835be99797bd18449a68a882c96ab3801bae612.jpg"></td><td><a><img alt="Blistering Firecat" src="https://i.ormos.uk/59/9c/599cfb7f97e9d74309c4266376785845c03980572c70f511a2cdb0df78a81325.jpg"></a></td><td></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/a0/a9/a0a91e69ef087e17e689f758224146196f0a29fad934eb2639d2fb73d4cf3c75.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c0/e2/c0e24bff2fa206011911cb4b5cb20b4559cb82301a77e757b6e86de3c1ae659a.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/fa/ab/faab219b94a5d6a8405ff77aefe6f815ba2bba52fc447652a633eec85e227836.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Over the years, there have been a ton of Ball Lightning remakes and variants, none of which have rivaled the strength of the original. Ranging from the sketchy Lightning Elemental, which was basically unplayable in Constructed, to the here-today-gone-tomorrow Viashino Sandstalker, to newer versions like Skizzik and Blistering Firecat, both of which are decent-to-good, but still often come up short for Constructed decks, the variations keep coming. Most of them try to make Ball Lightning into a more durable creature at increased cost, be it the Sandstalker’s constant mana cost or actually upping the mana cost, as in the cast of Skizzik, and making the creature permanent, with better toughness. The closest remake of Ball Lightning has been *Onslaught*’s Blistering Firecat, which tacked on a morph option, a generic mana and a point of power. Though strong, the combination of being a slower card and red’s less brutal damage spells have forced Ball Lightning-type spells away from tier 1. Without the correct mix of efficient creatures and burn spells, very aggressive Sligh-style decks aren’t strong, though the memory of Dave Price’s successes with the archetype inspire players everywhere to try and rebuild red beatdown in almost every metagame. As recently as *Onslaught* Block Constructed, players have successfully built aggressive red decks that have succeeded at the Pro Tour level.
