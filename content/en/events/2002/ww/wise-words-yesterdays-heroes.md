Today I am a sad man.

I should be a happy man. I've gotten myself a nice little place in Toronto's nether regions, Houston made for a nice little weekend and I have the cable modem, cable television and car I'd missed so much while in England. A lot of life's little things are going my way right now. You'd think I'd be more appreciative.

I went for the most depressing drive today. I went to the little barber shop where I got my $10 hair cuts before leaving for England, paying $23 this time around for what equates to little more than a head shave. I won't be going back. When I left, murmuring unpublishables beneath my breath, I hopped in my car and drove north instead of my usual south. I figured I'd go visit Shoebox Heroes.

I was introduced to **Magic** by my oft-mentioned friend Shawn Altbaum during Spring Break 1995, while home from school. He taught me the game. I bought my first cards, had them stolen from my coat at a club and went back to school, knowing I'd never bother to play again. Of course, I saw some people playing in my dorm, sat down to play with them and the rest played itself out predictably, but school ended a few months later, and after I'd returned to Toronto and taught Shawn the hard way what Chaos Orb did, he got tired of losing pretty quickly and I was left trying to find new places to play the game. I leafed through the yellow pages to the collectables section and called a few stores asking if they sold **Magic** or better yet, knew of places I could play. Most had no clue what I was talking about. Shoebox Heroes did.

The store was owned by two brothers, Mark and Mitch. Mark, the elder, was the businessman. Mitch was the goofy skateboarding member of this or that band. The store dealt in figurines, comics, RPGs and **Magic**, with one eight-person table for gaming. Heaven.

Every day, I'd make my way to the store's starchy white interior. "Gary, go look for a job," the parental units would urge. "No problem", I'd say, hop in my car, and head to Shoebox (it's ironic that I seem to have found a job there… that wasn't the intention, I assure you). Every day, I'd get together with the handful of other regulars, playing with them, stragglers, Mark, Mitch or whoever, trading, just having good times. I was addicted to the game: there was no Pro Tour, there were no checks showing up at the door. There was only the game.

I guess my time away from Toronto left me nostalgic for those people, places and things I'd taken for granted in my earlier years here, and that's why I went north today instead of my usual south. I wanted to go check out those old digs, abandoned for places with more players and more room to play, devoid of the warmth and comfort provided by that home away from home up in the burbs. I wanted to walk into that store and chat it up with Mark, tell him about the road he'd sent me down all those years ago, give him some small inkling towards the effect he and his open-armed attitude had on my life.

I drove through that stretch near the store I'd driven so many times before, with new houses having cropped up and business changed before time's very eyes. Finally, after a few false alarms, I saw the gas station that marked that all too familiar parking lot. It was still there. I revved up the engine and sped a little too fast into the friendly confines, drove up to my familiar parking space, and got out of the car.

Right in front of Ha-lal's East Asian Food Market.

Shoebox Heroes is gone. Lingering are the memories of days gone by, of opening an Ice Age sealed deck with uncommons in the common slot, of ripping off and getting ripped off, of a larger than life 5'5" guy named Ryan Yuen coming to the store sporadically with his gang of merry men, a very young Gab Tsang included in their ranks, carrying with them an attitude that said "These are our tables. You should feel privileged that we let you lose on them" with their confidence. Shoebox Heroes was my childhood home, struck down by the collective wrecking ball of competition and capitalism. All that remains is the residue that is its memory.

We each have our Shoebox Heroes. Those markers on our gaming journey that have sent us down the roads we've eventually chosen. Last week, before he won the Sunday PTQ in Houston, his next to last chance to qualify for Chicago, Dave Price, who has been around since the beginning of time, told me that if he didn't qualify for Chicago, he'd be done. With his victory in Houston though, Dave's journey continues. Not everyone's does.

What follows is a look at ten names for whom the Pro Tour odyssey is completed, but whose examples serve as reminders of what has made the game great. These are the guys who taught us how to play, either by innovation, explanation or example. Without them, it would be a different Pro Tour today:

### 1) Mark Justice

**Innovation: The Pro**

Simply, Mark Justice was the Kai Budde of the mid-nineties. Mark admitted to a degree of sketchiness after his career was done, but was universally considered the best player in the world around the time the Pro Tour was getting started, operating with what can simply described as a greater understanding of the game than the rest of the community. 

Justice never won that one worldwide tournament that would ice him on paper as one of the true greats, but those who were there will tell you his name should be mentioned with the likes of Finkel and Budde. He came in second at PT-Paris and Worlds 1997, won U.S. Nationals back in that day and transformed the idea of the full time **Magic** player from a joke to reality. He also tried to unionize all pro players and represent a few as an agent simultaneously, and while this conflict of interest worked against him, it could never be said that Mark wasn't trying to do more than just pick up his checks: he understood that helping the game would lead to bigger ones. It did. 

### 2) Brian Weissman

**Innovation: Card advantage**

<table><tbody><tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/65/b4/65b44cec90bda938bbb278a79acba1f455a38a19b714807bea68c253b77de30d.jpg"></td><td></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/dd/0a/dd0a617788694ee0e82eed58b0746ab9dbdbdb6e709ee5390dcfb32556a6990e.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/96/f5/96f5d53e3ef958055c4fdf5bf835be99797bd18449a68a882c96ab3801bae612.jpg"></td><td><a><img alt="Balance" src="https://i.ormos.uk/cd/e6/cde68f21f19dd707b0c9132a54a964f67481f3d62ca2d38be249b1cbe081e49f.jpg"></a></td><td></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/a0/a9/a0a91e69ef087e17e689f758224146196f0a29fad934eb2639d2fb73d4cf3c75.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c0/e2/c0e24bff2fa206011911cb4b5cb20b4559cb82301a77e757b6e86de3c1ae659a.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/fa/ab/faab219b94a5d6a8405ff77aefe6f815ba2bba52fc447652a633eec85e227836.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Weissman is the father of card advantage. He didn't give birth to the concept, though he had an understanding of it from the beginning, always favoring cards like Mind Twist, Chaos Orb, Balance and Library of Alexandria. None of this quite qualifies as brain surgery today, but before the PT was well under way, Juzam Djinn was still considered bad because the point of damage per turn hurt too much. Weissman didn't even create The Deck, the white-blue-red control deck he became famous for. No, that was his longtime running mate John Immordino, but Brian brought the concepts to the masses through newsgroup posts, forever changing the way people looked at the game and becoming a legend in the process. Weissman still shows up to hang out at the occasional California Pro Tour.

### 3) DKLA (John Yoo, Truc Bui, Brian Hacker, Jason Zila)

**Innovation: Beatdown**

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/GPSD99/images/gpsd12.jpg)Originally known by a much less appropriate name, DKLA adopted this one when Justice began his representation practices. These four Californians, later joined by the likes of Gab Tsang, Igor Frayman, Gabe Higa and Sigurd Eskeland in their escapades, brought to the table everything that was fun about the game while at the same time understanding that there was more to **Magic** than just controlling your opponent with card advantage.

These guys beat down. Hard. If it cost more than one, Bui likely wouldn't play it, and the others weren't too far behind. Like Weissman, DKLA was very active on the newsgroups, expressing opinions, wanted or otherwise, on everything from the cards to the players, and in doing so they taught the world how to draft and draft properly. Fifteen creatures. Seventeen lands. Eight spells. These were the guys who know how that worked. Brian Hacker still shows up to play once in a while. If he does and you like the forty card decks, be sure to thank him. 

### 4) Hammer

**Innovation: Trash talk** 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/USNAT01/929.jpg)Okay, Hammer didn't introduce trash talk to the game of **Magic**. He wasn't the first to use it, certainly won't be the last, but as for best… well, its hard to argue against him. 

He made Top 8 at the first PT and won the second, Hammer, a.k.a. Shawn Regnier, was a former professional arm wrestler and no stranger to the professional arena that so many of the old timers were being introduced to in a trial by fire. Hammer's trash talk was so fierce it is reputed to have caused Tom Guevin to vomit when they played during the Finals at 1996 PT-LA (Guevin denies this to be the case), and even after his game failed to keep up with his mercurial mouth, it was understood that regardless of the 24-inch pythons, this was not a man to be trifled with. Hammer last appeared at Barcelona a year and a half ago. 

### 5) George Baxter

**Innovation: Mass media**

One of the early greats, Baxter, a member of the 1997 U.S. National Team, was considered by many to be the best of them all (Baxter, Mike Long, Matt Place and U.S. Champ Dennis Bentley). He has been retired for about half a decade now. In 2001, he honeymooned in Toronto and while walking along the street during Worlds, I bumped into him, let him know what was going on and dragged him and his bewildered wife in to see the show, where he regaled us with war stories of yesteryear.

As good as he was, Baxter's play will not be what he's remembered for in this game. Instead, he'll be remembered for the books he wrote in the early years teaching the world the fundamentals of game play and deck building that became staples for the developing player and the parent looking for Christmas gifts for the gamer who had everything. Now somewhat dated, the Baxter books have all but disappeared, but they do offer a good look at what the game once was and offer an opportunity to understand how our perceptions of what it takes to win in this game have changed over the years.

### 6) Matt Place

**Innovation: Rules lawyering**

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/GPdal00/fm3place.jpg)Of course Place didn't invent this stuff. PCL and Tongo were doing it since the dawn of time, but Matt, he was the master. A fresh-faced kid out of the Midwest, Place was dominating the tourney scene in that area way before the PT came about. When it finally did, the man won Mainz despite a grand total of three practice drafts, testimony to the talent he possessed.

He found a rules loophole that allowed you to ask your opponent if you could Lightning Bolt them after they'd drawn, with a 'yes' meaning their main phase was done, effectively ending their turn. Of course, these problems were solved by the rules gurus as quickly as possible, but they left their mark.

### 7) Alan Comer

**Innovation: Fun on the Tour**

![](https://i.ormos.uk/5c/95/5c9589a9db8d044dcc4926d554c67a341bdf3e99287284272cdcf504f577867d.jpg)Shock, dismay, how dare I speak of such things? The Pro Tour and its players have a rep for a cold, calculating demeanor that in the eyes of casuals, that sucks anything that might be construed as fun out of the game. Alan Comer's six-year career proved the antithesis to these theories.

While Alan never managed to corral a title of his own, he did make it to five Sundays before hanging up the wand recently to go work for Wizards on **Magic** Online. While still active, Comer's forte was building unconventional decks. I still remember testing High Tide, Survival and Oath in Rome with Scott Johns and Sigurd Eskeland while Alan was in the corner building a white weenie deck with four Enlightened Tutors and one hateful enchantment for each major archetype. He played that deck and went 6-1 on Day 1.

Alan's creations included Godzilla, and Turbo Xerox. While he popularized Miracle Grow, and as befits his decks, any time you saw him at the table, he was there with a big smile on his face. Comer proved to the world you can succeed on the Tour while having fun and being a nice guy. That's pretty good stuff to be remembered for.

### 8) Jakub Slemr

**Innovation: European strength**

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gppra01/867.jpg)Another guy who had a lot of fun with the game, Jakub Slemr was around since the early days. Yes, other non-Americans like Olle Rade and Frank Adler had grabbed big titles, but when Jakub won World Championships, it was a wake up call to America: There's going to be competition from all over the world. 

Jakub really does have to share credit here. The Top 8 at 1997 Worlds was a study in diversity, with Slemr defeating German Janosch Kuhn in the finals, with Denmark's Svend Geertsen and Canada's Paul McCabe rounding out the Top 4. Slemr went on to make two more Top 8s before quitting for work reasons last year.

### 9) PCL (Pacific Coast Legends: Mark Justice, Mark Chalice, Henry Stern, Mario Robaina, Preston Poulter, Scott Johns, Frank Gilson, cast of thousands)

**Innovation - The team**

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/PTNY0001/images/528.jpg)Before YMG and Jumble and Godzilla and Mogg Squad and Deadguy and Punisher and all the rest, there was PCL. The first truly great team of the Pro Tour era, PCL dominated early on, both in the main events and side events. They understood before everyone else the importance of strength in numbers, and provided an example that is followed by the Tour's best teams today and will continue to be for the foreseeable future.

PCL placed Justice and Poulter in the Top 8 of PT-NY, and set the tone for their dominance in the first year. Johns made three consecutive Top 8s, Chalice made one of his own, Justice and Stern made Top 8 at 1996 Worlds… they were strength personified. Eventually, Stern moved on to WotC, Justice left the west coast and the team drifted apart for assorted reasons, but their mark had been left on the Tour. The smart gamer would never play alone again.

### 10) Frank Kusomoto

**Innovation: Internet utilization**

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTCHI00/921.jpg)While not a pro player in his own right, Frank Kusomoto may have made the most important innovation on this list. A naval officer and **Magic** enthusiast, Frank noticed a trend towards the game's intellectualization in newsgroups cropping from the likes of Hacker and Weissman and began organizing the better thought out posts on his website, www.thedojo.com. What started as a little hobby started consuming most of the man's time as more and more gamers, hungry for information, started flocking as word spread.

The Dojo was absolutely amazing in its day, with no competitors meaning no supply and demand for writers, in turn meaning that a lot of people gave of their time for no reason other than to spread the **Magic** gospel and share their gaming experiences. Most of the names on this list participated at one time or another. You can still find their work archived on the net. 

In the end, as competitors cropped up, the Dojo tried to stay competitive, with the likes of Rob Hahn and Mike Flores doing their best to keep it afloat, but times changed and left the Dojo behind as more money flowed into the game. Frank, overwhelmed by the task the Dojo had become, eventually sold it and got out of the game. He's the reason that sites like Star City, Brainburst, Grimmoire, Misetings and even Team Academy have found themselves an important place in the **Magic** world today.

That's about it. This list was inspired in part by my thoughts on Dave Price, the man who taught the world to beat down, but his continued activity forbids his place on it. I'm glad you qualified for Chicago Dave, you have to keep it going. The Tour wouldn't be the same without you.

Have a good week.
