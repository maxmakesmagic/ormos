At the time, I had opened maybe six total packs of Revised. I had been playing **Magic: the Gathering** for less than a month and I was sitting in the kitchen of the house of the friend who would someday be the best man at my second wedding, who had, accidentally, at a party some weeks before, introduced me to this fascinating collectible card game. His little brother started shuffling up some red and white spells and showed me two creatures that he thought I might like.

The first one was Keldon Warlord. That guy could be pretty big. Dreams of red and green armies led by an appropriately huge general, possibly being pumped by the ridiculously powerful Orcish Oriflamme, started to dance in my head. In that simpler twilight time, before we knew about complex strategies or even realized how asymmetrical a spell like Wrath of God could be, we saw mostly the potential of cards, not the fact that they could sometimes - even oftentimes - end up being relatively inoffensive 1/1 creatures for a pricey ![2 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif).

 ![](https://i.ormos.uk/c4/6b/c46b611a11a603ed6101091446be4539835e7a9b4c9739018b52b5d952e698db.jpg) 

The other card, and the card that forever changed my outlook on creatures, was White Knight.

I immediately wanted to trade for White Knight. It was so cheap and sleek and packed full of abilities. These were the days when we played with seven Terrors in our decks. He had protection from black! How could we possibly stop this guy? Sure, we had five or six Lightning Bolts in our seventy-five card decks, but those were much too efficient as damage packets sent to the opponent's dome to be wasted on mere creatures... why else would we devote so much space to Terrors? He also had first strike! This little beater could combine with everything from Giant Growth to Holy Strength to take down even the biggest creatures on the other side of the battlefield... even when they were as burly as Craw Wurm himself.

But the thing that really got to me was White Knight's cost. We knew that Craw Wurm was good. We knew that the one Serra Angel our play group owned was even better. Craw Wurm had 10 power and toughness for six mana... its pedigree came mostly from availability combined with its pure size. Serra Angel was just obscene. She had eight power and toughness for five mana, but added not one, but two special abilities to the mix. Serra Angel registered as "efficient" to our nascent **Magic** minds... but nothing we had seen thus far could have prepared us for White Knight.

 ![](https://i.ormos.uk/ca/05/ca05f9bc0cef3a7e31f8b1f519fd0ab823d3921ea593af24e171485a4084cb52.jpg) 

Here was a creature that was 2/2 for two mana. On its face, it was purely more efficient than any of the creatures we were used to playing in our decks. Sure, we had seen Grizzly Bears, but that guy was far too small when we had the opportunity to run Craw Wurms; in any case, Grizzly Bears was totally outclassed by White Knight. We had seen Elvish Archers - a rare creature even - and we thought he was good. Elvish Archers was a joke compared to White Knight. For the same total mana, White Knight packed just as much punch, had just as much first strike on his side and doubled the toughness… as well as complete immunity to one of our format's most defining creature elimination spells (it took us a while longer and much wiffle-waffling to figure out his interaction with the other, being Pestilence).

I saw White Knight and it was good.

White Knight was not just the most efficient creature I had, at that point, encountered; it was my teacher. Before White Knight, we looked for size, sometimes for speed, possibly for interesting long game abilities. We looked for killer combinations, like Ley Druid and Wild Growth, like Lord of the Pit and any number of pathetic sticks we might sacrifice to him, like Kird Apes and forest (you would be surprised, after our initial elation at this third combination, how long it took for us to realize that Kird Apes was mediocre in non-green-red decks). After White Knight, we learned that creatures could be self-contained packages of destruction, that they could define the board all by themselves, that they could do wonderful things on turn two.

 ![White Knight](https://i.ormos.uk/c8/7a/c87af6cd8c448c6c86d2497eb18ec6cdd8215aea3c0bb0171303859c00505e82.jpg) 

As I said, I just *had* to trade for White Knight. But my friend's little brother wouldn't budge. He knew how good this creature was as well. He planned to beat our faces in with it. What did I have that was worth the fastest, most efficient, beater we had yet seen? A Savannah? Yeah right. What a waste of a rare slot.

These days, when a player opens a *Legions* pack and sees White Knight, I doubt he will experience quite the life-changing event that I did when meeting this creature for the first time. But that doesn't mean that White Knight has become any less efficient. In a world where Faceless Butcher has been promoted from *Odyssey* Block Constructed sideboards to some of the Extended environment's most successful main decks, where Smother is being touted as the niftiest pinpoint creature kill spell in some time, protection from black has every bit of the potency it did almost ten years ago when White Knight first started dodging our Terrors. While it might not be quite so good as Wild Mongrel or Nantuko Shade in the two-power-creature-for-two-mana category, White Knight will undoubtedly fill a role in the resurgence of white weenie decks in Standard. If players are willing to run Patrol Hounds, a two-drop that lacks White Knight's protection from black and requires a card discard to gain first strike, it is likely that this veteran of Tom Champheng's 1996 World Championship deck will once again find a home.
