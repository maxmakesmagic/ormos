Rotations in the core set always have an incredible impact on the face of Magic. Blocks come and go, and we players know that. While our favorite cards from expansions rotate out after a couple of years, we've resigned ourselves to that fact. Basic set rotations, though, come as more of a jolt. They tear out from under us the very foundation we have come to depend on, and – barring previews like those done on [MagictheGathering.com](https://web.archive.org/web/20051130193929/http://wizards.com/default.asp?x=mtgcom) – these shifts come without warning.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/f0/6b/f06bbbcb5989f9a6a41b413dd9943b9a8bdb2313634d48b72296db76edec2e35.gif)When Fourth Edition replaced Revised, we said goodbye to dual lands. When Fifth Edition swept in next, gone were Lightning Bolt and Hypnotic Specter. Sixth Edition marked the last days of Necropotence, and Seventh spelled the end for Armageddon and Dark Ritual. The world of Magic is one of constant change.

Every time these changes come about, however, there are always those who claim that they herald the end of Magic. What is red without Lightning Bolt, they say, or black without Dark Ritual, or white without Armageddon? These sorts of things have been said since the very first rotations in the game, when many thought the changes between Unlimited and Revised heralded the end. "What is Magic without Moxes, Ancestral Recall, and Time Walk?" they asked. From our vantage point today, we can answer them – a much better game.

The changes in Eighth Edition will undoubtedly cause some of the same reactions among many players. They will rant and they will wail, fighting tooth and nail for their favorite cards. But those cards are gone, and others shall replace them. These changes are the sort of things that make Magic the game it is. Players must constantly learn to adapt to new cards, new environments, and new strategies. Sometimes those old standbys upon which we've relied for so long will disappear when we least expect it – and sometimes they'll resurface just the same.

Eighth Edition holds in store a great number of these changes, many of which will have a remarkable impact on the Standard environment. By now everyone should know that Llanowar Elves are gone and Vine Trellis is back, and that Obliterate and Nekrataal have returned, but Static Orb has not. These are significant changes, to be sure, but they are hardly the big news of the set. 

Counterspell is out. So long, Disenchant. Bye bye, Earthquake. Pain lands...we hardly knew you. Now, I know what you're thinking. Has Wizards R&D gone mad? Maybe... but maybe not. These staples leave some pretty big shoes to fill in their absence, but Eighth Edition gives us more than our fair share of exciting reprints to take their place.

Welcome back, Choke. Why hello there, Hammer of Bogardan. Karma... it sure has been a while. Nice to see you, Plow Under. Savannah Lions, even – what sort of world is this? The world of the future, it would seem, as well as the past. While many cards that have been around since the dawn of Magical time may be going away, others that haven't seen the light of day for almost that long are back and ready to shake things up at this year's World Championships.

Yes, Eighth Edition will be legal for Worlds in Berlin this year. As part of the 10th Anniversary celebration, WotC and the DCI are making a special exemption to the usual rotation policy which will allow for players to use Eighth Edition cards in their decks for both the Standard and Extended portions of the competition. What does this mean? Well, with both *Scourge* and Eighth Edition coming in close succession, the Standard environment will be completely turned upside down.

The changes the environment stands to face are tremendous. Just look at the list of tournament staples that will no longer be legal come Worlds and compare them to the cards in the top decks in this year's U.S. Regionals. Almost every major deck type takes a hit. Red-green loses Llanowar Elves, Wake and Psychatog lose Counterspell, and nearly every deck will miss the Ice Age pain lands. The only decks to survive even close to unscathed are g-r and blue-green madness – but the rest of the changes Eighth Edition promises are sure to shake things up even where these stalwart survivors are concerned.

Not convinced? Anyone who remembers the days of Necropotence can tell you the raw power of Karma against monoblack control decks, and nowadays there is no Nevinyrral's Disk to clear the lethal enchantment away. Blue-green madness decks must deal with the resurfacing of Slay and Nekrataal, potent weapons against Wurms, Arrogant and token alike, as well as possible new Ensnaring Bridge decks, given additional punch by the weakening of blue as well as the return of the mighty Hammer of Bogardan. They've also lost a powerful weapon against those black decks, with Compost no longer available in the Eighth Edition arsenal.

What else changes in this brave new world? Well, the loss of Llanowar Elves means green decks no longer have access to eight one casting cost mana accelerators, promising to usher in a new, slower Standard environment. Elves are not the only accelerators leaving the format, however. The Ice Age pain lands are gone as well, replaced with their Invasion Block counterparts. With mana diversity coming at the price of a one turn hiccup – and life totals no longer taking the hit from Karplusan Forests and Adarkar Wastes now and then – it looks like games will be taking longer in upcoming years.

Slow formats mean blue decks reign supreme, doesn't it? Even without Counterspell, blue control decks have thrived in block formats before, like Accelerated Blue in Urza's Block. If you think islands will rule the day when Eighth Edition rotates in, however, think again. Not only is Counterspell gone, but Memory Lapse has gone the way of the dodo as well – meaning all of the currently played base set counters are no more. Granted, Mana Leak has made a comeback to take their place, along with Rewind (which somehow beat out Dismiss in the online vote), but a conditional two-mana counter and a four-mana counter with a mediocre ability can hardly compare to the effectiveness of their predecessors.

If that weren't enough to keep blue in check, Eighth Edition is chock full of anti-control cards in general and anti-blue cards in particular. Defense Grid makes a comeback to keep counter-based control decks in check, while the recursive threat of Hammer of Bogardan promises to give any control strategy without graveyard removal headaches. Choke joins its cousin Boil to make sure playing islands is a dangerous proposition – and with the loss of the Ice Age pain lands, it's going to become considerably harder to find efficient non-island sources of blue mana.

Blue isn't the only color to have the metaphorical hose turned on it full blast. The return of both Slay and Execute means that wurms and elves alike are no longer safe, and even the board dominating Exalted Angel is vulnerable to a swift death at instant speed. As mentioned above, Karma punishes those same decks Mutilate and Mind Sludge reward. Even plains are not safe, as the return of Flashfires bodes ill for their survival. Red mages must face the Wrath of Marit Lage...let's just say not all color hosers are created equal.

So what will the Standard format look like come Worlds? That much is uncertain, but one thing is clear – it won't look anything like it does today. Every deck type takes a major hit, with even the least impacted tier one decks – blue-green madness and r-g beatdown – losing major components of their strategies. The loss of Merfolk Looter costs the madness decks one of their major discard outlets, and g-r loses both Karplusan Forest and Llanowar Elves. With Counterspell and Memory Lapse gone, base blue control decks will have to struggle to survive, while the loss of Duress and Corrupt cuts a leg out from black beatdown and control strategies.

The decks most poised to benefit from the new set seem to be aggressive white weenie and tribal strategies. Between the return of Savannah Lions, *Scourge*'s Silver Knight, and the game-winning Karma to combat black control decks, white weenie seems poised to be a top tier strategy once again. This year's Magic Online Worlds Qualifier – as well as U.S. Regionals and German National Championships – showed the power of the elf deck, while many players have turned to goblin beatdown decks as well. Eighth Edition marks the departure of Engineered Plague, a thorn in the side of the tribal mechanic. With the Plague no longer a threat, will even more tribal strategies emerge?

That's one of the wonderful things about Magic – we never can be sure what will happen next. Sure, things won't be the same without Counterspell, but the same has been true of Lightning Bolt, Hypnotic Specter, Dark Ritual, and Armageddon. The strength of Magic lies in its versatility and robustness. The game's appeal is not based on individual cards, but rather on the complex and elegant framework upon which those cards are built. Players must constantly adapt to new cards and new strategies, reevaluating everything they once thought they knew for certain.

With Eighth Edition on the horizon, there is only one thing that can be certain. It's a brave new world out there. I for one am looking forward to it.
