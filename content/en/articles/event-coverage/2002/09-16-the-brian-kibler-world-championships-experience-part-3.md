After dinner on Thursday night, I headed back to our hotel intent on figuring out what in the world I was going to play for *Odyssey* Block Constructed on Day 3. With an 8-4 record, I had a solid shot at a Top 8 finish, and that was something I certainly had no intention of squandering by playing a bad deck. During our testing at the Mini-Colony, much of my effort was directed toward the Standard format, and my OBC work was primarily focused on trying to find something viable outside of blue-green and monoblack control. We'd tried just about everything, from green-white to white-blue Screech/Speculation to Wake/Stretch to Solitary Confinement, but none of them could consistently put up the same sort of results as blue-green and black. I became rather discouraged by the format, so I more or less shifted responsibility for OBC to Ben and the German contingent.

Thankfully, I was fortunate enough to have quite capable teammates, and as such I was not left without a solid deck to play. I'd playtested enough matches with Ben against his version of black control to know that it was an extremely powerful choice, although I remained concerned about the amount of hate I'd face if I were to play it. The Germans produced an blue-green Quiet Speculation deck with the tools to be competitive both in the mirror and against black control, but which didn't seem particularly well suited for either matchup - that is, its plan against black didn't seem powerful enough to beat truly dedicated black control decks with Mirari and its plan against blue-green didn't seem focused enough to beat the blue-green decks gunning for the mirror. As such, when I sat in my room on Thursday night, I remained entirely oblivious as to what deck I'd be running the following day.

I spent much of Thursday night discussing the format with Ken Krouner and playtesting with Victor van den Broek. All three of us were legitimately in contention for the Top 8, so we had considerable vested interest in making the right deck choice. Ken was primarily asking me for advice on the best version of blue-green to play, and since his starting point was a Millikin-based Grizzly Fate deck, I gave him the German version of the deck, which was quite similar to what he originally presented me with, albeit better tuned. Victor had approached me earlier that day and asked if he could buy a deck from us, as he didn't feel comfortable playing one of the mediocre net decks the Dutch had access to. I'd told him to talk to Ben, who worked something out with him, and so for much of the remainder of the evening, I played various versions of blue-green against Victor playing our monoblack deck.

I got destroyed. Over and over and over again, I simply could not win. Sure, I squeaked out a game here and there, but by and large I got completely destroyed by the sheer power of the cards in the black control deck. Even with maindeck Envelops and Grizzly Fates, Catalyst Stones and Quiet Speculations, I got completely demolished game after game after game. Victor seemed convinced our deck was worth a cut of his winnings, but more importantly I decided that the deck was worth staking my own finish on. As terrified as I was of running into decks designed to do nothing but beat black control, I decided that if I was going to put my trust in anyone in the **Magic** community about the ability of a deck to win, it was going to be Ben Rubin. Black control it was.

I went to sleep that night later than I might've liked and - to be perfectly honest - far more nervous than I was comfortable with. I'd had a number of top finishes over the 2001-2002 season, with a Pro Tour Top 16 and three straight Grand Prix Top 8s, but my last - and only - appearance in the elimination rounds of a Pro Tour was Chicago 2000, almost two years prior. I felt like this was my chance for vindication - my chance to prove that I was more than just a pretty face. After that first top finish - after playing under the lights, in front of the cameras and in front of the crowd - I wanted nothing more than to get another chance to show that I'm truly one of the best in the game. What better way to do that than to claim the title of World Champion?

I woke, of course, far earlier than intended, courtesy of my miserable cold. I try to steam my sinuses clear in the shower, and that has some effect, but I still emerge without the ability to breathe effectively out of my nose. So it goes. Once again I join the usual suspects for breakfast while Ben and Eric sleep. Ken Krouner and I play a few test games in the café, as well as a couple more while waiting for the shuttle, and although we end up splitting 2-2, I feel extremely confident that the deck performs so well even against a deck with so much hate directed at it. I'm ready. I get to the site, sleeve up, and help people out with last minute tweaks and sideboard choices while I wait for pairings to be posted. 

### Round 13 - Alex Shvartsman

Alex and I have played once before, way back in the fourth round of Grand Prix Toronto in 1997 - my very first taste of Glory. I beat Alex then by Ray of Commanding his Necratog and attacking with my entire team of Vodalian Illusionists, Man-o'-Wars and Ophidians, breaking the cardinal rule of **Magic** by dealing damage with my card drawing snake for the final point. We'd never run into each other in a tournament since, but now was as good a time as any. As we're shuffling up, I ask Alex what his thoughts are about the format, and he says he's "jumping into the abyss" - that he's either going to 6-0 or 0-6. Despite the fact that I'd currently prefer he 0-6's than 6-0's, I wish him luck, and we begin.

When I play a first turn swamp in game one, Alex says something like "Exactly what I wanted to see!", so I'm somewhat concerned that he's one of those nutcases I was afraid of who built his deck to beat nothing but monoblack. He plays out a Millikin and starts casting Roars and Grizzly Fates and the like, while I remove a creature here and there and resolve a Mirari, but have no other spells to go with it, so I'm quickly run over with a hand full of land. Game two is similarly anticlimactic, as Alex only manages to draw a few lands, one of which I Rancid Earth, and I go off with Mirari while he can do nothing but watch. Game three is far more interesting, and once again I manage to resolve Mirari. Alex gets a Werebear and Squirrel Nest down, and casts Grizzly Fate for four tokens. I double Rancid Earth his Nested land and his only island, and he swings with his bears. The following turn, I double Rancid Earth him again - with threshold this time - killing all of his non-Werebear permanents. I play a Nantuko Shade, and Alex can't even attack with his bear because my Shade is lethal, so he passes the turn and I Edict and attack for the win.

Record 9-4

### Round 14 - Scott Richards

![](http://wizards.com/sideboard/images/worlds02/764.jpg)

This round is a Feature Match, so you can check out the coverage [here](/en/events/2002/worlds02/round-14-brian-kibler-vs-scott-richards). When Scott played a turn one plains and Spurnmage Advocate in game one, I had an enormous urge to ask him if he was joking - in game two when he played Tireless Tribe, I simply couldn't resist. Green-white isn't a bad deck in OBC, but it certainly doesn't have the raw power of either blue-green or monoblack - Tireless Tribe just can't compete with Nantuko Shade or Quiet Speculation, and the big allure to playing white- Glory - doesn't do anything against most of black's removal and simply gets trumped by Wonder against blue-green. Without an incredibly aggressive start for the green-white deck or a truly miserable draw for monoblack, the deck with Mutilate should generally beat the deck with nothing but creatures. Neither of these situations occurs, and I win in two.

Record 10-4

### Round 15 - Brian Davis

![](http://wizards.com/sideboard/images/worlds02/753.jpg)

Tournament reports get much easier to write toward the end if you're in Top 8 contention. Not only do you have the possibility for intentional draws, but you also get a lot more Feature Matches, so you can just say "[look here](/en/events/2002/worlds02/round-15-feature-match-brian-davis-vs-brian-kibler) to see what happened in this match". Brian's draws were unimpressive in both games, and game one showcased the power of Mirari. With Mirari in play, the black deck just plain wins if it gets a turn when it doesn't have to react immediately to some lethal threat - you just start Forking Diabolic Tutors to get another Tutor and whatever spell you want. Once you run out of Tutors - if the game isn't over yet - you can start going for Tainted Pacts. Game two just showed what happens when the blue-green deck draws its madness enablers and no madness cards. Aquamoeba just doesn't cut it against Nantuko Shade, and once again, I win in two. 

Record 11-4

### Round 16 - Zvi Mowshowitz

This match was Featured as well, but didn't get a reporter assigned to it, likely because Zvi and I got deckchecked so the reporters chose other matches to write about, or because I'd just had back-to-back matches on the site and they wanted to give someone else the spotlight. I just sat and listened to my CD player for much of the deck check period, letting [Moby](http://www.moby.com/) calm my nerves while I waited to play for Top 8 - or so I thought. Before the day, I thought I needed to go 4-1-1 to be Top 8, based on the fact that Matt Linde had made it with that record two years prior in Tokyo with even more players, so I sat down across from Zvi thinking I was in a "win and I'm in" situation.

With that in mind, I was quite happy when in game 1 I decimated Zvi's board with Mutilates and Edicts and followed it up with a Haunting Echoes for every damage source in his deck, save the Wonder he had in play and the Nimble Mongoose in his hand. Zvi continued playing even after I killed his creatures, despite the fact that there was no possible way he could actually win the game, and rather than kill him with damage, I decked him with his own Standstills. Game two wasn't much closer, as Zvi kept a land light hand and I destroyed his only island with Rancid Earth. I Mirari another Rancid Earth to destroy all his lands, and then Mutilate, and he saves his Wild Mongrels by discarding his entire hand, but I have a second Mutilate to leave him without permanents. Zvi then challenges me to "flawless victory" him, so I chain tutor up Mind Sludge, Haunting Echoes, Faceless Butcher, and Nantuko Shade, and over two turns I force him to discard his entire hand, remove his graveyard from the game, Butcher his discarded Rootwalla, and attack for 20 with the Shade. A fine way to win, methinks.

Record 12-4

### Round 17 - Carlos Romao

After my win over Zvi, I'm presented with a plethora of congratulations on making Top 8, as most people assumed that 12-4-2 would be sufficient to lock it up. It isn't long, however, until my euphoria is shattered by the mathematical prowess of Sol Malka and Zvi Mowshowitz - that is to say, until reality hit, and it hit hard. Apparently not only would 12-4-2 NOT make Top 8, but apparently some of the 13-5's wouldn't make it in because of the way things were going at the top of the standings. Because my tiebreakers were miserable, that meant I had to win the next round to be a lock with a draw. So as pairings go up, once again my name is called to report to the Feature Match area. Just one more...

I'd played Carlos once prior to this match, back in San Diego this year, where we played a blue-white mirror match in which both of us had access to copies of Cultural Exchange. This generally meant that whoever cast Cultural Exchange first would lose, so after winning game one because Carlos was forced to deal with my fliers with his Exchange, and I just took them back with mine, I sideboarded in a Demoralize for game two and just waited until I could definitely kill him with Rites of Refusal backup and go for it. I pulled that one out, and despite the complicated decisions involved in the game, Carlos managed to play at a tremendous pace, so I knew what to expect in this match.

Sure enough, Romao started the match off at lightning speed, both with his play style and with his extremely aggressive draw. I traded removal for a number of quickly thresholded creatures as Carlos curved all the way up to a pair of Krosan Beasts. With the help of Mirari, I Mutilated the Beasts away and played a Shade, which threatened to go lethal on the following turn. Romao played his only out, Bearscape, which was a significant threat given my single-digit life total. I untapped and, as everyone knows by now, said "Echoes you" - despite having access to an enormous amount of mana - rather than "Echoes you, Mirari it". Romao, of course, had the Envelop to win the game, since he was able to chump block my Shade and untap to just start churning out bears. Had I Mirari'd my Echoes, Carlos would've been left with only three basic lands in his graveyard, and in all likelihood my Shade would have overwhelmed him within a turn or two. C'est la vie.

I tried very hard to clear my head for game two, but I was admittedly quite shaken by my mistake. Again, Carlos gets a draw which gives him threshold by turn three or four, turning his Werebears and the like into enormous threats, and plays an early Bearscape to soak up my Edicts and Bloods. I take a lot of early damage, but eventually resolve Mirari. I'm forced to Mutilate and Tutor for a Shade to chump block his bear token for a turn to stay alive until I can take control with Mirari, but Carlos has the Aether Burst to clear my Shade out of the way and allow his bear to attack for the final two points.

Record 14-5

### Round 18 - Steve OMS

Due to rules dispute that required a lengthy appeal to the Head Judge during round seventeen, the final round didn't start for nearly an hour after the clock had run down. The huge lapse certainly gave me far more time than I might've liked to brood about my mistake. I explained what happened to at least a dozen people before I decided I just didn't want to discuss it anymore and just sat alone with my headphones blasting, trying to get my mind focused on my match to come. The way things looked to me, I still had a good shot at Top 8 with a win, so I didn't want to let my slip in the previous round keep me from playing my best.

Pairings went up, and I was slated to play Steve OMS. Steve and I are good friends, and we'd tested together quite a bit in the past. At 13-5-1, he had no shot at the Top 8, while I could still make it with a win. After a long period of soul searching and deliberation, Steve agrees to concede to me. It isn't until after the slip is signed and we're off at a table in the side events area playing for fun that Ed Fear comes over and tells me that I'm going to miss on tiebreakers no matter what happens. If I'd been aware of that prior to the round, there would have been no way I'd have asked Steve to concede, but there was nothing we could do now but wait and hope Ed was wrong.

Sadly, as everyone knows, Ed's math held up, and not only was I not in the Top 8, I was eleventh. Quite the rough beat, but on a happier note, Ken Krouner squeaked in at eighth place. Ken and I had joked earlier in the weekend about how annoying it'd be if we both missed Top 8 again on tiebreakers - at nationals Ken was ninth and I was twelfth. If this pattern keeps up, I hope next time Ken at least manages to finish fifth at the next Pro Tour, so I can be the one to squeak in at eighth seed. 

I make plans to meet up with the Dutch, among others, to spend the night celebrating - or drinking away my sorrows, depending how you look at it - and head out to get dinner with Bob Maher, his wife Courtney, Ed Fear, Neil Reeves, and Steve OMS. We try to get into a nice steakhouse we'd heard about, but we're told we need reservations, and we receive the same response from the Hard Rock Cafe down the street. We give up and eat at the first restaurant we come to, which ends up being pretty good - particularly the caramelized bananas. I go back to the room to find most everyone is still at the site, so I tell Hegstad I'm going to take a nap and to wake me up when people are planning on heading out for the night.

Of course, the next time I wake up is around 5AM the next morning, since Hegstad and co. had decided to nap as well and failed to get their wakeup call. I head over to the site to play in the Australian Open while team day is going on, and Hegstad and I both run Red Zone 2k2 once again. Despite my fears of falling asleep during the constant Psychatog mirror matches, I actually enjoy doing the commentary quite a bit. Control on control matchups may not be the most entertaining to watch for many players, but when you have the exact decklists and the time to analyze every play - and Dave Humphreys CERTAINLY gives us that - they're actually very interesting. I quite honestly don't think I've ever seen a better played game of **Magic** than that which The Hump put forward in the semifinals against Mark Ziegner, where he had his last damage sources in his deck Lobotomized and yet was still able to win by decking Ziegner with Deep Analysis and Cephalid Coliseum. Randy and I commented quite a bit on the unusual mirror strategy the Latin Americans employed, initially rather derisively, but as we watched it keep winning and thought about its implications, it made more and more sense. Congratulations to Carlos Romao and his entire playtest group for figuring out a way to break the most important matchup in the format with strategy, rather than with cards. His is a World Championship well deserved.

Well, that's all I've got - or, at least, all that's fit to print. I had a tremendous time in Sydney, **Magic** and non-**Magic** activities alike, and while I certainly wish things might've turned out a bit differently toward the end, I'm happy with how things went. It was quite the culmination of the Mini-Colony experience, but now I'm back in the "real world" - with school and other such commitments eating away at my time.
