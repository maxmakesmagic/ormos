### 

You want to qualify for Pro-Tour Venice.  
 You are going to play in one or more qualifiers soon.  
 You even did your homework, browsed through the PT-Houston coverage and tested the decks that did well there.  
 You looked at the coverage for Grand Prix-Reims… and couldn’t believe what you saw there.

No Entombs. No Pernicious Deeds. No Oath of Druids. No Aluren.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/da/00/da006311c6569f041965f0a2644b2f79ab91b92669b1173a3faa6ad5510d5c2d.jpg)Jackal Pup, Reckless Charge, Nova Cleric, Ramosian Sergeant, Dauthi Slayer and even Erratic Explosion all made it to the Top 8 instead.

You probably thought for a minute that it was just a very random thing and those people got lucky. Then you looked at the two decks that went 9-0 on Day 1. Two very well-known German pros, Dirk Baberowski and Patrick Mello, both went undefeated with pretty much identical decks – Piledriver Sligh!

But everything was not lost. Bram Snepvangers was at 8-0-1 with the Rock. Raphael Levy was close behind at 8-1 with Aluren. So the decks that did well in Houston were still around. Not so after six more rounds. Bram did finish in the Top 32, but Raphael missed even that.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>After fifteen grueling rounds, not one Rock-, Reanimator- or Oath of Druids-based deck made it to the finals.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Sitting at 13-1-1 after fifteen rounds was Patrick Mello with Goblin Sligh. Alex Mack followed at 12-1-2 with an old school Cursed Scroll-based Sligh. Emmanuel Vernay, Benjamin Caumes and Hans Joachim Hoeh all posted 12-2-1 records with suicide black, Scroll-Sligh and white weenie. Rounding out the Top 8 were Régis Lavoisier (Erratic Explosion – Draco), Christoph Lippert (white weenie with Meddling Mage, Phyrexian Negator and Duress) and Anton Jonsson (Psychatog). The only player to miss the Top 8 with a 12-3 record was France’s own Amiel Tenenbaum who showed up with blue-green madness, of all things.

So after fifteen grueling rounds, not one Rock-, Reanimator- or Oath of Druids-based deck made it to the finals. After three more rounds, the Top 4 decks all summoned creatures on the first turn and attacked with them. Sligh took 1st, 2nd and 3rd, while suicide black came in 4th.

Somewhere in the U.S.A. and England, Dave Price and Dan Paskins separately pumped their fists and laughed at all those fools who chose to run Llanowar Elves over Wall of Blossoms and anti-control cards over Spike Feeder.

And the reason for this Top 8 is not that no one played the decks that did so well in Houston. The only exception for this is Reanimator. Victor van den Broek, Jelger Wiegersma and Nicolas Labarre all played updated versions of Angry Hermit, but other then John Larkin, who missed Day 2 on tiebreakers finishing 66th with a 7-2 record, I saw literally no monoblack or black-blue Reanimator. There were a ton of Oath decks around, though. They got wrecked. The reason for that was the inbred environment of PT-Houston. For the first time in Pro Tour history, most people had the good decks and knew what the field would look like. The amount of graveyard hate was ridiculous and there were very few aggressive decks in the whole field, especially not on Day 2. As a result, a control deck ended up taking the tournament that had the tools to beat other slow decks and everything that won by reanimating something.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/ce/c3/cec312c5a1f5a39c178338e3ae6e91805ffba066ddd7705d27a854ed302bb980.jpg)Justin Gary faced one aggressive green-red deck in the whole tournament. His final record was 10 wins, 3 draws and that one loss. He played the rest of the tournament against favorable matchups and ran the tables. But the Oath deck just isn’t as good as it looks on paper. In the old days of Extended, when your opponent had a Jackal Pup in play and you dropped an Oath of Druids, he tried to kill his creature to prevent the control player from getting Spike Feeder recursion. When a Gary-style Oath deck activates its key card against a goblin deck, the red mage will smile at the Cognivore and proceed to turn his creatures sideways while the huge flier sits there, not blocking the most dangerous creature, Goblin Piledriver. Oath of Druids is just no longer what it was two months ago – a weapon against weenie decks. At least it isn’t in the deck Justin Gary played. Ruud Warmenhoven finished 16th in Houston and 10th in Reims with a blue-green deck that Oathed up Phantom Nishoba and had multiple Cunning Wishes to get Constant Mists. This design sounds a lot more viable to me, but I am still not a huge fan of it. Oath just doesn’t any longer do what it is supposed to do. It is a very good deck against the Rock and mediocre Psychatog designs, but that’s it.

After the 2nd day of PT-Houston was dominated by such slow control decks, people thought that Reims would be a sea of swamps and islands. People were just not prepared for mountains. Gilded Drakes were everywhere and most Rock versions were based on Darwin Kastle’s design. This version is generally good, but not at beating aggressive decks. There are no walls and no Feeders. A full compliment of both Duress and Cabal Therapy won’t get you anywhere against Sligh or Suicide Black. Players who copied those decks from Houston had a rude awakening in Reims. Sligh just walked over them and they weren’t really prepared to deal with the Meddling Mages that the white decks threw at them.

In this series of articles, I am going to cover the ‘new’ decks that emerged at the Grand Prix. The one with the best results is easily Sligh. Coming from nowhere, it dominated the Swiss and the Top 8 in Reims. The three players who brought red cards to the Top 8 tables did not lose a match against non-red mages and dropped only one game to Christoph Lippert’s white deck. That said, the red decks weren’t very similar at all. There were basically two different versions. The more aggressive one is the goblin deck designed by Dirk Baberowski and John Larkin that Patrick Mello finished 3rd with:

![](https://i.ormos.uk/60/99/60995ca50667014ed74d339f622c5a7a1a4fa9f63e26c75632f7bb1ccec2aab3.jpg)This deck is the most offensive version you can find. There are no Wastelands, no Ports. Everything in this deck is a basic mountain or deals damage. There are 25 creatures, 8 spells to accelerate the deck and 9 direct damage spells to get rid of any potential blockers. You can’t really build it any more straightforward then this.

Dirk and Patrick played slightly different sideboards. They key cards you definitely want to run are 4 Cursed Scroll, 4 Ensnaring Bridge and four more lands. Both of them had only three Bridges, but they said it was definitely a mistake. To a certain extent, the board was built around this card. Against both Reanimator and Suicide Black, the plan is to deal some early damage, then drop a Bridge and just Scroll or burn them out. The black deck usually boards in Masticore and Scrolls, but you should be able to race them quite easily, as you have burn to go along with your Scrolls.

The other version is a more control-ish deck that Craig Stevenson used to finish in the Houston Top 32 and Alex Mack used to win the GP:

![](https://i.ormos.uk/59/9c/599cfb7f97e9d74309c4266376785845c03980572c70f511a2cdb0df78a81325.jpg)This is as old school as possible. The deck doesn’t even run Reckless Charge. Ball Lightning was replaced by Blistering Firecat and Volcanic Hammer took Incinerate’s slot. There are 16 good creatures for ![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif) to deal some early damage and four Rishadan Ports and four Wastelands to slow the opponent down. If the initial creature rush can be dealt with, the midgame plan is to either deal the final damage with Blistering Firecat or the various direct damage spells including Cursed Scroll. This version is a lot worse against decks like the Rock, Aluren or Reanimator, as you can’t kill your opponent as fast as the goblin version can. The upside is that the mirror match and Suicide Black are a lot easier. This is pretty much what Dirk’s and Patrick’s deck want to board into. Game 1 against the goblin deck should be very easy if your opponent gets an average draw.

Benjamin Caumes took another approach and went with a mix of those two decks. He lost 2-0 to Alex in the Finals and beat Patrick 2-1 in the Semis.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/36/66/3666d3d80760dbaab290b5d942547cdde11ec2253763efeda760dac33beb0755.jpg)I think this is clearly the wrong way to go. If you want to play the goblin deck, I don’t think you can afford to run Wasteland and Rishadan Port. You have very few spells that cost colorless mana, which effectively makes the Ports and Wastelands spells. I can see running Wastelands if you are very afraid of Aluren or if you think that the Rock runs enough nonbasics to take advantage of that. But if you go with that approach and cut Reckless Charge and Reckless Abandon, the whole goblin plan doesn’t seem that good to me. The idea of the goblin deck is to deal as much damage as possible early on and then burn your opponent out. Without Charge and Abandon, that just doesn’t seem possible to me. Tangle Wire is interesting, but instead of slowing my opponent down to deal some more damage with my creatures, I’d rather have a burn spell that also does something when I don’t happen to have any creatures in play.

The goblin deck has, in my opinion, the most raw power, but it won’t win you any mirrors. Personally, I would never run Sligh. It is not my style of deck. I’ve tried it once and failed miserably. I am not going to touch it again. It is a pretty good deck anyway, just not my style. That said, if I had to pick a version to play, this would be it:

**Sideboard**  
 4 Ensnaring Bridge  
 1 Cursed Scroll  
 2 Rishadan Port  
 3 Fledging Dragon  
 2 Lava Dart  
 3 Pillage

The main deck is pretty much the goblin deck but with Scrolls instead of Abandons and some extra lands to give you a fighting chance in the mirror game 1. The board goes the same direction Baberowski and Mello chose at the GP. Against Reanimator and Suicide Black, the Bridges come in. Against Rock, I’d cut the 2 Cadets, 3 Seals and 2 Lavamancers and put in 2 Ports, 3 Dragons and 2 Darts.

This list might not be optimal though. Hopefully Dan Paskins is going to write an in depth strategy article covering the different red decks (see [http://www.brainburst.com/db/article.asp?id=2582](https://web.archive.org/web/20031218022316/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.brainburst.com/db/article.asp?id=2582&origin=sideboard_strategy_20021209a) for some commentary). He knows a lot better what is good and what is bad in this archetype. I think I am also qualified as I’ve tested a lot against Sligh decks. I was usually sitting on the opposite side of the red mage. Still, that gives me a good feeling for which cards certain decks don’t want to see.
