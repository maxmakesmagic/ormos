Rob Dougherty and I were tossing around ideas for an *Odyssey* Block deck based around the interaction between Malevolent Awakening and some of the Nightmare creatures from *Torment*. I got very excited and said, "It sounds good - let's build it." To which Rob replied, "That's the problem with this format - everything's good!" While Pro Tour-Osaka ended up only having 2-3 dominant archetypes, at that point in our testing it sure felt like everything beat everything else half the time. 

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>This format has so many good choices per slot available that each archetype has its own sub-archetypes.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

I'd like to draw an analogy to the current Standard environment. While not everything beats everything else, we are approaching a super-diverse Regionals. There are many, many good decks out there. Just look at Mike Flores's excellent [rundown](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/03-25-the-decks-to-beat-developing-your-playtest-gauntlet-for-regionals) of some of the current decks to beat to get a small taste. And then realize that Flores's list is by no means comprehensive (nor could it be) with the ridiculous amount of choices in this format. Within each of the archetypes there are different and, more importantly, good options for each slot. In red-green do you build the super fast burn-fest Frog in a Blender or the more resilient creature heavy version like the decks Steve O and Darwin Kastle piloted in the Masters? Do you play an updated version of Ryan Fuller's counter-heavy Psychatog with only 4 'Togs as the kill, or do you play Dave Humpherys's more combo oriented Finkel/Familiar/Upheaval build? Do you run Opt, Sleight of Hand, Peek or Obsessive Search? Why not run all four and play the Battle of Wits deck? This format has so many good choices per slot available that each archetype has its own sub-archetypes.

For all you list makers out there here's a quick roll call of what people are playing or have been playing over the last few months. 

For beatdown players you've got r-g with or without Frogs, Monoblack Kibler Style, or u-g Madness. 

For traditional control players you've got u-b 'Tog, u-w-g Enforcer-Go, u-w Millstone, b-u-g, u-g-r Snake Tongue or u-w-b Finkula. 

For control players who don't necessarily want to use a lot of permission you've got w-b with or without Angel, Domain, or Monoblack Osaka Style.

For combo players you've got Balancing Tings, Upheaval, Rice Snack or crazy Wild Research fun.

Not all of the above decks are tier one, in fact some are practically dead, but you see what I'm saying. There are a lot of options in this format. Which brings me to the actual point of this article: 

### In a Wide-Open Format, Build Proactively

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>&quot;There are no wrong questions [threats], only wrong answers.&quot; -David Price</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

In a clearly defined metagame it's easy to take aim at certain deck types. But the greater the diversity in the decks you will play against, the less you will be able to plan ahead for them. It all goes back to threat and answer theory. I believe David Price once said, "There are no wrong questions \[threats\], only wrong answers." Rather than trying to stock your decks with the best answers to Psychatog or Frog, instead be ready to play your own threats and force your opponent to react to you. 

When I first started playing competitive **Magic**, I was always scared of what cards and strategies my opponents might use. This led me to gravitate toward playing icky combo decks like Replenish so I wouldn't have to pay attention to what my opponents were doing. A lot of players I know tend to play beatdown in an unknown field. They figure they'll play some men and turn them sideways and their opponents can worry about all that cutesy wutsey "mana in my pool Balancing Act" junk. I'm not trying to advocate beatdown or combo decks in this field, I'm just saying that you should be proactive with individual card decisions. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/2e/46/2e464f6d62d82f155aea57c423d282bbc46762d1e105e3c98b30c2b287b8d908.jpg)

Let me back up and explain what I mean by proactive. Call of the Herd is a proactive card. It has a specific purpose: to bash your opponent. No matter what your opponent is playing, the Elephant is ready to send. Disenchant is a reactive card. It also has a specific purpose. But, like a mama's boy not allowed to play football with his friends, Disenchant will be stuck in your hand if your opponent doesn't play a target for it. If you know the format is full of Static Orbs, Saproling Bursts, and Story Circles, then Disenchant seems like a wonderful candidate for your main deck. But if the only enchantment for miles is Pernicious Deed and the best artifacts in the format are Eggs, then maybe Disenchant should warm the bench this season. These first examples may seem perfectly clear, but with certain cards and archetypes the water's a little muddy. 

Let's do a little case study. One of my favorite players, Brett "Chris Tucker Head Jiggle" Shears, played a version of b-u-g or the Kowal deck to a 1st-place finish at Grand Prix-Denver. While similar to other color combinations of control decks at the time, Shears's deck had an ace in the hole in the form of Pernicious Deed. Goblin Trenches, Collective Restraints, and non-regenerating creatures that got in under his permission all died to the super board sweeper. B-u-g decks became reasonably popular in the pre-*Odyssey* Standard mostly because of the Deed. Some of the best decks were Counter-Rebel, Merfolk Opposition, Fires of Yavimaya, and Spontaneous Generation - all decks with tons of permanents. Chim Chiminey - what a wonderful time to be a sweeper! And after *Odyssey* came out b-u-g players the world over rejoiced at the thought of adding Finkels to the mix.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/49/96/4996b657b30ecacff0c75dce95da832a1220d13fdd57272918be747c553f7fdd.jpg)

So why hasn't b-u-g been that popular lately? The answer is that right now in the post-*Odyssey* Standard, Pernicious Deed just isn't that exciting. It's still fine in and of itself and excellent against beatdown decks. If the metagame was unknown, the Deed would be a nice safety net in that it can handle most any dangerous permanent. But the metagame is not unknown. It may be wide open, but we pretty much know what's out there. We know that many of the main archetypes are built around lots of instants and few permanents and there are about as many enchantments and artifacts floating around. So while the Deed will never be a dead card, you have to ask yourself if it's worth taking a perfectly good u-b control deck and risking the mana base for what may amount to being a slow, situational, one for one removal spell. Sure sometimes the Deed is outstanding. But is sometimes enough? 

I would argue that while Psychatog in its various forms is a proactive deck, b-u-g is a more reactive deck because its power level ebbs and flows according to how good the Deed is in the relevant format. And make no mistake: Pernicious Deed is the only reason to play b-u-g. Spiritmonger is really cute and if both players could activate his abilities his name would make more sense, but you're not splashing green just for a five casting cost sorcery speed dude. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/38/1b/381b73ad207922372b69ba27a61ea286a3934cd8d159e20c014aec026805566c.jpg)

You might argue that Psychatog is a reactive deck in that it spends a lot of time sitting back and countering stuff essentially reacting to its opponent. However, I'm trying to define "reactive" not as a play style but as a design style. The 'Tog deck is designed proactively. The game plan is you counter their stuff (and unless they're playing an updated version of Dan Bock's all land deck, they're going to have stuff), you draw a million cards through cantrips and Fact or Fiction, you play a 'Tog and attack for whole big bunches. Whether playing the Finkel or Upheaval versions or the all 'Tog all the time version, each individual card in the deck should help you toward the goal of taking control of the game. The closest thing to a reactive card in the tog deck would be the bounce spells. Repulse and/or Aether Burst assume that there will be opposing creatures (a safe bet in this environment), and they are so cheap and efficient that they are worth the risk that you might find yourself facing some b-r-u creatureless deck or Kibler's anti-bounce monoblack. While the b-u-g deck's plan is essentially the same as the 'Tog's, the fact that the Deed is slower, potentially non-synergistic, not a super-star in this format, and involves 2 non-base colors scares me away from the deck.

I realize that I've spent an awful lot of time being mean to the b-u-g deck and many of you are like "Ok, ok I don't have the cards to build it anyway. But what SHOULD I play at Regionals?" Well, I could quote Shmi Skywalker and ask, "What does your heart tell you?" But that would probably turn into some cardio- anthropomorphic joke. So instead I'll direct you to the "next time" segment below.
