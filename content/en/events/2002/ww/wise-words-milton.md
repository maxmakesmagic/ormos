I am a jaded man. It’s my job to play games, traveling around the world to do so, then draw from personal experience in writing just about anything I please in what amounts to far less than forty hours a week. My working weekends take place in the dregs: Venice, Chicago, Yokohama… I’ve been around the planet a few times now, traveling so much that what used to be the enthused kid who clung to the window as the world shrank in front of him is now the desensitized man who reads until looking up and noticing we’ve taken off.

Jaded. 100%. You could even call me curmudgeonly. 

When I head to Pro Tours, I try to be friendly and sociable until I sit down at the table. Even then, I’m often playing against friends and acquaintances, so I try to keep things on the level, but there’s always mind games going on: looking for hints about what they’re playing, looking for advantages even when they’re unlikely to be there, trying to outthink the opposition in every possible way… **Magic’s** a great game, but when you play like that, the purity, the premium of fun, is taken out of it.

Fortunately, as this past weekend bore witness to, all is not lost. It’s been a while since I’ve written about my friends in Milton. Such titans as Mike Croft, Jay Robar, Graham Pocock and Boyd Hardie who, while each having their respective moments in the sun, never left an indelible mark on the Pro Tour because their **Magic** is what mine is not: a game. Something to do with spare time. An opportunity to spend a weekend afternoon with friends, cracking jokes, telling stories, feasting at an all-you-can-eat Chinese buffet and trying to defeat it. For these guys, **Magic** is the fuel instead of the vehicle and so be it. I guess if the game hasn’t jaded you, that’s the way it’s supposed to be.

You aren’t going to find too much in the way of technical jargon in this week’s Wise Words. As a matter of fact, I went 1-4 in sanctioned matches this weekend, with the one being a bye in the 0-2 bracket of Sunday’s sealed deck. The thing is though, this weekend reminded me of a few things I’d been wanting to mention in this space for some time, and as it is my space…

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>Interestingly, in a poll taken at www.mtgontario.com, readers felt Kai Budde had a slight edge over Toronto as favorite.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

The primary reason for the trip out to Milton was for Saturday’s Ontario City Challenge. Basically, the idea was that three man teams representing assorted townships throughout the province would show up and compete in a single-elimination Team Rochester tourney with the winners taking home all opened cards, a few foils and glory for their home city. Interestingly, in a poll taken at [www.mtgontario.com](https://web.archive.org/web/20030418000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.mtgontario.com&origin=sideboard_ww_20030418), readers felt Kai Budde had a slight edge over Toronto as favorite. My guess is that most of the voters were Americans: they’re the only ones I can see thinking Germany is a Canadian city.

Despite experts, Toronto managed to pull it out. Gab Tsang, Elijah Pollock and ABSOLUTELY, POSITIVELY NON-GRAVY TRAINER Rich Hoaen took the tournament in dramatic fashion, while my makeshift Team Thornhill (along with Steve Wolfman and Mark Zajdner) bowed out in the first round to eventual finalists Sarnia, featuring one of **Magic’s** clearest voices, Geordie Tait. Geordie and I had met briefly in Detroit a few years ago, but it was before he’d started really putting pen to paper and was only a passing conversation, so this was an opportunity I’d been looking forward to for a long time.

Geordie, a featured writer for StarCityGames.com who a few months back took it upon himself to write thousands of words each and every day, was definitely not quite what I’d expected. His writing has a sarcastic edge to it that made me think he’d have some attitude, the kind you might find in late night ***Magic** Online* drafts when your 1800-rated opponent is mana screwed. Saturday night, when tradition dictated we must find all-you-can-eat something-or-other, Tait piled into my car and we had what turned out to be a pretty thought-provoking conversation.

We talked about writing for a while and little about life in general, but the conversation inevitably turned towards the Pro Tour. We talked a little about the play, but mostly the conversation geared itself towards the perceptions people had towards the Tour and **Magic** as a whole.

It’s frustrating that people who don’t know the game don’t understand what it is we do. In a society ruled by the popular label and more popular media, unless the general public is told **Magic** is what’s hot, there’s always going to be a rather large faction who’s either never heard of it or worse yet, think of it as “that **D&D** card game” (I hate that *so* much). Time and again, my family will ask “what is it exactly that you do?” and when I try to answer, they stare blankly as if there were a couple grand stapled to the wall behind me, as if drool was at $63/share on the Nasdaq. The simple fact is that we aren’t mainstream and that mainstream is mainstream because they like being mainstream. That means that those who just don’t know have to be let off pretty easy.

However, as flaccid as our angst towards them was, when the conversation turned to back-biting gamers who try to tear the Pro Tour down, fury permeated from the car. A couple of weeks ago, in the wake of his Semifinals finish in Venice, I wrote that Bill Jensen may well be the next challenger to the throne. One site that I usually respect as funny and intelligent despite being a frequent target of their barbs posted an article obviously meant as a joke that contained the following:

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/usnat02/876.jpg)

“Billy used to come down and play basketball with us every Friday, and we’d go to parties and stuff together, but now he just sits at his computer,” said friend Ryan Johnson. “A couple weeks ago, the neighbors called the cops to his room because they thought they could smell a rotting corpse, but it turned out that Will just hadn’t showered for a week because he was too busy ‘testing Slide’ or some (expletive deleted) like that, it’s really sad.”

Now here’s the thing. Yes, it’s a joke. Yes, it’s obviously meant to be so. Bill Jensen is a social being with friends, showers and a life, but the sad and simple truth is that as long as players are going to be presented this way, people are going to be looking at **Magic** in the way they always have: the game of the great unwashed dregs. One thing that struck me about Geordie was the respect he had for the Pro Tour as a whole. For him, the achievement of even qualifying for a Pro Tour is the equivalent of playing your first big league baseball game or international football match. It’s the pinnacle of achievement in a game that any competitive player worth their boiled blood wants to excel at. Instead of being looked at in that way though, making the Pro Tour almost seems to suggest you’re worse off for the experience if the community’s word were bond.

*“Pro players cheat.”  
 “They don’t bathe.”  
 “They all smell.”  
 “They’re all rules lawyers.”*

It’s crap. All of it. We can’t be grouped into one demographic. There are girlfriends and wives and friends and lives and practitioners of good sportsmanship and honesty and good times. I hate to break it to you folks, but every time one of you says any of the above crap, you should really be looking at yourself and wondering why it is you feel the need to dump on people who, frankly, you don’t know from Barry Bonds or David Beckham. All you’re doing is voicing your own feelings of inadequacy and if it isn’t obvious by now, I for one and sick of your crap. You don’t have one iota of an idea what you’re talking about.

No, Pro Tour **Magic** is not Major League Baseball. I know, the number of **Magic** players in the world is in the seven digit range instead of the nine, meaning reaching that pinnacle is easier and even then, the money isn’t what you’ll find in professional athletics. You know what though? I just finished my tax returns and I had a pretty good year last year considering what I do for a living. Know what else? There are plenty of guys who did a lot better than I did. If you’re good enough and dedicated enough, Pro **Magic** is a viable option. As long as the money is there, you’re going to find that we are too.

My best guess is that the detractors come from one of three schools: 

1.  Players burned by pros in the past. There was a time when cheating was far more blatant and widespread than it is now, and while their view is hardly objective, I can see personal experience getting in the way of objectivity
2.  Players who want to be better. A foray into the subconscious, if you can’t join ‘em, beat ‘em. You try and try to make it to the Pro Tour but find that you can’t, so mise well tear the PT down to anyone who’ll listen so they know your failures aren’t really failures at all.
3.  Ignorance. You read on the internet that the PT is full of cheating losers. Must be true.

I don’t see any of this as reasonable. 

Look, I’m not saying everyone thinks this way. There are players who give the Pro Tour and the accomplishment it represents due respect. To those of you who fall into those three above categories, stop. As long as you keep telling people what crap the Pro Tour is, when in a roundabout way it represents you, you’re going to have to keep lying to girls about what you’re doing this Saturday and hiding from non-gamer friends all evidence of something you evidently love to do if you’re spending the time, effort and money you are. 

Anyways, after our twenty-one person party ended a Chinese food franchise, we went back to the home of Mike Croft where many of us would be taking up floor space en route to the next day’s sealed deck tourney. After a little Jackass: the Movie and a few games of pool, Boyd announced that his Cube was in attendance.

If you’ve never heard of Cube draft, I heartily recommend it. I have only drafted from four Cubes (a Cube is a self-designed collection of cards from which random handfuls of cards are taken and drafted), and their sheer diversity provides an example of what makes the format such a good one for the competitive player looking for the casual variant. Here are the four I’ve played:

1.  Mark Zajdner’s – a collection of the 350 most powerful cards of all time, each card is present in alpha, beta, black border foreign or foil version, whichever is the most exclusive.
2.  Pat Chapin’s – Pat’s are different each time you see him, because he’s always creating new cards. Chapin creates sets, mostly because the process interests him, so there’s a lot of proxy reading involved.
3.  Ben Bleweiss’s – Ben’s Cube is simple enough: it consists of one of every card ever made, all in one huge box he carts around to premiere events.
4.  Boyd Hardie’s – Boyd’s is a collection of utter crap, two card combos in a 700 card collection, one of every sub-$10 rare he could get his hands on. If you get enough for a deck, you’re lucky.

You shuffle them up, get three ‘packs’ of fifteen and draft like you were in the Top 8 of a PTQ. The thing is though, you never play for anything more than a dollar and usually go for less. You draft fun, combo-heavy decks because you aren’t playing for anything. You peek at neighboring decks, you trash talk as if you were a main character in *White Men Can’t Jump* and do whatever it takes to make the game fun. Sure, winning is nice. Zajdner’s group even keeps a record of wins and losses for their end of season Invitational tournament. You do what you have to, but it’s good times, along the same lines as Five-Color, but without the threat of losing your cards or damaging them beyond repair. Try throwing together a Cube of your own design, grab a few friends and give it a run. If you don’t have the cash, make it all commons. Sometimes, even the pros need a break from the rigors of playtesting and metagaming. This is a great way to do it.

The weekend went on, the sealed tourney Sunday went off without a hitch and after another session of fine dining, I was driving back to Toronto. Seems like every time I head to Milton, I leave with a renewed appreciation for the reasons I got into the game in the first place and what keeps me coming back. I don’t like playing in smaller tourneys much because I don’t want to anger everyone with the PT game face, nor do I want to perform half-heartedly and lose to players I know I should beat, but the times to be had in that small town outside Toronto, the chats, the stories, the reminiscences and yes, even the gaming, make it all worthwhile. Good times are a good thing. You have to savor them while you can.

    I mentioned in last week’s column that Antonino DeRosa was the designer of the beast deck Jordan Berkowitz played in Venice. In actuality, the deck’s designer was Roger Sorino, who’s been putting up consistent but unspectacular numbers for years, the kind of numbers that fail to get recognition in the Top-8-or-bust mentality of the Pro Tour. Gotta’ give the credit where it’s due, especially to a guy whose been fighting the good fight for years.
