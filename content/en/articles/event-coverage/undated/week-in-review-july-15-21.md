### Grand Prix-Sao Paulo and Taipei

Although relatively few pro players have made a journey to these last two Grand Prix of the season, the whole world was watching what they - and the eventual winners - were going to be playing. Standard with Judgment is a format that was not previously seen at high-level tournament play, and it is the format of the World Championships - so pros are not writing or talking about it yet. Over the course of the weekend, players had to decide between decreasing their chances at doing well in a Grand Prix tournament, or revealing their best decks to the world. Considering that the likes of Mike Pustilnik and the Ruel brothers spent hundreds of dollars on the trip, it is likely that they brought their best decks.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/41/ec/41eca202de4e516b06754ce34fd4d4dcf551260fd3dedaf603f6585fafc3bbf2.jpg)

Pustilnik's deck was among the most interesting. He ran a fairly straightforward Psychatog variant featuring three copies of Cunning Wish. His sideboard however, was something else entirely. He had thirteen one-of spells and a pair of Gainsays to maximize the power of his Wishes. Sideboard cards ranged from something always worth Wishing for - such as Fact or Fiction - along with cards that could be good in very specific situations, such as Aura Graft or Spelljack. Pustilnik placed 19th.

The only other American to travel to Sao Paulo was Steve O'Mahoney Schwartz, who played a red-blue-green Opposition deck utilizing one of the very best creatures in the new set - Phantom Centaur. OMS did not bother with Static Orb at all - not even in his sideboard. Instead, he used four Squirrel Nests to fuel it. OMS placed 11th overall. GP-Rio champion Carlos Romao played a very different version of Opposition. In fact, it was more of a hybrid between Opposition and a Liquid Tempo deck. He placed 18th.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/b2/a5/b2a57a434eedc1f8327146a2ee5cafe25dc912e1dcfb59106a23e50620914394.jpg)

United States was not the best-represented country from outside of Latin America at this tournament - France was. Ruel brothers were joined by Florent Jeudon in traveling to this event. They initially planned on coming to GP-New Jersey but could not find cheap airfare. In the process, they managed to get a good deal on a flight to Brazil. Olivier Ruel and Florent Jeudon both missed Day 2, but Antoine Ruel was on fire, finishing the Swiss rounds in the first place. Ruel played a Psychatog variant splashing green for Pernicious Deed and Seedtime in the sideboard. Antoine Ruel was the only player from outside the region to make the Top 8 and finished in the Top 4 overall.

It was Argentina's Gabriel Caligaris who took the top prize, playing a Psychatog deck very similar to that of Pustilnik, Caligaris's sideboard also featured eight one-of's featuring everything you could possibly wish for (the pun very much intended). He defeated a blue-green Liquid Tempo strategy navigated by Felipe Desiderati in the finals.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/59/e0/59e010077c0e8fa7f0e5fc1dc8297758e0beb50efca323296010c0d903e63348.jpg)

Meanwhile, at the other end of the world, players from around Asia met in Taipei to play the same format - but with very different results. No Americans on Europeans there - but plenty of the top Japanese players made the trip. For the first time however, none placed in the Top 8. Plenty of the well-known Japanese players finished in the money, but Taiwanese managed to rally this time, earning seven of the eight slots, with Albertus Law of Singapore being the only foreign player to make the cut to the single elimination rounds. Law and his teammates played a Psychatog deck splashing for Burning Wish instead of the Cunning Wish utilized in several successful decks in South America. The sideboard featured powerful sorceries such as Upheaval, Obliterate, and Haunting Echoes instead of the many fine instants.

The most interesting thing about GP-Taipei this year happened to be the deck that won it. Sheng Hsun Hsia built a blue-green-white control deck combining Living Wish with Hunting Grounds for some impressive results. Although the deck looks terrible on paper, it plays quite well, Sheng doubly earning the title both for playing well and being able and willing to show up to the major tournament with a rogue deck. 

### Worlds Preparation

There are no more major tournaments before pros will be spending a week in Sydney. We've entered the final stretch, so it is all about preparation right now, and as ever preparation is about politics and alliances.

![](http://www.wizards.com/tournaments/images/W_SM_1.jpg)

The biggest story of this testing "season" is the breakup of team Godzilla. Rather sudden, it saw some of the game's top names reshuffled into new teams. Kai Budde and Patrick Mello joined with other German players Christoph Lippert and Felix Schneiders and Austrian players Helmut Summersberger and Benedikt Klauser to form a German-speaking team. It was not long however until they re-united with Godzilla teammate Eric Froehlich and his new comrades Brian Kibler, Ben Rubin, and Brian Hegstad. This new hybrid of European Alliance and The Jumble has plenty of star power, but it remains to be seen how well the two groups will work together. There is certainly a high potential for impressive finishes, regardless of just how much the group is able to break the format.

The British announced their intention to test locally, perhaps reviving team HCP - but I have no details on their group as of the time this article is being written. Zvi Mowshowitz joined Your Move Games, replacing Dave Humpherys (who has no time to play **Magic** at the moment but will certainly be back in time to practice for PT-Boston). For the first time ever, Your Move Games has gone long-distance. With Justin Gary having graduated college and moved back to Florida and having picked up Zvi and myself in New York City, folks like Rob Dougherty are finally forced to learn the pleasures of playing on Apprentice. All of us are looking forward to building up our collections on Magic Online quickly, so that testing can be done via that software instead.

Not to be left behind, the Dutch set up a **Magic** Colony of their own. Several pros moved into Jeroen Remie's house to test for the event. Included are the reigning World Champion Tom Van de Logt, Kamiel Cornelissen, Noah Boeken (that's the whole National Team), Jan Doise, Victor van der Broek, Alexander Witt, Frank Karsten and Arjan van Leeuwen. This is the most organized effort put forth by the Dutch - a country already known for outperforming larger nations at **Magic** - and is certainly worth watching. One also cannot discount The Punisher - a team of Swedish and Finnish players that has been doing quite well since its inception about a year ago.

*How is it possible to win on turn 1 in current Extended format by casting only artifacts, red, and black spells?*

Play Badlands. Cast two Mox Diamonds throwing away any two lands. Cast Entomb to put Worldgorger Dragon into your graveyard. Cast Animate Dead on the Dragon. It comes into play and forces all permanents, including Animate Dead, to leave the game. As Animate Dead "falls off," Dragon goes back to the graveyard and your land comes into play untapped. Animate Dead comes into play and targets Worldgorger Dragon. Tap your Badlands for mana. Keep doing this until you generate enough mana to win the game with Ghitu Fire or another instant-speed burn spell.

### Good (?) Play of the Week

*Courtesy of Alex Werner:*

"I'm not sure if this qualifies as a good or a bad play, since nothing actually happened, but it was an interesting situation. 

I was playing in a draft on **Magic** Online playing green-X against black-X. Relatively late in a somewhat balanced game, I tapped 7 land during my opponent's end step, untapped one with Krosan Restorer, tapped it for an eighth mana, and flashed back Elephant Ambush. Only after doing so did I realize that my opponent had Crypt Creeper in play and could have removed the Ambush in response to me activating Krosan Restorer, which would have denied me an Elephant and forced me to burn for 7. ### Bad Play of the Week #2

![](https://i.ormos.uk/75/8c/758c526179bdda5f4b9fa194fc65a1226424fd44fd934e2ebfda8d3a342e0601.jpg)

Since the Bad Play listed above was short (but sweet), here is a bonus bad play from Pro Tour-Paris, ages ago. Dave Humpherys had two cards in hand - Foreshadow and Mangara's Blessing, when his opponent cast Coercion. Obviously, the opponent would take Foreshadow or a card Humpherys might have drawn off it instead of Mangara's Blessing, which would just return back to his hand at the end of turn. Humpherys cast Foreshadow, so that he could get a card off it at the beginning of next turn. He named... swamp! The most common card in his opponent's deck. The card flipped over was, indeed, a swamp - so when Coercion resolved, his opponent took out the other card rather than allow Humpherys to gain two life points for free. He would have been better served naming Black Lotus!
