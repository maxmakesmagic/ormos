Hello again! It's me, your friendly neighborhood **Magic** designer! You might remember me from my [recent *Guildpact* feature article](/en/articles/feature/2006/02-06-designing-guildpact) on how we used the flavor of the Gruul, Izzet, and Orzhov to design their mechanics. Since then, they've kept me working on **Magic** round the clock, co-designing *Coldsnap* and *Time Spiral*, while co-developing...*Coldsnap* and *Time Spiral*. My most recent nefarious scheme has been leading the development team for *Planar Chaos*. But now I'm here to give you the inside scoop on the design of **Magic's** snowiest, rippliest, recoveryest, most cumulative expansion: *Coldsnap*. 

As a player, I love building decks that tutor for a “toolbox” of carefully selected single cards, giving me the power to deal with any situation. That's part of why my favorite *Coldsnap* cards I have designed are Zur the Enchanter and Herald of Leshrac. And that's part of why my favorite *Coldsnap* cards I *didn't* design are Hibernation's End and Braid of Fire. You'll notice that two of those four cards are tutor engines. But *three* of my favorite *Coldsnap* cards share something entirely different. Yup, it's one of the hardest to evaluate, most mysterious, and worst-named keywords in all **Magic** history: Cumulative Upkeep.

*Ice Age* used cumulative upkeep mostly as a time limit on bizarre rules-changing enchantments like Illusionary Terrain and Energy Storm. They wanted to make sure that those rules changes wouldn't last the whole game. Fully 18 of *Ice Age*'s 30 cumulative upkeep cards were enchantments! *Alliances* took the mechanic a step further with the Battle-of-Wits-ish Thought Lash. *Alliances* cumulative upkeep also featured what I consider one of the most impressive, beautiful card designs of all time: Varchild's War-Riders. *Weatherlight* really raised the cumulative upkeep bar, with straightforward hell-raisers like Morinfen, clever interactions like Ancestral Knowledge, and bizarre cumulative costs like “Put a -1/-1 counter on Aboroth.”

But in our *Ice Age* design meetings, Bill Rose, Mark Rosewater, Aaron Forsythe and I knew that cumulative upkeep had never really hit its full potential. For one thing, those weird *Ice Age* cumulative upkeep enchantments weren't very useful at all in your everyday Limited or kitchen table decks. And almost every time cumulative upkeep showed up in tournaments was in the form of annoying cards that said “The opponent doesn't get to play this game,” like Infernal Darkness, Glacial Chasm, or the dominating Illusions of Grandeur (I'm serious - ask your friends!). For *Coldsnap* cumulative upkeep, we were determined to hit three goals: 1) Use simple, powerful cards to create truly interesting cumulative upkeep choices in Limited, 2) Provide compelling, interactive cumulative upkeep options for Constructed, and 3) Get *seriously* wacky. Here's how we did it.

### Cumulative Simplicity

This is the simplest kind of cumulative upkeep: A powerful effect at an attractive mana cost, balanced by adding cumulative upkeep. These cards' abilities don't fundamentally depend on having the keyword. You'll notice this is the smallest category – we wanted to lay out a basic foundation, then quickly advance to more innovation. In Limited games, you'll learn to time your cumulative upkeep cards carefully to have maximum impact without tying up your mana at the wrong moment.

**Ronom Hulk**  
 Vastly bigger than most protection creatures, look for this humble common to “Hulk Smash” his way past almost every single creature in *Coldsnap*. Usual anti-fatty defenses like Ronom Serpent, Rimebound Dead, Gelid Shackles and Zombie Musher just sit there and watch while the Hulk runs right past ‘em.

![phobian_phantasm](http://www.wizards.com/magic/images/cardart/CSP/phobian_phantasm.jpg)**Phobian Phantasm**  
 We almost never do creatures with two evasion abilities. Once you have the one, what's the point of the other? Just think about it: how often have you seen a creature with flying and forestwalk? The only exception is when we give some giant Demon Flying and Trample at the same time. (Or Akroma!) To justify cumulative upkeep on this card, we decided to make something that could get through no matter what. People in R&D sometimes joked about how ridiculous a Flying Fear guy would be, but we sure taught them not to make those jokes around us…by deciding to do one!

**Survivor of the Unseen**  
 First of all, this wizard has a trick that makes her better than you think: On the final turn before you let her go, you can put the cumulative upkeep on the stack, then use her tap ability before the cumulative upkeep resolves, getting one final card draw. Second, one question with cumulative upkeep is always “Should I pay or not? What if I draw something I want to cast?” If you use her ability to put a card onto your library while cumulative upkeep is on the stack, you'll always know what's coming up, and you can make a better decision about whether or not to pay it. Finally, it's always annoying to have two of the same Ripple card in your hand. You can't Ripple it out of the deck for free if it's already in your hand! Survivor can put your your *second* Surging Aether on top of the deck just in time for you to cast your *first* Surging Aether. You can guarantee a free Ripple, then who knows how many after that.

### Cumulative Power

One of the threats to cumulative upkeep is that it's a drawback. In *Coldsnap* design, we started by asking “How can we get excited about a keyword that's a negative?” The answer? When it's actually a positive! *Coldsnap* features a variety of cumulative upkeep cards that get stronger and stronger as you pay for them, rewarding you for keeping them around with increasingly powerful abilities.

**Earthen Goo & Balduvian Fallen**  
 Many old-school cumulative upkeep creatures like Illusionary Presence give the feeling of getting more and more clunky and burdensome over time. This Ooze and Zombie duo feel like the exact opposite – instead of getting clunkier over time, they just hit harder and harder. When you cast Illusionary Presence, your opponent avoids killing it, hoping you'll have to give it up eventually, or spend all your mana to deal just 2 a turn. When you play the Goo or Fallen, your opponent has to scramble to kill it as fast as possible, before it grows too large to deal with at all. Earthen Goo is a lot better *with* cumulative upkeep than the Gray Ogre he would be without it!

**Glacial Plating**  
 The question here was simple: What numbers do you have to write on an Enchant Creature to make it appealing if it does nothing the turn you play it? Answer: How about +3/+3, +6/+6, and +9/+9? The more cumulative this upkeep gets, the better. If you draft a ton of snow-covered lands, cast this on Frost Raptor or Zombie Musher to make an unblockable, unkillable nightmare.

**Magmatic Core**  
 Designed at ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif), Magmatic Core went steadily up in cost as playtesting revealed that this is less like an upkeep cost and more like “Pyrotechnics your team. Every turn of the game. For more damage and more damage and more damage...”

![](https://i.ormos.uk/8a/a0/8aa0246babe7427a4f3fbe7a42c8d1244f78c4fb297c55ac0baf017f3a8cd32f.jpg)**Hibernation's End**  
 This enchantment's cumulative upkeep basically says “Hello, I'm a ![4 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/36/29/3629288966daa042decf01d3752789ab6676fddd67c3679fa580cd3baecae158.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif) Enchantment. Instead of paying upkeep, just select and cast a creature of your choice out of your deck every single turn. Yup, go ahead and tutor for six creatures in a row with just one card.” What a sick one. Part of what I love about this card, in both Limited and Constructed, is the way it makes you go through your deck trying to make sure you have enough creatures at every mana cost to wake from hibernation. How high do you go? In Constructed, I love how it makes you ask: “If I could assemble a perfect combination *A-Team* of any 1-mana guy, 2-mana guy, 3-mana guy, 4-mana guy, 5-mana guy, and 6-mana guy, getting them all in a row with clockwork consistency, what would I pick?”

**Kjeldoran Javelineer**  
 Cumulative upkeep here enables that bizarre animal, the one-mana creature you won't play on turn 1. Instead, her single mana cost lets you deploy this underrated soldier on the same turn as a four-drop somewhere in the midgame, where the Javelineer's tap ability will quickly dominate the battlefield. A Heavy Ballista for X damage at common? Sign me up.

**Freyalise's Radiance**  
 Development didn't want a severe snow hoser in *Coldsnap*. (You know, like, um, the card Cold Snap.) But they wanted a little something to fight back against your local Yetis, in the old-school *Ice Age* vein of global enchantment cumulative upkeep.

**Cover of Winter**  
 With all these cumulative upkeep cards getting more powerful the more times you pay their upkeep, you may finding yourself asking “I wish I could skip ahead to my fourth upkeep right now. Go Go Earthen Goo!” So we decided to make a card where you can. Cover of Winter stops a whole army of Kird Apes and Scab-Clan Maulers dead in their tracks. Unlike other defensive white enchantments like Worship and Pariah, Cover of Winter protects your creatures as much as it protects you, letting you send your army of Savannah Lions fearlessly into enemy Rumbling Slums and laughing off their 5-power fists. Best of all, you can keep your upkeep payments low until you actually need the protection. I can remember people yelling “Shields up!” and throwing a bunch of pennies onto the Cover when the attack phase got hairy.

### Cumulative Payback

These cards are the pension plans of **Magic**. They let you invest mana now for a reward that matures over time and pays you back later. The only difference is that real-life pension plans don't let you beat down with a 6/6 Trampling Cat Warrior while you're waiting.

![arctic_nishoba](http://www.wizards.com/magic/images/cardart/CSP/arctic_nishoba.jpg)**Arctic Nishoba**  
 This creature accumulates counters over time and pays you back in life, in much the same fashion as Icatian Moneychanger. Except Icatian Moneychanger is an 0/2.

**Jotun Owl Keeper**  
 Like Anger, The Owl Keeper is one of the few creatures in **Magic** whose offensive potential can actually *increase* when it dies. Best of all, you don't need to sac him in your upkeep to get the tokens. Often your opponent will be forced to trade a Hill Giant with your Jotun to stop you from building even more counters, and you'll *still* get a pair of owls out of the deal.

**Krovikan Whispers**  
 We designers are suckers for exploring the flipside. We also searched for the most interesting ways to do a cumulative upkeep card that *punishe*d you for keeping it a long time. Control Magic is certainly an effect you'd never want to sacrifice. But if you hold onto it too long, you can trap yourself on a path where losing control of the upkeep can be literally lethal.

### Cumulative Bizarro

I've gotta hand it to the *Weatherlight* design team – they have some pretty crazy cumulative upkeeps. And we followed in their footsteps. But every time the *Coldsnap* developers gave us feedback (does that mean I gave *myself* feedback?), they demanded even more cumulative upkeeps… and wanted them even more bizarre! Fortunately for them, we're just the kind of maniacs to give them exactly what they wanted. This category includes a lot of fun Johnny build-around cards, with some cumulative costs that can get totally out of hand and really hard to pay really quickly - unless you're smart and you find a way to break the system. Hey, know any smart **Magic** players?

**Jotun Grunt**  
 A 4/4 for two mana? Really? All you have to do is keep your graveyard stocked…or your opponent's…or both. Know any good ways to do that? I bet Savra has some friends who might have some advice for you. Or you could get some clues by listening to some Tolarian Winds. Or by peering into a Mesmeric Orb. Or casting Savannah Lions and Lightning Helix and attacking!

**Vexing Sphinx**  
 Here's a variant of Air Elemental that deals about eight more damage, about a million times more quickly. Even without sick combos, let's look at what you're getting here, straight-up. Say you keep the Sphinx for two turns. On turn 4, you discard a card and attack for 4. On Turn 5, your discard two cards and attack for 4 more. On turn 6, you add a third age counter, choose not to pay the upkeep, sacrifice the Sphinx and draw 3 extra cards. So for a total cost of ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif), you got “Draw 3, Discard 3, Deal 8 damage.” In blue. Um, what? Seriously! And as if that's not already one of the sickest filtering spells of all time, I'm sure you can find ways to enjoy discarding a card in the upkeep of your fourth turn. Hey, what does Zombify cost again?

**Phyrexian Soulgorger**  
 I guess 4/4 for two or three mana wasn't good enough for us designers - no, we had to go and give you 8/8 for three mana. But I warn you, you have to give it haste, or sac a bunch of Zuberas. Wait, what do you mean that isn't a drawback?

**Wall of Shards**  
 Ever gotten a blocker like this for two mana? I gotta say, I *don't* recommend this in a Boros deck. Or White Weenie. Or a Zoo deck. But the creative among you just might think of ways to win a game of **Magic** that don't care about giving the opponent an extra 30 life. The *extremely* creative among you might even think of ways to win a game of **Magic** *through* giving the opponent an extra 30 life. I've got at least four ideas on this one, but I don't want to spoil it - post your best ideas on the boards!

**Sheltering Ancient**  
 Here's another weird cumulative puzzle to solve. A 5/5 trampler for two mana certainly gives you incentive to solve it. Played in mono-green, this guy starts out great but eventually gets bogged down under the weight of the bulk he's giving the opposing army. Good thing Green has other friends. Negate the drawback with Pacifism or Gelid Shackles… a turn 2 5/5 is pretty obviously insane when all the counters he's giving away go on a pacifist! I've even seen the truly brave or truly crazy run this combo in Limited. (You know, like me!) But true hax0rs won't settle for “just a two-mana trampling 5/5” – they'll want to turn the enemy counters to their advantage. Try hitting with your 5/5 for a couple of turns, building up the cumulative counters on a single enemy 1/1. On your fifth turn, you've put a total of six +1/+1 counters on that enemy creature, and it's finally gotten big enough stop your Ancient. Which is just what you planned, so you cast Threaten and bash your opponent with your 5/5 and his new 7/7 buddy. Take that, “drawback.” Threaten does give the creature back at end of turn…so next turn you can stack four more counters on that enemy creature, sending it up to 11/11, then cast Blind with Anger and send them both in again! Sometimes I don't know how the development team lets us get away with this stuff – oops, I was on the development team too. Let's just say I was more of a mole, working undercover to get more of our sneaky design tricks through unhindered. (Let's hope they don't read this.)

### Cumulative Benefits

This final category contains the craziest cumulative upkeeps of all. There's nothing in the rules for cumulative upkeeps that *technically demands* we build a cost into the mechanic. We wondered, what could we do with turning the mechanic for “cumulative upkeep” into a pure benefit? Then you could get the benefit once the first turn, twice the second turn, three times the third turn and so on. Oh, we liked the sound of that. Psychic Vortex was the only cumulative upkeep card ever to touch on this area. It's a pretty cool design, providing a lot of raw power and some very harsh drawbacks to try to keep you honest. I warn you, you might want to cover the eyes of young children in the room – these next designs can get off-the-rubber-walls insane-asylum crazy.

**Karplusan Minotaur**  
 “Cumulative Upkeep – Flip a coin.” What does that even mean? Oh right, the *other* abilities. With this kind of upkeep, it doesn't take long until this guy is spraying pings all over the board. The good news is you'll know to come prepared with fatties, Circle of Protection: Red, and Urza's Armor, while your opponent will be stuck counting his dead Birds of Paradise and Hypnotic Specters. If you have two Karplusan Minotaurs out, each one sees all of each one's coin flips. The board will quickly turn into a bombed-out warzone. For multiplayer chaos, try adding Lashknife Barrier so your own Minotaurs live through the shell shock. The best part about building with the Minotaur is the fact that he pings off of all your other coin flips too. Crazed Firecat? Check. Fiery Gambit? Check. Chance Encounter? Check check check.

**Phyrexian Etchings**  
 It just wouldn't be right to finish the *Ice Age* Block without an homage to one of the sickest cards in **Magic's** history: The Skull. “Cumulative,” hmm? So across four turns, you draw ten extra cards? Hey, do you know any ways to use ten extra cards to win a game of **Magic**? Keep them cheap, since Etchings will be eating a bunch of your mana. You could try to fit in some lifegain to get around Etchings' life payments. Hmm, if only *Coldsnap* had a black card that let you use extra cards to gain tons of life and deal tons of damage at a very low mana cost… Blammo! Or just use your extra cards to win the game before the Etchings ever goes away. If the Etchings never goes to the graveyard – you never pay any life for your extra cards at all.

**Braid of Fire**  
 “Cumulative upkeep – Add ![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif) to your mana pool.” Now this is playing with fire. Most cumulative upkeep designs focus on the word ”cumulative”…but what about the word “upkeep?” What could we make you do in their upkeep that would be more interesting than making you do it in your main phase? The answer is giving you a gushing fountain of mana in the upkeep phase. There's a lot of power here, and a lot of potential to go out of control. You just have to find a way to use it. What's the last cheap permanent that gave you 1 mana, then 2, then 3, then 4, then 5, as long as you spent it on a certain kind of spell? Ah yes, AEther Vial. You know, the artifact that made so much cumulative mana it got banned in Extended? Braid of Fire is AEther Vial for burn spells. Or Firebreathing creatures. Or Rakdos Guildmage. Or Dark Depths! Or any other activation cost in **Magic**. Many developers wanted to “lock you in” and prevent you from being able to sac the Braid of Fire once you started it. We liked the fitting image of the power-crazed pyromancer having something go horribly wrong, losing his mana sinks, and dying to his own beloved burn, of the mana burn variety. But in the end, the development team decided to make it work just like every cumulative upkeep, and let you turn it off whenever you want.

**Herald of Leshrac**  
 And so we come around, full circle, to one of my favorite cards I designed for *Coldsnap*. The *Coldsnap* development team really wanted a weird *Ice Age*'ish demon like Infernal Denizen. Infernal Denizen is a wonder of a **Magic** creature. A unique mana cost across 13 years of **Magic**, chunky near-unique stats, a powerful ability, and a huge drawback. He can even go out of both players' control, raging around howling insane with his unsated hunger for swamps, giving everyone control of everyone else's creatures. I love how when you look at Infernal Denizen, you don't even have any idea whether he's a good card or not, especially in those days. He's just so… weird. And so… awesome.

Herald of Leshrac is similarly difficult to evaluate. So he's sort of a flying 8/10 Opponent-Armageddoning Personal Heartbeat of Spring Demon. And he's sort of a seven-mana Fighting Drake that dies in a few turns. Which one is true? The answer is somewhere in between. If your opponent has seven lands like you do, let's look at what Herald can do. First turn he Stone Rains them, gives you a land, and hits for 3. Then he Stone Rains twice more, gives you two more lands, and hits for 5. By the third turn, he's taken six of your opponent's lands and taken you up to thirteen lands, and hits for 8 more damage. In a duel, he'll sometimes live to go up to 12/14, but he'll often have to die before you can pay that last upkeep. If he dies on schedule like that, he'll “only” have dealt 16 damage, torn your opponent's mana apart for 2-3 turns, and given you a huge mana boost. And in multiplayer - hoo boy. Let's just say you'll pretty clearly be the big man at the table when you've stolen about 21 of your opponents' collective lands, are playing 20 mana of spells a turn, and are polishing off players one 23/25 flying demon hit at a time. I leave the combo applications up to you. Really. Okay, I just can't resist: Think Sylvan Safekeeper, Overgrown Estate, and good old *Ice Age* Zuran Orb!

### Cumulative Farewell

I hope you've enjoyed this whirlwind tour of the design of *Coldsnap*'s cumulative upkeeps, including 1) Simple undercosting, 2) Cumulatively-growing abilities, 3) Cumulative upkeep cards that pay you back for your investment, 4) Bizarre build-around upkeeps, and 5) Cumulative benefits sitting where costs are supposed to be. This isn't your grand-dad's Arnjlot's Ascent! Let me leave you with a final plug for *Coldsnap* draft in particular. As other authors have explained, we worked hard to deploy a variety of intriguing “collect-me” cards, making *Coldsnap* draft a truly unique experience where having 7 of the same card in a 40-card deck becomes both a dream and a reality. I hope you get a chance to check it out!
