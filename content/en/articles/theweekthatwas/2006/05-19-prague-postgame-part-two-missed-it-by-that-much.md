Going into the last draft of [Pro Tour-Prague](/en/events/2006/ptpra06/osawas-wurms-flog-prague), there were two potential stories being written. The first is the one you already know. It is a Sunday featuring breakout performances by players who have been butting up against the glass ceiling for years: Antonino De Rosa, Joe Crosby, Rasmus Sibast, Takuya Osawa, and Quentin Martin, with a sprinkling of Cinderella topping via Aaron Brackmann and Christian Hüttenberger, and topped off with another sparking performance by Shuhei Nakamura.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptpra06/prague_5.jpg"><br><i>Ahh, sweet memories of Prague ...</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

I don't want to take anything away from the performances of those players. Years from now – even months from now – we may look back on this and see that it was one of the best Top 8s in recent memory should any of the seven players who experienced their first trip to the Sunday stage be invited back for encore engagements. As those final rounds played out, those of us in the coverage game couldn't help but fantasize about the Top 8 that almost was.

You had Pro Tour-Columbus winner and reigning Rookie of the Year Pierre Canali, Pro Tour-London winner Geoffrey Siron, money-finish machine Jelger Wiegersma, Road Warrior Olivier Ruel, and bluffmaster/Augermage Terry Soh all battling for that oh-so elusive Top 8 berth in the waning rounds. 

It's a theme I talked about after Hawaii – it doesn't take much to miss a Top 8. Sometimes the difference between a Top 8 and Top 16 is as miniscule as decimal points to the thousandths. Sometimes it is a poor decision in the last round, as was the case with Canali. 

Pierre was in sixth place after 14 rounds and looked to be in great shape for making his first Top 8 since his very first Pro Tour. He has been working very hard for the past two seasons to get back to Sunday. He practices constantly and yearns to show people that his best finish was not just because Arcbound Ravager was just *that* broken. He has put up decent finishes over the two seasons, along with some solid Grand Prix performances, but after round 14 he had never been closer to his goal in half a dozen Pro Tours. 

His opponent for that round was Joe Crosby, and when the two of them looked at the standings they determined that a draw should get them both in. As soon as they drew you could see the look of pain on Randy Buehler's face. Randy had been following how the rest of the top tables had either drawn or played and knew that Pierre had at best drawn himself into ninth, with a clean break at 35 points making up the Top 8.

Pierre was crushed by the turn of events – and don't for a moment think it was about the money. More than anything Pierre wants to get back to the Top 8. His approach to the draft had put him in a perfect position to make it to Sunday, but instead one ill-advised decision dropped him down to 10th. 

I asked the reigning Rookie of the Year what approach he had taken and it turned out that he fell into the power-drafting camp. He felt that *Ravnica* was the weakest of the three packs and could dedicate those picks to mana fixing if there was nothing spectacular to jump into. 

“I would first-pick Civic Wayfinder,” Pierre explained. “First-pick signet, bounce lands – and then play five-color good stuff. I tried to make green and another color my main color and then I would splash the other three colors. You really have to remember your entire draft though – you can't always pick the best card, you have to keep an eye on which are your main colors and which ones are your splash.”

That is not to say that Pierre was locked into the five-color strategy: “For example, you could have no red cards and only play four colors.”

One of the fascinating developments of drafting this block is that where people could get cut out of a color in past formats, in this one you could get cut out of a strategy. If the guy to your right is drafting mana-fixing highly, you are unlikely to see a karoo, or Farseek, and the chances of seeing a Wayfinder are almost zero. In the case of that emergency, Pierre had a second technique under glass. 

“Sometimes you see no fixers at all,” grumbled Canali. “So you have to go for mono-colors in Ravnica. All the colors are good actually. You should not force anything – just let the cards lead you. If you were blue in *Ravnica* you will have a choice between red, red-green, and black-white in *Guildpact*.”

From there you just follow the colors in the third pack. If you were blue-black-white, you look to be Azorius, and so on. 

“With the exception of maybe green-white-black, all the three color combinations are playable,” Pierre advised. “But five-color is more fun.” 

Sometimes missing the Top 8 can be about something as innocuous as a Bramble Elemental. Coming into Prague, Johan Sadeghpour was practicing with some of the best Limited players in the history of the game, including the Mantle and Mays of the format in Anton Jonsson and Nicolai Herzog. Johan is no slouch himself, finishing third in the last individual Limited Pro Tour, London. 

While Pierre opted for power, Johan was looking for consistency in his drafts and had a plan coming into his first draft of the weekend. 

“I figured out that you want to be open for Azorius or Simic in *Dissension*,” Johan elaborated. “So you should just try to figure out in pack one if you're getting passed green or white and draft accordingly.”

There may be some temptation to draft Selesnya in pack one but Johan does not advise going that route: “I think usually that's too greedy since it leaves you a bit short in *Guildpact* and most of the Azorius/Simic cards are pretty mana-intensive. I have seen a few very good blue-green-white decks that obviously are playing like 12 *Dissension* cards – I guess it can happen if you see those evangels tabling in pack one.”

The strategy paid off for Johan all weekend. After three drafts, he was 7-2 and in an excellent position to make a run for the Top 8. 

“I drafted four good decks but I messed up one draft – entirely my own fault,” Johan said as he recalled his debilitating fourth draft of the weekend. “I should've moved into green pretty late pack one but figured I was in good shape anyway since the guy I was feeding was getting all the late green anyway, but he stuck to his guns and shipped all the green back in pack two so I got nothing.”

Johan opened the fourth draft with Galvanic Arc, Compulsive Research, and Auratouched Mage before being given a choice between Boros Signet and Bramble Elemental: “So I signet. Then fifth it was like a decent white card over decent green and after that it was all green but I still could've switched.”

Johan righted his ship in the last draft and swept the pod to finish at 11-4 and in 14th place, a solid follow-up to his third-place finish in London. “I'm very happy with Top 16,” he concluded. “Especially after bombing that fourth draft. I managed a 1-2 with an 0-3 deck.”

### Deeper in the Standings…

Two guys who were just as happy with their finishes – despite being outside of the velvet ropes – were 37th-place finisher Przemyslaw Wolowiec of Poland and 94th-place finisher Anthony Williams. 

Przemyslaw was the winner of a special online tournament that awarded him and his friend Krzysztof Hoffmann airfare and accommodations to Los Angeles for E3 and the **Magic** Invitational. He had scarcely finished his 15th round Saturday in Prague when already he had to think about heading back to the hotel and packing for his trip to L.A.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/04/58/0458266b2df337e25fc745d22255e24256626be8d2fcf4d66887b33ae20283f5.jpg"><br><i>Przemyslaw Wolowiec enjoyed two weeks of criss-crossing the globe, all for <b>Magic</b>.</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

“We are looking forward to having a lot of fun watching the world's best players fight for a very precious prize – making their own card,” beamed Wolowiec after the first money finish of his career. “The Japanese players are fascinating. They are so different. I also like the Ruel brothers because they are so easy going and friendly. I am looking forward to seeing some high level play to learn more about the game.”

He was looking forward to not only the Invitational, but the sights and sounds of LA as well. “I would like to see the ocean and wander around town. I am looking forward to a short but extremely efficient vacation. I could never afford such a trip. Even if I had such money I could not imagine spending it on a trip like this. I am very happy.”

To earn the prize, he won a nine-round online qualifier tournament that featured well over 200 *Ravnica-Guildpact* sealed deck competitors and then cut to a Top 8 draft. 

“I went 7-1-1 and then in the draft I was able to draft a Crosis-colored deck built around Niv-Mizzet. I had tutor for Niv-Mizzet, some tricks to reanimate him on turn four, and some solid Crosis-colored tricks – transmute, removal, card-drawing. The other players did not seem to have decks that were as good and my deck was very cruel,” he recalled with a rather evil grin. 

Przemyslaw has been playing **Magic** for six years and this was his third Pro Tour. “I won a PTQ in Krakow. It is my third Pro Tour – Nice, Venice, and here. I had a couple more qualifications but could not afford to go. Here I had lots of fun and posted good results.”

He even beat an Invitationalist during the Swiss, defeating Latin America representative Jose Barbero.

When asked if it felt extra special to take down a name player, Przemyslaw shrugged, “I have beaten Kai Budde three times, so Barbero is not a big deal. I think Kai is the best player ever. I have seen him many times winning unwinnable games – just a juggernaut.” 

Pro Tour-Charleston is not on the Polish player's agenda, however. “I am focusing on my doctoral studies so I do not think I will find good enough players to form a team. I will focus on individual tournaments.”

There's no doubt he was wiped out from the whirlwind trip back and forth to Los Angeles but hopefully we will hear back from Przemyslaw next week with his insights into the Invitational experience. 

England's Anthony Williams was a first-time Pro Tour competitor who achieved a personal goal he set for himself coming into the tournament. 

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/71/29/7129de0e5b6b6bcbce31b4f5174306ac35b76b375e9ecbae2cb7dfe8622b1c9c.jpg"><br><i>Williams shows off his scorepad from his win over Masashi Oiso.</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

“It has been absolutely amazing,” beamed Williams. “I came here wanting to make second day as a dream. I made the second day, finishing 8-7 and beating some really good players along the way. I am so pleased." 

Anthony won a Manchester PTQ to earn his invite to Prague and has to go back to the PTQ scene if he wants to see Japan. 

“I am probably qualified for U.K. Nats on rating. I will try for Kobe but I may have used up all my luck in one go.”

Anthony took down a couple of pretty big names over the course of the weekend, and I asked him if he was going to lord it over his mates back at the local card store. 

“I'm not that kind of person,” he laughed as he fanned out his scorepad from each and every round that he is keeping as a souvenir. One in particular was more precious than the others and he held it up. 

“I might show a few people the slip though.”

### Get Out Your PDAs

There have been a couple of schedule changes for upcoming events that you should mark down. The most important change involves Pro Tour-Kobe. The last Pro Tour stop of the year will now take place October 20-22. The format is *Time Spiral* booster draft. This moves the event back so that it will take place two weeks after the release of the new set. 

Standard Champs for North America, Latin America, and Japan is also going to be moving, shifting one weekend to October 28. *Time Spiral* will still rotate in on October 20, as is the normal case with set rotations.
