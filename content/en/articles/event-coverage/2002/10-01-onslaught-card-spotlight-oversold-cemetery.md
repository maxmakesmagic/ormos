Anyone who played competitive magic during Rath cycle remembers Counter-Phoenix. One of the purest control decks of all time, Counter-Phoenix was largely based around the combination of recurring Shard Phoenixes and Forbid, which together formed a soft counter-lock against most of the decks in the format. With the ability to stop nearly every spell the opponent cast, the Counter-Phoenix player ended up in a position to win more or less at his leisure.

There were few things in that format more empowering than to be the one casting Forbid in these situations, and few things more frustrating than being the one having all of your spells countered. The typical foil for control decks – an early creature assault – was of little use against Counter-Phoenix decks, which played red removal spells to solve just this problem. Thus, the other decks in the format had to look for different solutions to Counter-Phoenix’s soft lock, which seemed to many to be nearly impenetrable.

The “lock” wasn’t truly such, as the opposing player still had the opportunity to play all of his or her cards – however, most, if not all, of them simply got countered. The Forbid-Phoenix lock was based on the assumption that the opponent would only be drawing one card per turn, and that since not every card in that player’s deck would be a threat, the recurred Phoenix plus the one random card drawn per turn by the Counter-Phoenix player would be able to trade for threat cards quite easily.

Enter Oath of Ghouls. This simple two casting cost enchantment provided the answer to Counter-Phoenix’s Forbid lock, at least as far as the popular Living Death deck was concerned. Oath of Ghouls allowed the Living Death decks – or any other decks with a significant creature base and black component – to recur a threat creature every turn. This not only improved the number of cards the Living Death deck now got to play with, but ensured that one of the cards every turn would be a significant threat to the Counter-Phoenix player, like Thrull Surgeon or something similar. With such a low casting cost, Oath of Ghouls could hit play before the Counter-Phoenix player could stop it, and since blue/red decks traditionally have no way of effectively dealing with enchantments – Counter-Phoenix being no exception – a turn two Oath of Ghouls could often simply decide the game.

Modern control decks have leaned away from the pure control route, with Psychatog decks far more capable of simply killing the opponent before such a battle of attrition becomes decisive, but with the loss of Fact or Fiction and Nightscape Familiar, it seems likely that control decks will slow down enough for an effect like that Oath of Ghouls provides to be relevant. We have seen the impact this sort of recursion can have already in the form of Genesis, but the mana requirements of that Incarnation make it significantly more difficult to use it as a tool for recurring important threats. Oath of Ghouls itself, or a close cousin, would be much more effective in that department.

Lo and behold, *Onslaught* has provided just that. Oversold Cemetery is a somewhat toned down version of Oath of Ghouls, in terms of its effectiveness against control decks, since it’s not active until you have four creatures in your graveyard, but once you reach that point in the game – which you plan on doing in such a matchup – you’re golden. Oversold Cemetery can recur creatures at no mana cost every turn, allowing you to present threat after threat efficiently, pressing your control opponent to answer it or die. With the slowing down of Psychatog decks – and the likely rise in the number of black control decks, with only Haunting Echoes to answer the Cemetery – this sort of card advantage and card quality engine stands to be extremely powerful in the upcoming Standard environment.

One of the most interesting decks I remember seeing while testing for the World Championships this year was a g-b-r deck based around Buried Alive and Genesis. The deck used Buried Alive to fetch Genesis and a number of utility creatures and then used Genesis to recur them repeatedly. The deck also included a significant land denial component as well as Pardic Miner, which allowed for some hilarious situations in which the opponent never got to play a land again due to Miner recursion. Oversold Cemetery allows for the same sort of degeneracy, and with no mana cost to use the ability, will make such combinations much easier to accomplish.

With the plethora of utility creatures in the new Standard, along with the slowing down of the format – as well as the fact that Oversold Cemetery only works for you, and not your opponent – it seems possible that this may in fact be the new Oath of Ghouls. The requirement of four creatures in the graveyard before it starts working is somewhat steep, and may be prohibitive to its effectiveness, but time will tell whether this can hold back this extremely powerful card. Control players, look out. Your counterspells just aren’t what they used to be.
