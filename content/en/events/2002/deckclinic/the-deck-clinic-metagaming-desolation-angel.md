In my last column, I revealed that I would play a deck from the Deck Doctor series at U.S. Nationals. The deck I chose was the u-b-w Desolation Angel deck, a decision made for three reasons. First, and foremost, the deck is a lot of fun to play. I have played in enough tournaments now that I want a deck I can enjoy, even if it is not necessarily the best deck to play. I know I play a lot better when I am enjoying the game. That said, I also really liked playing the monoblack deck. The second reason for my choice was that the deck was made specifically without adding any rares, and I wanted to see what would happen if I removed this limitation. Finally, I thought the Angel deck was stronger than the black deck. Here then, is the deck as we last saw it:

The first part of tuning a deck for a specific environment is to understand that environment. Fortunately, Grand Prix-Milwaukee & German Nationals were held just in time for me to make an educated guess as to what this metagame would be. In addition, I would have time directly after the meatgrinders (U.S. Open) to make any last-minute adjustments. I studied GP-Milwaukee and German Nationals, plus reports on the net as to how people were reacting to it. I decided the metagame would have three main decks: Psychatog, Goblin Trenches and blue-green Opposition. There was also the possibility of the Ensnaring Bridge/Grafted Skullcap deck that took 2nd in Germany. Additionally, I had advance warning about a blue-green tempo deck that the rest of Team Godzilla was working on. However, I considered that information off limits, as we never metagame against ourselves. A little more research found out that Psychatog decks were switching from Standstill to Deep Analysis. Since Deep Analysis can be cast on your opponent, I felt that this change would provide a natural barrier to Ensnaring Bridge based decks surviving. With this information, I limited the decks I was most concerned about to the original three.

Now that I had a metagame, I needed to look at how my deck stacked up against the field. The analysis was actually quite easy, and the results were not surprising since the metagame had taken a dramatic shift since I wrote the original article. Decks with token creature generators (Squirrel's Nest and Goblin Trenches) are problematic for Chainer's Edict and Innocent Blood. This was bad enough that both Opposition and Trenches were bad match-ups. Psychatog usually degenerated into a card drawing battle. Originally, they had Standstill and Fact or Fictions. The hard part here is that Standstill was just a nightmare for the deck. Since I have to cast Diamonds in order to have a good mana base, I wind up blowing the Standstill in most games. This would lead to a rapidly declining game where they would get to draw extra cards first, then counter my card drawing and then draw even more cards. However, the metagame solved this problem for me, as the Standstills became Deep Analysis. While Deep Analysis was still bad for me, it was nowhere near as bad as Standstill. Still, it was another bad match-up for which I needed a solution.

The first order of business was to get rid of the rule that I could not add additional rares. While this had made for an interesting deck before, that was to allow the original deck owner to be able to build the deck. Since I would have no problems getting the rares, this limitation was removed. Having decided this, I could change the mana base a little. The original deck didn't really have enough white to be able to cast Wrath of God reliably, or other any other white spell I wanted to put in. For Nationals, I would need to make sure I could reliably cast these spells. As such, I replaced some basic lands with Caves of Koilos. An important part of playing control vs. control match-ups is the quantity of land in each deck. Against countermagic, you not only need to be able to cast your offensive spells, you also need to be able to back them up with Counterspells. Since all three of the decks I expected to face were slower, control style decks, I increased the land count. The last mana change was to get the 4th Coastal Tower into the deck. I don't expect the control decks to be able to punish me like the more aggressive creature decks could, so again, I concentrated on getting the colored mana. Although the Caves and the Tower helped shore up my colored mana, getting the right color of mana to cast my spells would continue to be a concern right up until game day.

Another rare-related change was one that I mentioned in the original article: Replacing the Dismantling Blow with a Vindicate. While this was weaker in general against Opposition, it was definitely stronger against Psychatog, and still had the potential to be better against Opposition under the right circumstances.

Next, I eliminated the Innocent Bloods. Since I felt that I still needed some answers to creature decks, one of these became the 4th Wrath of God. While the new mana was still not reliable enough to cast Wrath, the removal of 3 Innocent Bloods was going to wreak enough havoc with my early creature defense that I felt global removal was really my only chance against these decks. Too often, when I did face red-green in earlier playtesting, a fast Wrath would have been enough to pull out a victory.

Looking at the three decks, there was a card in my sideboard that I almost always brought in. Since this cards came in so often, I made it part of the main deck. This card was Engineered Plague. Starting 2 Plagues had a profound effect against both Trenches and Opposition. I now had an answer that was sorely needed for their token-generating cards. This was enough to even the match-up against both decks. It also had a positive effect on Psychatog, but not enough to allow me to reach parity. The ability to lock out the Nightscape Familiar or force a Psychatog to be able to kill me in one shot bought me a lot of time, but usually they managed to put me away in the end.

For a while, I tried Haunting Echoes and Disrupting Scepter. While they seemed good at first, continued playtesting brought to light that they just weren't getting the job done and I went back to the drawing board.

Finally, I came across a card that I hadn't considered in the original article because of the unreliability of getting white mana: Gerrard's Verdict. The Duresses came out and Verdicts went in, although only 3. Although I would really like to have all 4 in the deck, I simply ran out of room, and these seemed like the weakest link. In testing, they performed extremely well, although not as well as they would later at Nationals. These managed to pull me up to even against Psychatog for a reason I wouldn't have suspected: I would often gain 9 life off of casting 2 Verdicts, which would prevent the Psychatog from being able to kill me directly after Upheaval. This rare benefit of gaining life was totally unexpected.

As I moved on to the sideboard, I made one of the most common mistakes: not leaving enough time for this task. Much of the sideboard became a total rewrite. While I kept the 4 Slays and the 2 Dodecapods, I put in 3 of the 4 Duresses that came out of the deck, along with the 4th Gerrard's Verdict. 2 Dismantling Blows were added to deal with Opposition. I chose it over Aura Blast on the off chance that I did run into an Ensnaring Bridge deck. The Disrupting Scepter that never worked out in testing wound up in the sideboard, as I was never able to let go of the idea. This card was an obvious mistake, and I never brought it in. Another mistake was 2 Altered Realities. Although 1 certainly belongs, the 2nd should never come in, and thus wasted space. In retrospect, the slots used by Disrupting Scepter and the 2nd Altered Reality should have been used on Mana Shorts.

With these changes, I felt I had a reasonable chance against the field. While the deck isn't stellar, it is definitely playable. I rate the deck as a tier two deck, one with which I hoped to go 4-2 with. My results were less impressive. The colored mana requirements are simply tougher that the mana base can support. I had expected to lose multiple games to this problem. Ironically, this wasn't the case. No matter how bad the deck is at getting 2 white mana, and no matter how good the deck is at getting to 3 or more mana, there is still the chance that on any particular day, getting to 3 mana will be the problem. This was exactly what happened at Nationals. I think I lost 1, maybe 2 games to color problems. I lost 7 to not getting to 3 mana. While this happens to all of us at some point, I was disappointed that it happened while trying to show the deck.

Finally, a note on playing the deck against Psychatog: Often, these games come down to a lot of card drawing. Here, Gerrard's Verdict can look a little weak since the opponent tends to just refill his hand. What I learned while playing at Nationals, and alluded to earlier is that you simply want to hold them in your hand for a while. Your opponent is very likely to start drawing lots of cards, and in the process, discard most of the junk. If you just sit back on your counters and allow this card drawing to go on, often they will go ahead and draw a bunch of cards, discarding the junk in their hand. Once they have done this, you need to detect a lull in the action, where they seem to have run out of card drawers. At this point, the Verdicts really hit home. Another nice side effect of this strategy is that you might draw one of the Angels or the Yawgmoth's Agenda, and this will allow you to lead off with the discard spells. Still, you really want to look for that lull where they seem to be out of card drawing.
