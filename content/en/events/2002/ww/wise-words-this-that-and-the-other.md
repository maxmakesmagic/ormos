Before I left for Philadelphia, I got an interesting email from Dirk Baberowski. He wished me good luck, but in doing so sent with that thought a warning: be sure to play your deck a lot during your byes, because you've almost certainly built it wrong. Dirk's point was that in the sealed decks he'd seen thus far in this very intricate Onslaught Limited format, he'd seen very few built correctly. 

 ![Centaur Glade](https://i.ormos.uk/a0/17/a017ad8351a416781e960121b44026fa23ada62433a80cb5c858210717da803c.jpg) 

Dirk's advice proved important when I started playing some test games with my green-white deck (splashing black for Cruel Revival). My green was pretty much insane (Centaur Glade, Hystrodon, Silklash Spider), my white solid, my other colors weak, so there were no problems there, but I made the mistake of building inside the box, adhering to past rules of sealed deck engagement. That was in error.

After a few games with Scott McCord where the sheer power of the Spider made itself apparent, we deconstructed my deck and Scott pointed out an error I'd made in playing Symbiotic Elf (my deck was non-tribal) over Haunted Cadaver. The logic goes that the off color 2/2 with one swamp and Explosive Vegetation in my deck for the fluke chance of Fugue-ing my opponent would better serve my mana curve then would a four-mana 2/2 in a deck full of expensive green fat. The logic was correct. We all still have plenty of lessons to learn: what follows are a few of the ones that were made apparent to me in Philly:

**Read the Cards** 

More than once, I made mistakes because I wasn't aware of the wording on cards. I attacked with a Gustcloak Harrier into Venomous Brackus because I thought the beast targeted any flyer (I only found out they had to be attacking or blocking when I read the match coverage on *Sideboard*), and that's just not how you win games. Every word on an *Onslaught* card has importance, perhaps even more so than sets of the past.

### Some New Thoughts on Tribal

There are few players whose opinions on Limited I respect more then Mike Turian's. The man picks up Limited formats very quickly and has as innate an understanding of the forty-card game as anyone on the planet, so I was pretty surprised when he told me "I never draft tribal".

In the past few weeks, I've stressed the importance of going tribal, and in part I still adhere to the idea that your best decks are going to be based around that concept, but Mike's opinion has always had enough influence on me that its forced me to go back and weigh the importance of staying within the tribal lines.

I don't wholly agree that tribal isn't a dictum that shouldn't be adhered to, but at the same time, after trying to force assorted tribes in practice drafts through the last few weeks, it's become apparent that while you should definitely be focused on the tribes you are drafting, you can also push the notion too far.

Have you ever had one of those drafts where you're trying everything in your power to figure out which colors you should be, but every pack you receive suggests you're on the wrong track? You have cards in all the colors but white, and then get passed Pacifism and a bunch of awfulness. When you're struggling to find a deck, eventually you have to suck it up and realize that the only way your deck will be strong enough to survive is to go three colors with sealed deck mana and hope to get the good draws, and the lessons learned in that example can and should be applied to the drafting of tribes as well.

If your first few picks fall into the same tribe, you should definitely keep that in mind when making each ensuing pick. Once you're entrenched in a tribe, it's even acceptable to make small sacrifices in card quality in the name of good deck synergy. The trick is learning to recognize those moments when you're making too big a sacrifice in the name of your particular tribe.

 ![Profane Prayers](https://i.ormos.uk/a0/75/a0752ad94bba759f60bcec2da5218b9c5c07b4b363ef3054f0b06dd027354984.jpg) 

For the moment, as you go through the early-in-the-season rigors of learning the format, here's what I recommend: in the first pack, try to stick to the best cards and see what patterns emerge from the packs that are passed to you. If you see a 12th pick Profane Prayers, that should tell you that clerics are being underdrafted, and that you'll get a chance to draft the rest of them, meaning you should gear yourself more towards getting clerics for your deck. Just about any other tribe-reliant card can be used for a similar example.

Once you're into the second pack, the competent drafters at your table will have established themselves in certain colors and potentially in certain tribes as well, and at that point, wanting to stay out of their way while keeping them out of yours, I'd suggest you start looking at your deck along tribal lines as that's where you'll find your best potential for synergy lifting the deck's strength beyond the sum of its parts. Of course, the moment you open a lord, all the rules change.

In the end, this all goes back to the basics. Understanding which cards work well together is part of being a good drafter, and the tribal mechanic provides an easy to follow guideline on such things. Don't overextend yourself too much in your attempts to have one unified creature type in your deck, but don't ignore the fact that you can exploit this format by reading the line under the picture too.

### Sandskin

 ![Sandskin](https://i.ormos.uk/d4/dd/d4ddb23ff7e590cbae8a95b25a4562dc9b1d399d8bd6fdd6286f4436edcb4ff9.jpg) 

Basing my feelings about this card on environments of the past, I hadn't really realized until this weekend how important it is. The poor man's Pacifism, what that really means is that it serves a similar function while being much easier to obtain in the draft, with the white mage often getting it seventh or eighth without much difficulty.

The popular line of thought with this format thus far has been that you need to draft either red or black to be successful, and while that isn't entirely true, the process of surviving without either kill spell color is a tricky one that needs to be learned, and Sandskin is a major part of that. Without the ability to remove evasion creatures, white decks have four choices: trade their own evasion creatures with them, outrace them, lose or Sandskin. With the reintroduction of fear in this format, the last option is likely the best one.

Gluttonous Zombie and Severed Legion have been giving gamers combining white with either blue or green fits for weeks now, increasing Sandskin's value to those players. With fear guys taken out of the racing equation, white's flying creatures have the opportunity to outrace black. Yes, Sparksmith remains an issue, one around which three-toughness creatures should be drafted a little higher when not playing the 'kill colors', but aside from that little pest, Sandskin goes along way towards solving the problems white otherwise faces.

### Five Good Non-Rare Reasons to Play Blue

Everyone's been talking about how bad blue is of late, but I've drafted it twice now in big events and have been very happy with the results. Here are a few things to keep in mind before entering a draft assuming there are only four colors in *Onslaught*:

 ![Lightning Rift](https://i.ormos.uk/50/02/5002f0b54d0fbee8a11abd9565e91501d5637907decf1b5468c32c3490a6b1b1.jpg) 

**Cycling** - No color does it as well as blue. If you get your hands on Lightning Rift or Astral Slide, you'll want to get yourself as many cycling cards as possible, and if that's the case, blue is the way to go. Choking Tethers is a bit of a bomb (Ensnare anyone?), Backslide is a playable trick, Slipstream Eel will occasionally win random games later on and I know I've already won a few games on Mage's Guile. With blue's lack of popularity, these cards will come later than their power level suggests they should, making your 'when a player cycles…' card degenerate, and your deck more efficient in the process.

**No one else wants to** - The best reason in the world to draft a color. Yes, blue is ultra-weak, but that has made the idea of drafting it distasteful to 90% of the globe, which in turn makes it very viable as you could well be the only one working with that fifth of the card pool. This is especially true in Rochester, where your decision to play blue will almost certainly scare off other prospective blue drafters who hoped to dominate the color in the same way.

 ![Riptide Biologist](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/f4/f9f43fd0b42b9bf6b066376c00eaa5f4a66c848503154c3f293a8c4409c270e8.jpg) 

**Riptide Biologist** - Such an innocent looking little card, isn't it? I mean, a 1/2 with protection from creature type; it just doesn't seem substantial enough. That all changes once you've had it in play a couple of times. At the Prerelease in Boston, this was my only non-land permanent, but I was feeling pretty good against my opponent's board of Fogbeat, Fogbeast and Wretched Anurid, and through the last month a number of similarly ludicrous situations have arisen when this thing's been really good. We're not talking 'pick it really early' good here; it's more subtle than that. It's the fact you'll get it seventh that makes it a little powerhouse.

  

 ![Riptide Shapeshifter](https://i.ormos.uk/12/4c/124ce4ac0ccd551af7ec7a4d4f836efff824e46d1d3ac40c70e3276586c9bed6.jpg) 

**Riptide Shapeshifter** - This card is absolutely insane for the blue mage. I know, getting the bomb creature is half the battle, but once you do, this becomes an easy first pick, because naming "legend" with Rorix as the only one in your deck is just abusive, and there are so many other solitary creature type first picks out there: spider, orgg, gorgon and so on and so on. Those cards may be tough for you to cast, but hey, who needs to cast them? The best part is that if you want, you can wait on shapeshifting long enough for that 3/3 body to either trade with another or do some damage. Scary.

  

 ![Lavamancer's Skill](https://i.ormos.uk/b0/d1/b0d1bbf85aba59c15fe26b914b08732ffa23d122c3b09af86a9451294d52ca98.jpg) 

**Lavamancer's Skill** - In most decks, the Skill, while playable, is nothing special, oft-times facing down opposing armies without one-toughness creatures to be seen. Drop this in a wizard deck though and trouble's a-brewin' for the opposition. That little spellslinger becomes something of a shotgun, getting off a round every turn, taking out Severed Legions and bird-soldiers and morphs of all shapes and sizes. No wonder red is blue's most popular partner in crime.

  

### …And Five Rare Ones

**Quicksilver Dragon** - You have got to be kidding me. It's not the 5/5 flying body for ![4 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/36/29/3629288966daa042decf01d3752789ab6676fddd67c3679fa580cd3baecae158.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif), not the way you can't target the thing and not the fact that it morphs: it's how little it morphs for. Sure, Exalted Angel and Silent Specter are comparable, but neither of those are splashable! For ![4 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/36/29/3629288966daa042decf01d3752789ab6676fddd67c3679fa580cd3baecae158.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif), you get to flip over this monstrosity and effectively end the game, and that is probably the best return for your mana you'll find in the format.

  

 ![Read the Runes](https://i.ormos.uk/97/78/97785c75e2f96407ea3ee05836a42d701f0e5ee6b94e0408ddab237b18a07069.jpg) 

**Read the Runes** - Might as well call this Stroke of Genius. Sure, you have to lose cards equal to the number drawn, but you can wait until your creature have damage stacked on them, suppressing enchantments on them, or sufficient lands behind them to leave them alone, and if X = more than five or six, you're going to draw the cards you need to win the game. Another splashable, it's the casting cost that makes this card so attractive.

  

 ![Arcanis the Omnipotent](https://i.ormos.uk/b0/48/b048385549af936ba517da751c86680b6e9a6d27512c8f637b1846cc31ea3693.jpg) 

**Arcanis the Omnipotent** - Omnipotent is right. Generally, you activate this thing once and you're going to win, especially when you consider summoning sickness has worn off so you've untapped your lands, allowing you to protect this monster from the few cards that can deal with it. People said my Invitational card, Brainstorm machine it was, was a little ludicrous. At least that thing made you put cards back and didn't beat down for three a turn when you were done decimating your opponent with card advantage.

  

 ![Future Sight](https://i.ormos.uk/92/79/9279b221a3f1b483796b2aa1f25032a33b14bd120c6df28d5099e489b30ba1db.jpg) 

**Future Sight** - The one problem you'll find yourself having with Arcanis is the fact you need him to stay alive for a turn to get going. Your opponent will have time to get rid of him and there are plenty of cards that will help to accomplish that task. Not so with Future Sight. This card is going to see play in Constructed, and purely for its obscene power level, and that translates well into Limited, where a plodding casting cost isn't going to hinder its playablity nearly as much as it would in the 60-card formats. I like those cards where you get 3-4 free cards a turn quite a bit. By the way, casual players: you may want to try this with Fastbond.

  

 ![Mistform Skyreaver](https://i.ormos.uk/c7/c0/c7c0b15a35a3853271032b93e49b16dc930377be49b4ce822f5a982baec7fcde.jpg) 

**Mistform Skyreaver** - 6/6 Flyers that can't be killed by Cruel Revival seem worth picking to me. Disagree? Assuming you've done two points of damage at some point, the Skyreaver puts your opponent on a three turn clock, can't be burnt out of the sky aside from the fluke Erratic Explosion, and can't have nuttin done to it because this thing's going to kill your opponent before they have a chance to even think about it.
