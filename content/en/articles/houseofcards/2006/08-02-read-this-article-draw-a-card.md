I was very excited to learn that **magicthegathering.com** Editor and Magical Academic Ted Knutson had decided that this week would be Catnip Week. My imaginary pet tabby, Cringer II, was even more thrilled. What catnip had to do with slinging cardboard spells was unclear, but I had enough faith in Ted that I let it pass without comment. It wasn't until my first draft was rejected on the grounds that it was written in hairballs and had nothing to do with **Magic** that I realized something was up. Apparently, the hallucinogenic ramblings of a fictitious feline are not suitable content for this website. Who knew? Eventually, we figured out the whole Catnip / Cantrip thing. It was all just a big misunderstanding. 

With that said, welcome to Cantrip Week! For those who don't know, “cantrip” is **Magic** slang for “bathroom break.” It's also slang for those minor spells that feature the most excellent text, “Draw a card.” For our purposes today, I'll be using the second definition.

### Elvish Vanguard

In case you were unaware, one of the formats for this year's **Magic** Invitational was called the Auction of the Geniuses. Each “Genius” had to build a Vanguard deck using the Online Classic card pool. By sheer dumb luck, Albert Einstein, Thomas Edison, Leonardo da Vinci, and Michael Bay were all dead or otherwise unavailable, so one of the Genius slots was passed down to me. When I got the call from Auction organizer Zvi Mowshowitz, I immediately built a small house out of the fresh bricks that I suddenly found in the seat of my pants. Shortly after that, I started brainstorming. I came up with six goals, and put two of them back. Here they are:

1.  Use an Avatar that no one else would want.
2.  Use the Avatar to good effect.
3.  Make a deck that is fun.
4.  Make a deck that will get played.

### Selecting an Avatar

When it came to choosing a Vanguard Avatar, I didn't want to pick something obviously powerful. With my limited knowledge of Vanguard gleaned from Bennie Smith's Into the Aether column, I quickly ruled out a few of the more powerful Avatars: Akroma, Elvish Champion, Seshiro, the Annointed, and Birds of Paradise.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/3d/9b/3d9bbcebeb34a28f7554252a795acf616dbd491501e32768b0d386c0eff05d2f.jpg)In the end, I went with Stalking Tiger. This Avatar allows you to pay 1 whenever you play a creature spell, and if you do, you can draw a card. Since I'm such a cheapskate and don't like to pay mana for anything, I quickly hit on Tangleroot, which would cover the mana requirement of the Avatar's ability. After a few failed attempts with decks based around Shrieking Drake, I eschewed the nine-card combos and went instead with my first love: Elves. The only thing better than an Elf is a cantripping Elf. My first draft used Words of Wind, with the hope that I could play and replay an Elvish Pioneer and bounce all of my opponent's permanents. Unfortunately, it never really worked out. It required too many cards, too much mana, and was too easily disrupted to be effective. Instead, I went with Words of Wilding and Words of Worship (which turns all of your creatures into little Loxodon Hierarchs). Here's the deck I ended up submitting:

If I had to do it all over again, I think I would touch Red for some “reach.” Words of Wilding works very well in the deck, but the deck already makes a ton of creatures so the extra Bears aren't really needed. They're just more of the same. Words of War, meanwhile, would allow me to send a Shock to the dome every time I played an Elf. In my testing, this happened pretty frequently. Using Words of War is like making a Bear token with Haste. The fact that you can aim the Words at creatures too, is also important. Otherwise, you might flat-out lose to a Goblin Sharpshooter.

The other thing I'd gain from adding Red is Burning Wish. Since we were building decks without sideboards for a largely unexplored format, having a toolbox full of answers is extremely useful, allowing you to win games you would other lose. I really had no idea what other people would be playing, and my “metagame predictions” were wildly off-base. (To give you an idea of what I'm talking about, I almost maindecked Llanowar Knights!) All I knew was that Mark Gottlieb was playing Dragons, and I joked that I was going to put Rashida Scalebane and Dragonstalker in the sideboard as Living Wish targets. ### To a shadow of a doubt … and beyond!

As the Ask Wizards question from Monday notes, getting the card-draw effect usually adds about two generic mana to the mana cost of the spell. Just compare Trained Armodon and Kavu Climber, Stone Rain and Implode, Unsummon and Repulse, Lightning Bolt and Ember Shot. Okay, skip that last one.

(A quick aside: A friend lost a multiplayer free-for-all game to an Ember Shot. So long as I live, he will never hear the end of it.)

When it comes to cantripping, some colours are just better at it than others. *Ravnica* Block is infused with some nice cantrips, most of them Blue, Black, or both. Repeal, Remand, and honourary cantrip, Ribbons of Night, are all top-notch cards worthy of our attention. Quicken is a neat trick which allows you live in the Blue Mage's Paradise, the End-of-Turn Step. The *Ravnica* cantrip that gets me the most jazzed is Shadow of Doubt. Half Stifle and half Counterspell, Shadow of Doubt can effectively counter Sakura-Tribe Elder activations, fetchlands, tutors of all kinds, and cards like Cranial Extraction.

![](http://www.wizards.com/global/images/mtgcom_daily_cm30_picMain_en.jpg)It seems like a purely reactive card, but after doing some Gatherer searches, I found that you can also use it proactively. It's a totally new paradigm! For one thing, you can neutralize the drawback of Hired Giant (blah). No lands for you, opponent! You can break the symmetry of Oath of Lieges and Verdant Succession (double blah). Arcum Dagsson allows for some interesting possibilities, transforming into a Blue Visara, the Dreadful if your opponent is playing with Artifact creatures (which can be arranged). Even better than Mr. Dagsson is *Ice Age*'s Jester's Mask. In conjunction with Shadow of Doubt, the Mask will let you put your opponent's hand on top of his or her library, but when it comes time to find the replacement cards, you won't be able to. You can get the same effect from *Onslaught*'s Head Games. It's a little more mana-intensive, but it's available Online. As devastating as the combo can be, it still seems like a long way to go for a Mind Twist (even if it's a cantrip Mind Twist).

By far the best card to pair with Shadow of Doubt is *Dissension*'s pseudo-Strip Mine, Ghost Quarter. A two-card combo that destroys a single land isn't that exciting. It'd be much better if you could do it every turn. Luckily, *Mirrodin* Block powerhouses, Isochron Scepter and *10th Edition*-inductee Crucible of Worlds allow you to pull off the trick every turn. Even in the absence of a Shadow of Doubt on a stick, you can still use Ghost Quarter and Crucible of Worlds to colour-screw your opponent. Just focus on one colour and over time you can completely eliminate your opponent's ability to cast spells of that colour.

Isochron Scepter is, at worst, a half-priced Jayemdae Tome. If you can't find a Shadow of Doubt, go ahead and put Remand on the Scepter. I assure you, it's annoying. Quicken is also a decent card to Imprint. One especially nifty trick is to use the card-draw of your Quicken-on-a-stick to Dredge back Nightmare Void. The Void is a Sorcery, which you can now play at instant speed. How convenient! End of turn Coercions are pretty exciting, even if they do cost six-mana. Muddle the Mixture is the only other Imprint-able card, but it can also be used to Transmute for Shadow of Doubt or Isochron Scepter. (Naturally, there are many other cards that work well when on a stick. Boomerang, Counterspell, Orim's Chant, etc.)

The deck wins either by decking your opponent with Haunting Echoes, Duskmantle, House of Shadows, and Cephalid Coliseum recursion, or by Stalking Stones / Urami beatdown. It's not fast, but if your opponent can't keep a land on the table, you can take your time.

The deck has some weaknesses. For instance, it's possible that you could lose the game to a turn 1 Nimble Mongoose, since the only “good” answer is to block with Stalking Stones. I'd consider cutting Ribbons of Night for Chainer's Edict and Head Games for something like Barter in Blood. ### And now for something radically dissimilar to the preceding

Since I've been building Standard Tribal Wars decks for several weeks now, I think it's time that I addressed a certain elephant in the room. No, not Babar. I'm not even talking about Loxodon Hierarch. I'm talking about Spirits. They're such a big tribe, and form the basis for some tournament-worthy decks, easily adapted to the Standard Tribal Wars format. I've been avoiding them for a while, but I think they can still be fun to play.

With the smaller tribes, the key is to find the best support cards. You won't have many choices to make to establish your creature-base. With my Centaur deck, I decided to take advantage of Dowsing Shaman and filled the deck with Enchantments that could kill creatures. With my Assassins deck, I complemented all the assassinating with a little graverobbing, courtesy of Nezumi Graverobber and Crime of Crime // Punishment.

With the larger tribes, like Spirits or Humans, you have a little more flexibility. You can choose a build-around-me card as your starting point and figure out the creature-base from there. As I mentioned before, I've played against a Warp World deck that used Elves and a Wildfire deck that used Zuberas. This week, the card I'm going to build around is Clash of Realities. The Clash turns all of your Spirits into little Flametongue Kavus. Obviously, the Clashes are basically dead cards against other Spirit decks. But who plays those?

The downside to Clash of Realities is that it turns all of your opponent's creatures into little Flametongue Kavus, too. Luckily, there are a few ways to get around this drawback. Since the Enchantment grants the ability to the creature that's coming in to play, Protection from Red won't help you much (unless of course the creature coming into play is Red). There aren't any Spirits with Protection from Red anyway, so that's a bit of a dead end. There is, however, a Spirit with Protection from Monocolored (Guardian of the Guildpact). Unless the creature coming into play is multicoloured, the Guardian will be immune to Clash of Realities. It's also a fine Defender and shrugs off a lot of targeted removal. Blinking Spirit, meanwhile, is very tricky to kill, what with its blinking and all. With its ability to return to your hand at will, you can also use it to trigger Clash of Realities repeatedly.

Clash of Realities is a “may” ability, meaning that you (or your opponent) can elect not to use it. Why would your opponent not want to off one of your creatures? Maybe that creature is Yosei, the Morning Star or maybe you have a Promise of Bunrei on the table. Either way, your opponent will be in trouble, effectively losing his next turn or losing many of his creatures.
