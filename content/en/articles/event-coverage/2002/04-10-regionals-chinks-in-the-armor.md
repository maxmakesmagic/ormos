Danny Mandel's article ["Is Black-Blue-Green worth it?"](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/03-27-is-black-blue-green-worth-it) offered some great advice for a diverse field: build proactively. In a well-established and narrow metagame, you can sometimes come up with a hand-grenade that beats rock, paper and scissors. The more diverse the field, the less likely it is that this approach will work, both because there are too many strategies to be countered by a single answer and because you are likely to face strategies you didn't consider, and get crushed.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>Build a deck that plays its own game and plays it well, regardless of what the other player is doing.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

The solution, as Danny suggested, is to build a deck that plays its own game and plays it well, regardless of what the other player is doing. That means emphasizing proactive cards (Call of the Herd) over reactive cards (Disenchant). I would add that flexible reactive cards (Vindicate, Counterspell) are better than narrow ones (Ghastly Demise, Disenchant), since they are more likely to have a useful target.

But is that the end of the story? Is our ability to metagame limited to building Mike Flores's Decks to Beat, Kibler's decks, or some other gauntlet and playing "good" decks against it?

![](https://i.ormos.uk/18/5e/185e7e318427f84c96bea9f51b1f21e9072b32c5ab59b80ef3c558e66c2e38e9.jpg)Of course not...even when a metagame is wide open, there are themes that can be identified among the best decks. Sometimes it's specific cards that are so unbalanced they will show up in any deck capable of playing them. Flametongue Kavu is so good that Kibler's monoblack control deck added 8 non-basic lands to support four copies. Sometimes it's a whole category of spells. Zvi Mowshowitz won a Pro Tour partly because he recognized that virtually all the good removal in *Invasion* Block was red. Pro Tour-Osaka's metagame was certainly affected by the lack of good permission, making it possible for monoblack decks to run a relatively small number of extremely powerful spells, knowing that few, if any, could be countered.

In any case, where there's a theme, there is often a card or strategy that is good against it, and that can give you an edge on the field. Themes also provide constraints; that is, when a card or strategy is going to be popular, you need to avoid cards and strategies that are poor against it. So what themes can we identify in Standard leading up to Regionals? Here are a few:

**Creatures.** None of the decks to beat are truly creatureless, and at least two archetypes that are likely to be popular (r-g and b-x) have a lot of creatures. Psychatog and Balancing Tings are the exceptions, but given the troubles they have with Kibler Black, I think they are likely to be in the minority. The combination of Braids and Chainer's Edict also make life tough for any rogue decks with few creatures, so you can be pretty confident that most of your opponents at Regionals will be playing with men.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/c9/07/c907675a7eb027009b9b82c14778b1313f325e25d765cb479da1b293b37c2e79.jpg"><br><i>It can do 3, but it's really a 1/1.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**One-Toughness Creatures.** When has a metagame been so full of weenies? 60% of Frog in a Blender's creatures have a toughness of one, with the rest beefing it up at two. Half of Kibler Black's creatures have a toughness of one, although Ichorid has a tendency not to stay dead and Nantuko Shade pumps. Steve OMS's gateway and Masters r-g decks have bigger offensive creatures, but his early game is all about Birds and Elves letting the big guys come out to play. Flores didn't include u-g in his decks to beat, but Kibler's version and the ones I've seen online all use cards like Merfolk Looter and Werebear, both for synergy and to help the deck get away with a modest land count.

**Nonbasic Lands.** The only monocolor deck we're likely to see a fair amount of at Regionals is monoblack, i.e. a Standard version of Initial Public Coffering. Even that may not show up, since the power of the Coffers may not be that enticing with Kibler Black hitting you with Braids and with splashing offering so much. (I've heard a lot more people talking about alternate splash colors for Kibler-style decks than talking about going monoblack.) This shouldn't be surprising. With pain lands for every color combination, the dual lands from *Invasion*, the b-x lands from *Torment* as well as Lairs and filter lands, adding a second color is easy. With powerful gold cards from *Invasion* Block and Wizards of the Coast doing a pretty good job of returning each color to their traditional weaknesses, the value of adding a second (or third) color is fairly high. And, aside from mana-smoothing, Standard offers powerful utility lands like Barbarian Ring, Centaur Garden, Cabal Coffers and Deserted Temple.

**Flametongue Kavu.** That slight improvement on Viashino Warrior is still good enough to be an environment-affecting card. Every red deck will run four, and Kibler won't be the only person who adds red solely or primarily to run a 4/2 Nekrataal.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/1a/a0/1aa06258532d7f6f71a996189d6adac7143fefba8ede14d9122c0935fb3cb849.jpg"><br><i>Creature-light decks have trouble with the Edict.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**Braids and Chainer's Edict.** While most decks will run neither of these cards, they are still enough to present a design constraint. Any deck that wants to play land-go for the first few turns, has some serious thinking to do about Braids, especially since the Braids decks will be backed up by Duress and Mesmeric Fiend. You can't assume you'll be able to counter her, so your deck has to be able to cope. That's not to say that you have to build a deck that crushes "Turn one, Duress, turn-two Fiend, turn four Braids," but you shouldn't automatically scoop to it either. Chainer's Edict, meanwhile, is a serious headache for decks that rely on gaining board control and then winning with a Psychatog or Enforcer.

So these are the themes, the potential chinks in the armor of today's Standard environment. What weapons can we bring to bear to take advantage of them?

Let's start with the assumption that creatures, particularly X/1s, will be popular. The first implication of this is that dedicated creature removal spells are a lot less risky than other reactive spells, like Disenchant. It also means that you should strongly consider cards that crush weenies.

Removal that is good against weenies needs to be cheap and/or kill two at once. Vindicate is a good spell, but you don't want it to be your answer to a r-g deck's early creatures. Shock, Fire/Ice, Innocent Blood and Chainer's Edict are all modestly costed removal spells. Mutilate and Wrath of God are good too, because they hopefully make up for the early damage they let through with card advantage.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/13/b0/13b0da5230c449cee6fe30e4b6ce89d863a5dd60847503907ca07155b4e42713.jpg"><br><i>Show a couple of lousy elves what's what.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

My favorite candidate at the moment is Engulfing Flames. This card is simply unfair vs. many r-g builds. Against Frog in a Blender, it kills 60% of their creatures for just one mana - and it kills them at instant speed so you can wait for them to Reckless Charge or pump their Rootwalla. Only Wild Mongrel survives if you flash it back. Against any mid-range r-g deck, Engulfing Flames is incredible mana suppression. Steve OMS ran just 22 lands in both his r-g decks, and that's probably typical. It's also not nearly enough if their Birds and Elves die. The fact that Flames can kill a mana creature on turn one and then come back and kill another one later is amazing.

Against Kibler Black, Engulfing Flames is at least decent. You can discard it to Rats and kill them later with no card disadvantage. (That can be important if they play Braids and are looking for surplus permanents to feed her.) Once it's in the graveyard it protects your hand from Mesmeric Fiend. It also forces Kibler Black to keep mana open to protect Nantuko Shade or risk losing it. Later on, it can hold off Ichorid for two turns, or take out Braids at instant speed.

And don't forget, if they're running Nightscape Familiar or Spectral Lynx to hold off your creatures, Engulfing Flames isn't so bad either.

Jay Schneider put two Flames in the board of Frog. Steve OMS didn't board any for the Gateway but boarded three for the Masters. If you think that decks like r-g, u-g and Kibler Black are going to be popular at your Regionals, you could seriously consider running some number of Engulfing Flames main. I guarantee I'll play at least three between main deck and sideboard if I play red at Regionals.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>The Judo strategy to playing in a creature-heavy environment is to play with no creatures of your own.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

The Judo strategy to playing in a creature-heavy environment is to play with no creatures of your own. The logic is that everyone else will be packing removal spells that will be dead against you, while you can use global removal spells like Wrath of God to kill multiple spells of theirs while losing none of your own. Some versions of this approach run zero creatures (e.g. u-w Millstone), while others run just a few finishers.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/04/d7/04d72164c3fe7ac43dc3b5a578612b446f34892be4c724744ab9a27d16fb796d.jpg"><br><i>Question: Can you deal with this card?</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

The trick to building a deck like that will be to take advantage on one theme (lots of creatures) while not getting wrecked by another (Braids/Edict). Dan McDonnough is a strong deckbuilder at YMG, and his response to Braids/Edict was to give his u-w-g deck more creatures, with Meddling Mages, Mystic Snake and Call of the Herd in addition to Enforcers. Another approach would be to keep the number of creatures small, but to use Dwell on the Past. Just two copies would give the deck a big late game advantage against other control decks - the fact that you don't get to draw a card as with Gaea's Blessing hurts more in the early game than later on. It would also basically eliminate the strategic problem of Chainer's Edict, since you could just keep recycling your threats until your opponent ran out of Edicts. Two slots would, over a long game, provide an unbounded number of creatures.

U-w Millstone has to address the Braids question directly. Can you reliably survive if Braids hits play? For conventional builds, which avoid low casting-cost permanents in favor of cantrips and permission, the answer is probably no. So how do you respond?

First of all, you probably want to add Diamonds (either Sky or Marble). These would be good in an environment of aggressive weenies anyway, since permission is often a poor answer to quick creatures. You want to develop your mana quickly in order to be able to cast Wrath of God or Teferi's Moat. Adding Diamonds greatly reduces the danger that an early Braids will leave you stuck on mana. If you play a Diamond on turn two, you can always Wrath away Braids, even if you're playing second.

Please note, I'm not advocating u-w Millstone. I don't think it's a good choice for this weekend, and Braids is only one hurdle among many it has to overcome. I'm just using it as an illustration of how any deck can be tuned to fit the themes of a given environment.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/11/6f/116f7599864f608a60a5ff62f1c4b1043215b47c4861f03e84ec71c79c044c60.jpg"><br><i>Nonbasics are operating in a consequence-free environment.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Now let's look at nonbasic lands. I suspect that this is an example of a theme that's difficult to take advantage of. Nonbasics are popular, but not so dominant that a Domain deck with Destructive Flow would have a potential auto-win against most of the field. OMS r-g runs a lot of nonbasics, but with 13 basic lands and 8 mana-creatures, even a turn-three Flow won't be completely devastating. Nor are most decks putting so much strain on their mana bases that targeting them will be crushing. Decks are running lots of nonbasic lands because they can, rather than because they have to, so adding Rancid Earth to your black deck isn't likely to push it over the top.

The environment is also short of ways to punish nonbasic lands. Wasteland is gone, as is Dust Bowl. A monoblue deck with maindeck Back to Basics could be a powerful force at Regionals if it didn't keep losing games to opponents responding with, "Judge!" Maybe an aggressive red deck could look at Lava Blister, but I doubt that it's good enough. As far as I know, I'm the only player who has tried to build a deck around Rith's Charm.

Finally we have Flametongue Kavu. This, like Braids and Chainer's Edict, is more of a constraint than a potential chink. The likely popularity of Flametongues simply means that some cards that would otherwise have been worth a look aren't. A good example would be Thought Devourer. A cheap flying fattie is clearly powerful, and a g-u deck that dropped a few quick weenies and then dropped a Devourer with a bounce spell in hand would be asking some pretty difficult questions. With no Flametongue Kavu, that's an avenue I would seriously explore. With the Flametongue, however, a single card takes out the flyer and the biggest ground-based threat, and suddenly the bounce spell isn't much of an answer. The fact that Flametongue Kavu can take down an entire Beast Attack (taking four points from one but spending six mana less) shows how much less fun it is to play with 4/4s than it might otherwise have been.

There are several good Standard decks out there to choose from, or you may have built your own rogue creation. Whichever you go with, as you're tuning your main deck and choosing your sideboard, a careful look at common themes in the metagame can help you find the best cards for the field.
