Well, this is it. As I sit down to write this report, I'm on a train pulling away from Eindhoven station and headed for the airport in Amsterdam. There I will board a plane back to Atlanta, where I will set foot on American soil for the first time in over three weeks. My European tour is finally at an end. It started out rocky, fraught with jet lag and illness at Worlds in Berlin where I posted a disappointing finish one win out of the money. While my eventual finish at Grand Prix-London was rather frustrating as well, I can at least head home satisfied that my trip wasn't altogether a failure. Ninth place stings, to be sure, but it's money in my pocket and the first two Pro Tour points toward the brass ring – the title of Player of the Year, and that juicy $20,000 end-of-season check that goes with it.

In any case, when last we left our intrepid hero – and by that I mean me – he had just left Berlin with the Dutch contingent and headed back to Holland to spend the interim period between the World Championships and Grand Prix-London. The first week that I was in Eindhoven – crashing on the floor of a very generous Victor van den Broek – the country was experiencing some kind of heat wave. A heat wave in Holland, it seems, means about the same weather as the typical summer in Atlanta, so one wouldn't expect it would bother me so much. Unfortunately, most of Holland lacks air conditioning, a luxury to which those of us in the American south have become accustomed, even dependent upon. While Victor finished up his last week of exams, I spent the days trying to keep cool. Just because I used to wrestle doesn't mean I enjoy sweating that much.

By the end of the week, though, the weather cooled down and – with Victor's exams over – the action heated up. Eindhoven might not be a particularly large city, nor one with particularly active nightlife, but that weekend goes down as one of the more memorable I've had in recent history. Maybe it had something to do with seeing entire stages full of dancing Dutch gamers, from Vic to Jeroen Remie to Frank Karsten to Jelger Wiegersma. Maybe it was because we started off every night in an Australia-themed bar called the Krazy Kangaroo that sold one euro shooters – which nearly killed Vic one night when he decided to go one for one with me – or maybe it was the fact that the Dutch can apparently turn any song into Happy Hardcore, even the likes of 10,000 Maniacs and Bryan Adams. Whatever it was, the weekend flew by in a blur, and suddenly it was Monday and I still didn't have a deck for the Grand Prix.

I'd done a bit of testing, of course, but I wasn't happy with anything I had so far. During the day on Saturday, Jelger and Jeroen and Frank came over to Vic's to do some testing, which basically amounted to Jelger and Frank playing the r-w mirror all afternoon while I tried out everything from u-r to g-b to u-g. None of these decks were very impressive against both Goblins and w-r, so I kept searching. Chatting on IRC with one Gabriel Nassif lead me to look into u-w, and when Antonino De Rosa mentioned that a similar deck was doing well in Italian qualifiers, my curiosity was piqued further. When I heard that Eric Froehlich won a PTQ in Maryland with the deck that very weekend, as well, I decided to devote all of my testing time to learning and tuning the deck as much as possible. I'm going to devote an article to analyzing the deck itself, so I won't go into much detail here, but after playing and winning dozens of test matches on Magic Online and Apprentice against everything from w-r to Goblins to Zombie Bidding to monowhite control, I was the picture of confidence heading into the Grand Prix.

I traveled from Eindhoven to London with VicVdB, Jelger Wiegersma, Frank Karsten, and Vic's housemate Eva, who was just coming along for the trip. After a forty five minute flight, an hour on the train from Stansted Airport, and at least another forty five minutes of bumbling navigation on the Underground, we finally arrived at the site to meet Ben Seck, our host for the weekend. Our motley crew staying at Ben's cousin's Notting Hill flat included five Dutchmen, an Australian, a Canadian (their national champ, no less!), and an American. That may sound like a recipe for a really bad joke and in reality it almost was. Hardly anyone in the flat got much sleep that night, as most of us were up late putting together our decks and loudly discussing the merits of Fog Guy – whom you might know as Frontline Strategist – as a sideboard card against goblins. It wasn't long before our theoretical morph sideboard included Willbender, Daru Mender, Liege of the Axe, and the mighty Daru Lancer – because of his synergy with Fog Guy, of course. Who needs Exalted Angel?

I kept a Fog Guy sleeved up in my pocket, and before the tournament actually started I had him in my deck box and pulled him out when people asked what I was playing. I don't know how many people I actually convinced with my little game, but it was amusing to see people's reactions – that and to hear the cries of “Fooooog Guuuuy!” going up seemingly at random throughout the tournament hall.

I spend my byes getting some breakfast with Alex Shvartsman and Ed Fear, the two other YMG players to come across the pond. Unfortunately, I hadn't gotten in touch with them before the event, and for some inexplicable reason they decided to play Bad Form. Personally, I prefer playing decks that actually have at least one good matchup in the entire field, but to each his own, I suppose. After getting a nice full English breakfast in me, I head back to the site to take a look at the field and wait for fourth round pairings to go up.

### Round 4 – Kai Budde

Just the pairing I always want for the first round of any tournament. Kai and I sat down at the Feature Match tables long before they announced for us to do so, and lo and behold, we were featured. Shocking, isn't it? I was surprised to see Kai playing goblins, since he so detests playing creature decks, but I was in no place to question his choice after he mauled me in two straight games. My draws were quite poor and he won the die roll – sometimes that's all it takes. For more details, check out the Feature Match coverage [here](/en/events/2003/gplon03/feature-match-round-4-brian-kibbler-vs-kai-budde).

Simon seemed somewhat confused when I sat down across from him, asking “Didn't you have three byes?” I confirmed and told him that I'd played Kai last round, and suddenly everything seemed to make sense. Simon was playing r-w, and in game one did not play an early Lightning Rift – that's another way of saying he lost. He tried to cycle Decree of Annihilation at one point, but my response was the float fifteen mana. I didn't mana burn. Game two was somewhat more interesting than the first, with a turn two Silver Knight coming down on his side, followed by a morph. I Stifled his Eternal Dragon landcycle, as he'd mulliganed and I felt I could likely keep him off of sufficient mana, and it seemed to work since he stalled and couldn't flip his Angel right away. I played a morph of my own and he Oblationed it to maintain tempo – and he laughed out loud when I revealed it to be Daru Sanctifier.
