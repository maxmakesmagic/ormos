In an attempt to shift the blame for my recent poor performances in draft, I have been working on a theory that it's not how good a drafter I am it's how good a drafter the people around me are. Even though I deftly try to avoid the colors of my neighbors, they blindly trespass into mine. By misreading signals and making drafting errors, they hurt their own game, but since booster draft is so tight-knit, their every mistake has a ripple effect that harms those around them causing us both to lose, while the other side of the table walks away with the prizes. There is actually discussion on the Pro Tour on whether it's better to be beside a good player or a bad one in draft. If you're beside Kai then you know he's going to try and signal his colors and stay out of yours. If you're beside a random player they may pass you a great card that they shouldn't have, but by doing so they send you the wrong signal and you're both screwed.

In an attempt to help everyone, I'd like to outline some tricks and tips for drafting and signaling using the *Odyssey* block for examples. I would also like to bring up some difficult situations and choices that every drafter will face that have no correct answer. Hopefully by making people aware that they are in the situation, they can read the draft better and make more informed choices.

### The Early Signals

The most important pack for signaling is the first one that is passed to you. It gives you the clearest information and the earliest opportunity to take advantage of it. Most people have an emotional link to their first pick and stick to that color despite the draft clearly indicating they shouldn't, so knowing what they first-picked is even more relevant than it should be. Here are a few quick easy things to keep in mind when reviewing the early packs:

### 1) Rarity of the Already-Drafted Card

As I have mentioned in the past (["Faerie Squadron or Duskwalker"](https://web.archive.org/web/20020715000000/http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/article.asp?x=sb20010309a) on the *Sideboard* last March), it is very important to review the rarity of the card missing from the pack passed to you. If you get an *Odyssey* pack with a Wild Mongrel in it and there's one common card missing, then you know that the player to your right is not playing green because the best *Odyssey* green common is Wild Mongrel. Similarly, if there's a Ghastly Demise, Aven Windreader, Firebolt or Mystic Zealot and a missing common, you can almost be positive that they're not playing those colors.

If there's a Wild Mongrel but an uncommon was taken, then the signal is less clear. They could have taken a Roar of the Wurm or Overrun or Beast Attack. You can take the Mongrel, but keep a watchful eye out on future packs. If there's a rare already drafted, then it depends on the color. In the case of *Odyssey* green, there are very few green rare cards better than Wild Mongrel - only Call of the Herd comes immediately to mind. But in *Odyssey* black there are up to a half dozen rare cards that people will take over Ghastly Demise such as Cabal Patriarch and Stalking Bloodsucker.

This is where **Magic** Online and real drafting comes in very helpful over building your own draft packs or using Netdraft. If you open enough *Odyssey* packs, you eventually come to memorize small portions of the print runs and will know that Wild Mongrel and Aven Windreader often come together. If you see one and not the other and there's a missing common card, it's very likely that the person ahead of you is in the other color. Similarly, Werebear and Aven Windreader often come together, so if you see the Bear and a common has been drafted, you know the person on your right is likely into blue. It's best to memorize the sequences around the top cards in each color as listed above.

Print run information can also be of great help later in the draft. In *Odyssey*, Muscle Burst and Krosan Archer often come in the same pack, yet both are decent picks. A novice drafter will get a late Muscle Burst or Archer and think green is available, but the more experience drafter will pass it along knowing that it is a false signal and just means someone upstream took the Archer instead. Dusk Imp and Morgue Theft are other solid cards that often seem to come with better black cards such as Morbid Hunger or Dirty Wererat.

### 3) Look for Common Foils

This trick doesn't work when you're at the highest level event where the packs are pre-opened and the foils removed, but is definitely effective at lower level event such as PTQs, store drafts and **Magic** Online. The existence of a common foil card is often a signal that a normal version of the card was also in the pack. The problem with this strategy is that players normally take the foil version first because it's worth more! What players should do if they really want to send the right signal is take the non-foil. When the person you're passing to sees a foil Repel, she'll know that someone has likely already taken a Repel from the pack and hopefully stay away from blue. If they haven't read this article though, then you've just cost yourself a foil! This technique is risky not only because you may lose out on a foil - if the person you're passing a decent foil to doesn't realize what it means, then they will draft the Repel and be in your colors and taking your cards. If you feel your draft table is not of high quality, it is often best to pass both Repels for a weaker card in a different color and let the next two drafters fight it out among themselves.

### The Later Signals

After the first few packs the signals become much more difficult to read and even more difficult to respond to. The signals become much more vague and hard to interpret - instead of "she's playing blue" it becomes "is blue over-drafted?" 

### 1) The "Second Best" Trap

The fundamental knowledge that all top drafters must have before they go into the draft is what cards are really good (first tier) and what cards are solid and playable (second tier). A common drafting error is to play a color and get a pick in that color each pack, but still end up with a horrible deck or a deck that's weaker than the remainder of the table. This is because you were getting leftovers in each pack. Once again, the most important cards are the commons because you can be sure that in the first three to four packs at least a few of the top commons should make an appearance. The top black commons in *Odyssey* packs are probably Ghastly Demise, Afflict and Morbid Hunger with Patriarch's Desire and Dirty Wererat right behind them. If you get passed Demise, Desire, Wererat and Afflict, it is a very clear signal that top picks are getting through the players on your right and arriving in your hands. If instead you get Demise, Gravedigger, Dusk Imp and Whispering Shade, it's likely that someone is plucking all the good stuff right ahead of you. A bad drafter thinks the person on their right isn't drafting black, a good drafter realizes it and uses the information to their advantage. While this isn't so important with black due to the *Torment* coming from the left, if your first few picks are Windreader, Cephalid Scout, Syncopate and Peek, I can guarantee that you won't be seeing many Wormfang Drakes in *Judgment*.

The key is to notice that you're getting leftovers before it's too late. Sometimes there's nothing you can do about it - taking Mystic Visionary as your first white card over the fourth pick Peek is hardly an effective fix. But if you're passing Firebolt to stick to green for the Leaf Dancer or Refresh, then you're probably making a mistake and should consider switching colors.

An even more complicated situation is where you are getting first tier picks in your early picks, but you're still sharing your colors with players nearby. If the person on your right picked Cabal Patriarch, Painbringer and Childhood Horror, then it's understandable that you may have received the run of first tier black commons listed above. These situations are extremely difficult to extract yourself from and more often than not, you're just screwed. What you have to watch for is a sudden drop in the quality of the cards you're getting passed, as it would be extremely unlikely that every pack was that strong in a single color. So if you find that there are no more black cards getting passed, even second or third tier cards, then it likely indicates that someone near you is in black or that black is heavily drafted at the table.

### 2) The "Color Depth" Trap

While it's nice to see two first-pick green cards get passed your way, it's also important to notice whether you're getting any late-pick green cards. You may not have any green mages to your immediate right, but there could still be four other green mages at the table. Sometimes, at the end of the first pack, you'll find you have drafted two Wild Mongrels and absolutely no other tier one or tier two green cards. The key to avoiding this situation, as with all the others, is realizing it early. If you haven't seen a third playable green card by your sixth pick, but a bunch of off-color cards are coming through, it may be time to switch colors and just accept that your first two picks were wasted. There's a chance that you'll get decent green cards in the second pack, but you already know you'll be cut off in the third set. And if there really are four other green mages a the table, it is unlikely that you'll get more than two or three quality green cards from the left, even if you have totally cut it off. It is a well-known draft rule that you don't want to be playing the colors of the person on your right because they have the most effect on you and they are the easiest people to get signals from. But it's almost as important that you're not drafting an overdrafted color - you never want to be the fourth or fifth player in a color if you could be the second or third instead (outside of specific color strengths in a particular set).

While finding out that you're in an overdrafted color is a matter of cutting your losses, the opposite situation of finding an underdrafted color is all about taking a risk hoping for great rewards. Underdrafted colors are only noticeable when you're not in that color, otherwise you're just reaping the rewards and trying not to smile at the seventh pick Ember Beast. The problem with underdrafted colors is that they are difficult to notice until late in the first pack and sometimes not until the second pack. Recently at Canadian Nationals there were no decent red cards coming through for me in *Odyssey*, but all of a sudden *Torment* was filled with Violent Eruptions, Fiery Tempers and Sonic Seizures. I assumed that someone to my right had cut off all the red and were now getting rewarded. It turned out that there was only one red drafter at the table after *Odyssey* because the early pickings were so weak and that drafter had done an excellent job of taking every decent red card he saw.

Taking advantage of an underdrafted color becomes a judgment call in each draft. If you're already heavily committed to two colors with solid cards and positioning in each, then there's little benefit to risking it all when you get passed a Violent Eruption or see a late Reckless Charge. If you haven't picked a second color yet, then you're in great shape as these are the signals you've been waiting for. It's when you have a main color and two or three decent cards in a second that you're faced with a make or break decision. If it's the seventh or eighth pick in the first set then it's a low-risk pick - you're not likely costing yourself much by taking it and seeing what comes in the second set. But if it's the second *Torment* pack and you're faced with a choice between *Compulsion* and Volcanic Eruption or Cephalid Sage and Fiery Temper and you've already picked a few blue cards as your second color, then you have a tough decision to make. Will red keep coming through or is it just a one-time pass because the player immediately to your left isn't in the color?

There are no easy answers to this sort of decision and you'll kick yourself if you choose wrong and end up passing lots of good cards or end up with the Temper in your sideboard. It's all a matter of risk tolerance. If you're playing green and think you can easily splash the card as a third color if nothing else comes through, and the card is worth splashing, then take the plunge. If you just need to go 1-1-1 to make Top 8, then you may want to hold back and play the consistent deck. But if you need to 3-0 and your deck isn't looking too solid, then reach for the stars and hope that things go your way.

In the draft itself, the best indication that you can look for regarding underdrafted colors is to watch the third-tier cards circling the table in the first set. If Halberdier, Frenetic Ogre and Anarchist were circling the table forever and you ended up hate-drafting one eleventh, then it's likely that red is a bit under-drafted. Alternatively, if you saw no red cards at all, then you know that the drafters to your left didn't see very many either and it's unlikely that they are in red as well. This will result in you getting the best pick of the red cards for the first four or five picks in the second set. However this can backfire tremendously if the reason you saw no red cards was because the persons to your right were taking them all. You will receive no red cards in the third set and end up with only four or five very good red cards in all, which is too much for a splash, but not really enough for a second color. All of a sudden you're in three colors and your deck is very messy. The second-pack color shift is a very dangerous undertaking!

### 3) The "Inconsistent Signal" Trap

Cards are randomly seeded in packs and with any random endeavor, there are going to be packs that are on the extremely unlikely side. A difficult situation for the novice drafter is when one pack is extremely strong or has four or five decent cards in a single color. Our rookie drafter takes a look at the eighth pack and sees an Elephant Ambush and Seton's Desire staring her in the face and assumes that this is a signal that green is underdrafted, despite not seeing any decent green cards in the five previous packs. What she didn't see was the bomb rare, two strong uncommons and four other good playable commons in the pack that led the other seven drafters to leave the Ambush for her. Or they she didn't see the Call of the Herd, Beast Attack, Rabid Elephant and Firebolt that the other four green players (one green-red) picked up for their decks. With four other green players, green will be overdrafted leaving our rookie with a weak deck or a wasted green pick plus any follow-up wasted green picks before she realizes the color is overdrafted. When you see a late signal it's almost always false and should be ignored. The only time when you may switch colors and take the gift pick is if the card is first tier and/or you've seen a lot of second tier cards in that color going by and you think it may just be a case of weak packs being opened and so no one else is in the color yet.

When the reverse happens, and there are no red cards in the first two packs other than Molten Influence just because the packs were very weak for red, it's very difficult to take advantage of it, but the rewards can be high. Effectively the color has been cut off for you, so if you realize that this is what happened, perhaps because there is a series of really good red cards right after that, you can almost guarantee getting lots of quality cards later. This is similar to noticing that a color is underdrafted. The problem, as with the underdrafted strategy, is that if you don't realize it immediately, then the person downstream of you may realize it and ruin everything. Say you've been drafting a monoblue deck so far and there are no white cards in the first two packs passed to you. Suddenly you get passed a Mystic Zealot and a Syncopate. Since no other white cards have come through, you are inclined to think that this was just a strong pack for white, so you pass the Zealot and take the inferior blue card. But then you get passed another Zealot. Two Zealots is a consistent signal, so it's now pretty apparent that white is underdrafted and the first two packs were just statistical anomalies. But you've just passed a fourth pick Zealot to the person on your left! What do you do? There is no hard and fast answer to this fairly common situation, and it can result in some weird situations. Sometimes everyone just keeps passing the Zealots along until they finally get picked up sixth or seventh, other times both players take a Zealot and suddenly the underdrafted color is being fiercely contested. It's these kind of decisions that can make or break a draft for someone and is a strong argument for keeping your colors flexible early (preferably by just choosing one) so you can take advantage of late picks in underdrafted colors.

### Conclusion

While I've loosely based this advice on Odyssey block draft, it applies equally well to all draft sets. Just be aware that the sets themselves may change the relative importance of certain traps and signals. Taking the foil Mongrel and passing the non-foil may be okay when you don't care if the person on your left is playing green because *Torment* is so weak. Avoiding black because you're only getting second-tier cards in *Odyssey* is an amazing idea in *Odyssey-Odyssey-Torment* draft, but it's a lot less important in *Odyssey-Torment-Judgement*.

Also be aware that more than one set of signals and traps may apply in the same draft. You may find that a color is under-represented due to a mixture of trap #2 and trap #3. This is where experience, practice and skill come in as you try and be a fortune-teller and interpret what the cards reveal about your future. Of course, the best precognition in the world won't help you if the other people at the table are inexperienced and taking the less powerful cards or rare-drafting or drafting four colors. Advice for those situations will have to come at a later date! But if you're at a table with experienced drafters, or you find yourself drafting beside me somewhere, hopefully this article will help clarify the signals and we can congratulate one another while playing the finals.
