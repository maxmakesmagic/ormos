I would like to take a brief intermission from the main series to explore a match of **Magic** hands on and to discuss the merits and the flaws of the mulligan noted in the previous article. This sample match will illustrate how one can overcome even the worst luck. In the following match, I suffered some "unlucky" breaks. Had I played a bit better, I would have likely won regardless. We will look at the principles behind the play in the game as well as the principles involved in deciding when to mulligan. 

The sample in question is a match from the second round of a draft I did on **Magic** Online. The match saw my solid white-black deck pitted versus an opposing green-black deck.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/79/af/79af33945598e7b5de837df933519542b2a6983434bb98a4603653fcaea21ef2.jpg)

We can see that the deck has a few simple strategies that it will try to employ. First, it will attempt to stall the early game with its high-toughness creatures and win after these creatures achieve threshold or greater power/toughness (Mortivore, Mystic Zealot, Dirty Wererat). It can also stall the ground and win the game with a large Mortivore or with its Aven Flocks and Angel of Retribution. Against blue-white decks, it can abuse its utility creatures such as Cabal Torturer and Hallowed Healer to win in combat. The deck is highly dependent on a long, drawn out game to maximize the power of its creatures, since its card advantage is lacking.

### The Mulligan

If you recall from my last article, I discussed a choice to mulligan the following hand: Swamp, Swamp, Skywing Aven, Filthy Cur, Aven Windreader, Dreamwinder, Skeletal Scrying. The situation was drawing versus a blue-white deck.

Before, I had presented a sample replacement hand upon mulliganing. This was merely for convenience of continuing the example, but when we consider our choice to mulligan, we cannot consider this sample hand as any kind of argument in favor of mulliganing. I spoke with a number of pros about the situation I presented, and most disagreed that the hand should be mulliganed, and some agreed.

The hand in question is a very borderline call, but rather than try and definitively say we should mulligan or not mulligan the hand, it is helpful to know how to make these types of decisions. 

First off, a good rule of thumb is that if you draw a one-land hand in an "unwinnable match up," if you get an insane one-land hand, you keep.

We must then consider the number of islands in our deck. If we have 10 islands, we are less likely to mulligan than if we have 9 or 8. We should note that given 9 islands in our deck, we have a 63% chance to draw an island in our first three cards.

> The Math:  
>  There are 33\*32\*31 ways to draw 3 cards=32736.  
>  24\*23\*22 ways not to draw island. =12144  
>  12144/32736=37% chance not to draw an island.

We should also consider the relative strengths of the cards in our hand to the rest of our deck. Therefore, if Skywing Aven and Aven Windreader are some of the better cards in our deck, we would be less likely to mulligan. Also, if we consider the cards we drew especially strong in the match up, we would be less inclined to mulligan. 

Another often-overlooked factor is the relative strength between you and your opponent. If your opponent were significantly weaker than you, you would be inclined to mulligan more often since you feel you can overcome a one-card disadvantage but not a complete mana screw.

There is, of course, the factor of whether or not you are playing or drawing. This is a little more difficult to evaluate however

In the given scenario, you are tempted to keep because you have a good hand and are drawing. However, drawing also means that a mulligan is less detrimental, so you are tempted to mulligan. You think you and your opponent are roughly equal, and you are playing with 9 islands. It comes down to the relative strengths of the cards in your deck. If your hand is strong for your deck, then you should probably keep. If they are weak or average, you should probably mulligan.
