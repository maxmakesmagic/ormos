![](https://i.ormos.uk/a1/c3/a1c32cb4cea706bb480729e50669771d75d76b266de86e82df6e9d8f4d16d6ec.jpg)Wizards of the Coast welcomes Hall of Famer Raphaël Lévy as the featured player here at "Ask the Pro." A fixture on the Pro Tour for the last eight years, Raphaël is uniquely positioned to answer your questions about the life of a professional **Magic** player, give a historical perspective on the game and high-level tournament scene, share stories about travelling the world, and talk about the role **Magic** plays in his life.

Fifth on the lifetime Pro Points list, Raphaël began his Pro Tour career back at Pro Tour-Paris in 1997. He became a regular on the tour starting at the 1998 World Championships. Since then he hasn't missed a Pro Tour, an astounding streak cresting 50 consecutive events. He was recently honored as a member of the [**Magic** Pro Tour Hall of Fame](/en/articles/event-coverage/undated/tournaments-events-trading-card-game-magic-the-gathering-2)'s 2006 class and was inducted at the 2006 World Championships in Paris. Send your question, along with your name and location, via this email form. Answers will be posted every Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday. 

Note: This feature isn't intended for rules-related questions. For those questions, try our [Saturday School archive](https://web.archive.org/web/20070810075256/http://www.wizards.com/default.asp?x=mtgcom/columnarchive&column=SaturdaySchool), the [Rules Q&A message board](https://web.archive.org/web/20070501000000/http://boards1.wizards.com/forumdisplay.php?f=154), or visit irc.efnet #mtgjudge.

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>May 31, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: The ability to have eight board clearing spells with <a>Wrath of God</a> and <a>Damnation</a> in a deck paves the way for near creature-less control. This, combined with ultra-powered cards like <a>Extirpate</a>, <a>Glimpse the Unthinkable</a>, <a>Induce Paranoia</a>, and the awe-inspiring mana base <i>Ravnica</i> block provides through its dual lands and signets should provide the perfect backdrop for a good old fashioned eat-your-library <a>Millstone</a> deck.</p>&#10;<p>My questions are: Why do you think this hasn't happened yet? Has this build ever even been considered on the Pro Tour? Lastly, if you were to build a Standard mill deck, what would it look like?<br> -Russell<br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hi Russell,</p>&#10;<p>Milling isn't a strategy that has been used very often in the game. If you think about it, milling is attacking your opponent's library just like direct damage would attack your opponent's life total directly. The main difference between the two aspects is that your opponent is starting the game with 20 life and about 40-45 cards. </p>&#10;<p>The problem with cards such as <a>Millstone</a> or <a>Glimpse the Unthinkable</a>, which only aim at exhausting your opponent's deck, is that they have no influence on the board. Given the fact that you're playing a control deck along with the milling arsenal, <a>Glimpse the Unthinkable</a> works pretty much like a direct damage that deals five damage (mills one-quarter of your opponent's library, just like five damage would hit one-quarter of your opponent's life), meaning that you would need all four of them to kill your opponent. Control decks need reliable and efficient kills that won't take too many slots. In the old days, the job was handed to Morphling, which could single-handedly deal 20 damage to your opponent and block without dying. </p>&#10;<p>Milling is overall a bad strategy. It works fine against control deck which runs few kill cards, as if you mill them, you are likely to win; but has a hard time against anything else. I'm talking about nowadays formats. That means I wouldn't even think of building a mill deck in Standard.</p>&#10;<p>The most successful strategies that aimed at exhausting your opponent's library have been either decks with a complete lockdown combo (<a>Kismet</a>/<a>Stasis</a>, or <a>Winter Orb</a>/<a>Icy Manipulator</a>) or combo decks that kill with one card (bunch of mana/<a>Stroke of Genius</a>, very high spell count/<a>Brain Freeze</a>) and except for very few decks, none were playing actual <a>Millstone</a>s.</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>May 29, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: In Mike Flores' article &quot;<a href="/en/articles/swimmingwithsharks/2005/09-29-everything-old-is-new-again">Everything Old is New Again</a>&quot; he writes about the power of control decks long past and how then-modern day control decks were very different than the ones he cites from 1996. Sadly, Flores's article was written almost two years ago.</p>&#10;<p>The introduction of <i>Planar Chaos</i> into the Standard format, gives us <a></a><a>Damnation</a>—<a></a><a>Wrath of God</a>'s evil twin. WoG has been acclaimed as one of the games most powerful cards and is consistently seen among the top decks in Standard tournaments both live and online. However, after watching many recent Standard replays on <b>Magic Online</b> almost no deck runs both WoG and <a>Damnation</a> together. Why?<br> - Russell <br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hello Russell,</p>&#10;<p>Both cards are very powerful, <a>Wrath of God</a> has seen A LOT of play since it was printed in 1993. However, it can't be good in every format. Look at today's Standard and its dominant decks. Against most of them, your <a>Wrath of God</a>s or <a>Damnation</a>s will be dead cards, or just trade one for one. You rarely want to hold a WoG against <a>Dragonstorm</a> or any decks running the UrzaTron. In most cases (in today's Standard), you won't want to run more than two or three <a>Wrath</a>s in your main deck, and just fill your sideboard with the other one or two.</p>&#10;<p>I've been asked this question quite often, people wanting to play <a>Damnation</a> and <a>Wrath of God</a> in the same deck. Why would you want THAT many? They both require double colored mana, so you should just stick to one of them. If you're running a black-white deck and are expecting creature-only decks, then you might want to have more than four board-sweeping sorceries. But even then, if your opponent knows about your strategy, he will never commit too much by playing too many creatures. In the best case, you're going to trade two for one (one WoG for two creatures), the rest of the time, you're going to have to trade one for one to kill a creature. And four mana is a lot to kill one creature. That's why, in most cases, you will want to play cheaper removals over a WoG. </p>&#10;<p>Also, your strategy can't rely on WoGs only. When you're building your deck, starting with &quot;OK, I have eight WoGs, now what?&quot; is usually a bad method. They don't do much by themselves as they are purely defensive, and almost dead cards against creature-light decks. They are support cards for decks that can't deal with creatures in combat (have creatures to block, basically).</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>May 26, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: I was wondering what pros think of the Eternal formats, especially Legacy. Do you see it as a &quot;good&quot; format or do you not care about it? Do you keep up with what is happening in Legacy and Vintage? I would like to know because Wizards is giving Legacy a little push with the few Grand Prix, but there hasn't been a Pro Tour for any Eternal format. Would you like to see an Eternal Pro Tour? <br> - Simon <br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hi Simon,</p>&#10;<p>Pros are basically interested in anything that is played competitively. If Wizards announces a Vintage Pro Tour tomorrow, pros will try to catch up with everything they have missed. I don't think that will ever happen, unless Wizards wants pros to spend all the money they win from the tournaments to dealers for Power 9 cards. There are very few pro players who play Vintage seriously. Like any other format, it takes a lot of preparation to master, and for no real reward. They'd rather put their efforts into the next Pro Tour's Constructed format. </p>&#10;<p>Legacy is a wide format, and by the time you read this answer, Grand Prix–Columbus will be in the books, and we'll know the impact <a>Flash</a> decks had. Legacy doesn't exactly have the financial constraint Vintage has. Dual lands are a lot easier to get a hold of than Moxen and you'll probably find people around you who won't need the cards you'll want to play (so they can lend them to you) ... once again, unlike Moxen. And even then, they are many very competitive decks that don't cost a thing, like Goblins for example. </p>&#10;<p>A Grand Prix once in a while isn't that a big deal, but a Pro Tour would be. Not being able to playtest on <b>Magic Online</b> would probably be a real issue.</p>&#10;<p>I don't play either format. I do read a few things about both, just to keep track of what's going on, more because it's entertaining than because I have a real interest in them. Would I like to see a Pro Tour in any of these formats? Honestly? Not really!</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>May 24, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: What do you think about Vintage because I think it is losing its popularity. Only because everyone thinks that you can only play Vintage when you have a full set of power, which isn't correct. I have seen a lots of decks winning tournaments &quot;unpowered.&quot; So why is Vintage fading?<br> - Ben <br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hi Ben, </p>&#10;<p>Is Vintage actually losing popularity ? I would rather say that it's been a very long time that it hasn't been popular. The only time I remember what is now called &quot;Vintage&quot; to be very popular, was actually when it was called ... well, <b>Magic</b>. Before anyone had heard of Standard, basically before the DCI had taken control of organized play, everyone was playing with the cards they had.</p>&#10;<p>The good thing about Standard, Block Constructed, and even Extended, is that everyone has access to the cards. You may not own the cards yourself, but you can still borrow the cards you're missing here and there, or even buy them without having to work overtime for five years. The investment you have to make to play Block Constructed, Standard, or Extended isn't comparable to the one you have to make to play Vintage. The only limit players have, except for very limited budgets, is their deckbuilding skills. Everyone starts with the same chances.</p>&#10;<p>Even if some Vintage decks don't include the Power cards (Mox, <a>Black Lotus</a>, <a>Ancestral Recall</a>, etc.), it's tough to be interested in a format where you could play against someone fully equipped. When you're building your deck, you're limited to the cards you own when others aren't, and it doesn't sound too fair. </p>&#10;<p>Vintage would be a lot more popular if Wizards gave away free Moxen and <a>Black Lotus</a>es to everyone ... but I doubt this will ever happen. </p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>May 22, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: I played in a Pro Tour Qualifier for San Diego last weekend, and my partner and I had built great decks. But unfortunately we missed the top 4 by only one victory, which was lost by mana screw of my deck (red-black). I put the right amount of land of each type (17 total for a 40-card deck). My starting hand after one mulligan had three Mountains but I didn't draw a land seven turns in a row. After the game, I saw that a run of 6-7 land was in the middle of my library. I realized maybe I should have taken another mulligan to be sure to have at least one land of each type (Mountain and Swamp) or more land in my hand. Do you have any advice for me, to avoid this in the future? Before each game I reshuffle my deck by alternating one land of each type and then two random cards. But the way that my opponents reshuffle my deck before the game starts (by putting my cards in six different piles) seems to have made a pile of land. Do you have any advice for me? How do pros shuffle their libraries to avoid this? Thanks!<br> - Christian <br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hi Christian,</p>&#10;<p>First of all, you should have a look at the Universal Tournament Rules, section 21:</p><b>Shuffling</b><br><ul>&#10;<p>&quot;Shuffling must be done so that the faces of the cards cannot be seen. Regardless of the method used to shuffle, players' decks must be sufficiently randomized. Each time players shuffle their deck, they must present their deck to their opponent for additional shuffling and/or cutting&quot;.... &quot;By presenting their decks to their opponents, players are stating that their decks are correct, legal, and sufficiently randomized.&quot;</p>&#10;<p>&quot;After decks are presented and accepted, any player who does not believe his or her opponent has made a reasonable effort to sufficiently randomize his or her deck must notify a judge. The head judge has final authority to determine whether a deck has been sufficiently randomized. The head judge also has the authority to determine if a player has used reasonable effort to randomize his or her deck. If the head judge feels that either the deck has not been sufficiently randomized or that a player has not made a reasonable effort to randomize his or her deck, the player will be subject to the appropriate provisions of the DCI Penalty Guidelines.&quot; </p>&#10;</ul>&#10;<p>The method you are using to shuffle your deck is not legal if you don't riffle shuffle or use another way to shuffle afterward. The technique you're using is called &quot;weaving&quot; and is not randomizing your deck at all. By doing that, you're almost sure to draw two active spells and a land every time ... and this is not random. No need to say that it's also considered cheating. Your opponent, who seemed to have noticed the way you &quot;shuffled,&quot; should have called a judge and told him about that. The penalty for such a thing in a PTQ is a disqualification. What your opponent did was wrong too, as committing an infraction to offset a perceived infraction is still illegal. If an investigation (both inspecting the deck in question and talking to both players) would reveal that you weaved intentionally, and your opponent unweaved intentionally in an effort to screw you, it is likely that both of you would be disqualified.</p>&#10;<p>The pros shuffle according to the rules.
