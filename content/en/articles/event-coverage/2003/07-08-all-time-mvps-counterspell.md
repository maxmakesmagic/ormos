*Counterspell* it is. After utterly demolishing Fact or Fiction (and Impulse, which barely registered a blip next to the two more powerful spells), it’s obvious why people wanted to see the Counterspell: power and nostalgia. Counterspell is out of *Eighth Edition*; it’s one of the hottest topics in **Magic** right now. This is quite possibly the largest upheaval in the core set since Revised, which took out Black Lotus, Ancestral Recall and the other power cards. The last major shift close to this magnitude was the release of *Seventh Edition*, which rotated out Armageddon, white weenie’s most powerful spell. As with the loss of ‘Geddon, the loss of Counterspell will cause immediate and major shifts in the metagame that will change the landscape of Standard.

But why did Fact or Fiction and Impulse lose out to Counterspell in the poll? One interesting point was brought up by Kinny Fain. Half the time, what are you Impulsing or Fact or Fiction-ing for? Counterspells. Impulse and Fact or Fiction may be enablers, but the Counterspell is the MVP. This time, anyway. 

This week’s poll is all about ![White Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif) casting cost creatures. White has gotten the lion’s share (cough) of good weenies. This week, we’ll pick one of its cheapest. The first option is Mother of Runes. In a time when white weenies were able to foil Sligh in the metagame, the Mother was the anchor for the deck, getting two for one advantage on removal spells, creating unblockable guys and generally holding down the fort. Up next is Ramosian Sergeant. While Lin Sivvi was decidedly the leader of the rebels, most players felt that it was the Sergeant who did the grunt work, making rebels the dominant deck of their time. Finally, there is Savannah Lions. The original Jackal Pup, the lions have been notably absent from Standard and Extended for quite some time. Their return in *Eighth Edition* has been hailed as a harbinger for the re-emergence of white weenie. Will it live up to expectations? Vote at the end of this article.

### What’s Counterspell?

It’s a hard life, being the template around which an entire class of spells is formed. When Counterspell debuted, there were only a handful of counterspell cards. Four of those, Lifeforce, Deathgrip, Red Elemental Blast, and Blue Elemental Blast are color hosers first and counterspells second (though Red Elemental Blast did see plenty of use as an anti-counterspell). In Alpha, you have three strict counterspells: Spell Blast, Power Sink and Counterspell. The design principle is obvious and elegant, though it stumbled in practice. You have two situational common counters, one that is best-used against cheap spells (Spell Blast) and one that crushes high-cost spells (Power Sink). Of course, this is an optimal situation. It assumes that you always have both Power Sink and Spell Blast in hand, so that you can choose the most appropriate counter. The concept completely breaks down when either A. you don’t have Spell Blast and Power Sink available or B. your opponent’s deck is very focused on casting spells that all cost around the same amount of mana (for example, a white weenie deck). That, not to mention the fact that you have to keep a tremendous amount of mana untapped to utilize these two spells, weakened the concept of counterspells. Though Power Sink made appearances in Constructed, mainly due to its secondary ability (tapping an opponent out) being good for blue, the dismal Spell Blast has never been considered a decent counter.

Luckly, there was an alternative. For cases where neither Spell Blast or Power Sink would do, you have the uncommon, more powerful Counterspell. A big reason Counterspell was effective was that it had a flat cost, as compared to the much more situational costs associated with Blast and Sink. Of course, it was tremendously helpful that the flat cost was a very affordable ![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif). The problem with Spell Blast and, to a lesser extent, Power Sink was the amount of mana required to keep the card active. For example, if you are worried about your opponent casting a Disenchant, you always have to have ![2 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) to Spell Blast. If you’re worried about, say, Wrath of God, you need ![4 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/36/29/3629288966daa042decf01d3752789ab6676fddd67c3679fa580cd3baecae158.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif). ![4 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/36/29/3629288966daa042decf01d3752789ab6676fddd67c3679fa580cd3baecae158.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) is a lot to keep untapped, just in case your opponent casts a Wrath of God. Then, if they’re casting an X spell, you can forget about countering it with the Blast, unless you have at least one more mana than they do, and are willing to keep that open forever. With Power Sink, you have the opposite problem. If they’re casting their big spells, you’re okay. But you have to be mostly untapped if you want to snag their cheap spells. After playing with either of these cards, it’s easy to see why fixed-cost counterspells are highly preferred. The variable amount of mana you have to pay with other counters leaves the blue player taking unacceptable risks while being forced to hold back too much mana. 

<table><tbody><tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/65/b4/65b44cec90bda938bbb278a79acba1f455a38a19b714807bea68c253b77de30d.jpg"></td><td></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/dd/0a/dd0a617788694ee0e82eed58b0746ab9dbdbdb6e709ee5390dcfb32556a6990e.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/96/f5/96f5d53e3ef958055c4fdf5bf835be99797bd18449a68a882c96ab3801bae612.jpg"></td><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/de/e8/dee85638ff89a4ceaccab3caa622211e54971c85e4d536e07ac03d722750b275.jpg"></td><td></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/a0/a9/a0a91e69ef087e17e689f758224146196f0a29fad934eb2639d2fb73d4cf3c75.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c0/e2/c0e24bff2fa206011911cb4b5cb20b4559cb82301a77e757b6e86de3c1ae659a.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/fa/ab/faab219b94a5d6a8405ff77aefe6f815ba2bba52fc447652a633eec85e227836.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Counterspell addresses this weakness of Spell Blast and Power Sink, as well as another. Spell Blast always, always cost you more mana than the spell your opponent played. Power Sink often did, as well, but with the caveat of tapping your opponent out, neutralizing them (this was a lot better than it is now, because of the old interrupt rules). Of course, this usually also made it impossible for you to play additional spells. In fact, since most good **Magic** decks consist mostly of cheap cards, Power Sink will often cost you just about as much as it costs your opponent. A big reason that Counterspell is good is that it is mana efficient (this is where many of Counterspells descendants fail). It always costs ![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif), while the cost of the spells that it counters vary, with many of them costing more than two mana. Each time a Counterspell removes a card that costs more than it does, its caster gains a mana advantage. This can often translate into indirect card advantage. For example, let’s say both players are at six mana and I cast a Shivan Dragon. My opponent casts Counterspell. I have no mana untapped. He has four. This could be used for many things, like Fact or Fiction. Or, conversely, a blue player could use that four mana in his or her main phase (casting something good, like Control Magic or Nevinyrral’s Disk), knowing that ![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) in reserve would be able to stop a dangerous spell, like Disenchant.

### When It Was Legal…

Counterspell, like many All-Time MVPs, has always been around. Though Counterspell is a format-defining card, it’s very hard to look at what that means in context, as we’ve never had an environment outside of Block Constructed (which is very limited, anyway) without it. Even in metagames where blue was practically non-existent, like Vise Age and Black Summer, Counterspell has been at the forefront of blue’s offerings. 

<table><tbody><tr><td><table><tbody><tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/65/b4/65b44cec90bda938bbb278a79acba1f455a38a19b714807bea68c253b77de30d.jpg"></td><td></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/dd/0a/dd0a617788694ee0e82eed58b0746ab9dbdbdb6e709ee5390dcfb32556a6990e.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/96/f5/96f5d53e3ef958055c4fdf5bf835be99797bd18449a68a882c96ab3801bae612.jpg"></td><td><a><img alt="Whispers of the Muse" src="https://i.ormos.uk/f4/81/f481ca680f86cb966cf1f67bea97736c5e89d2b4574a872f5095a1b0271cb404.jpg"></a></td><td></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/a0/a9/a0a91e69ef087e17e689f758224146196f0a29fad934eb2639d2fb73d4cf3c75.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c0/e2/c0e24bff2fa206011911cb4b5cb20b4559cb82301a77e757b6e86de3c1ae659a.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/fa/ab/faab219b94a5d6a8405ff77aefe6f815ba2bba52fc447652a633eec85e227836.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table><br><i>Counters and card-drawers are like peas and carrots.</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Counterspell basically has two uses. It can form the core of base-blue, counter-heavy control decks. Draw-Go (a monoblue counter deck, usually packing more than a dozen various counters), Five-Color Blue (a card-drawing machine that revolved around Whispers of the Muse and Gaea’s Blessing, with various kill cards), Counterpost (which functioned as blue-white control, with Wraths and Disenchants to supplement the counters and Kjedoran Outpost for the kill card), Maher Oath (a mainly blue-green deck using Counterspells to defend and exploit Oath of Druids in Extended) and many, many other decks used Counterspell as the first line of defense, which was supported by additional counters, Disks and other control cards. In modern Standard, Counterspell is a regular feature of Psychatog decks, where it helps stave off creature and burn rushes from green-red, bad news like Phantom Centaurs and opposing Upheavals in the mirror match.

In its other use, Counterspell is generally more vital, as it doesn’t have a ton of redundant spells to bail it out. Counterspell can also be used as a 4-of (sometimes, with a little backup). In this role, Counterspell is more of a hoser card, used to knock out silver bullet cards that defeat your strategy. Counterspell is often seen in this role in aggressive blue decks, like Fish (a merfolk-themed offensive deck), Countersliver, and even early iterations of modern blue-green. In all three, the Counterspell is there to guard against potential disasters and accentuate the nature of aggressive decks. If you’ve ever played against Strip Mines or Wastelands in Sligh, you know how powerful that one-for-one trade can be. What happens in Sligh is that I play Jackal Pup. My opponent plays his land. I Wasteland it, attack for two and play another Jackal Pup. It’s basically still turn one, except that I’ve done two damage and have two Jackal Pups in play, instead of a single one. If I have another Wasteland and my opponent isn’t sitting on a hand of basic lands, this game is all-but over. In aggressive decks, Counterspell can work in much the same way, especially against creature-light control decks that rely on creature sweepers to stop the rush. You spend the first few turns playing out creatures. Then, when your opponent finally has the mana to cast their threat spells, you have the Counterspell. It ends up acting as a Time Walk, much in the same way Wasteland can grab an extra turn or two. Counterspell can be the difference between your opponent resolving his or her Wrath of God at six life and shuffling up for game 2. 

### The Psychology of Counterspell

> *“Against two untapped islands, you definitely start to second-guess your decisions.”* -Craig Batzler

The beauty of Counterspell is not what it is, but how it works. More than any other card in **Magic**, Counterspell gives a player ample opportunity to bluff. Unless your opponent has a way to look at your hand, he or she will always be guessing whether or not you have a ready Counterspell if you’ve got ![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) untapped and a card in hand. This psychological advantage can be huge, especially if your opponent is not super-confident in his or her own playing abilities. Since any card in your hand could potentially be a counter, a player will have to take that into account before he or she plays each spell. You have to ask yourself “do I want this spell to be countered?” “Can I play around the Counterspells?” This causes a drastic shift. For example, if you really need to resolve a Disenchant to blow away your opponent’s Winter Orb and you only have three Disenchants in your deck, it is extremely important that your Disenchant resolves. It could be devastating if it doesn’t, because you only have two more – and who knows how many counters your opponent is packing, not to mention spare Winter Orbs. If your opponent has ![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) untapped and some cards it hand, it’s not looking like a safe bet to play that Disenchant. You may have to play some diversionary cards to try and draw the counters out. Of course, if your opponent gets wise to what you’re doing, he or she probably won’t counter your first spells, holding back for the real threat. Or maybe there is no Counterspell in his or her hand. How can you tell? Well, you can play your spells and see what happens, but that can backfire. In the end, it’s very difficult to figure out how to play around untapped islands – even the best players get it wrong from time to time. 

### Why Was It Great?

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>“As long as there have been overpowered cards printed, there has been Counterspell to stop them.” -Sean McBee</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

The Counterspell comes up as an All-Time MVP practically every time it’s played. From generating mana advantages to breaking up combo decks to protecting your own combos to guarding against an opponent’s key cards, the applications of Counterspell are varied and powerful. What’s more is that this card, now widely considered too powerful, survived set rotations for a decade before being given the boot in *Eighth Edition*. It has been a factor in practically every metagame in that same stretch of time, putting a stopper in aggressive decks, creating crazy blue standoffs and throwing off the math of just about every situation.

Really, what makes Counterspell great is its place in the color blue. No other card has so steadily guided a color for so long. While you may say that Lightning Bolt defined red, Disenchant defined white, Necropotence defined black and Birds of Paradise defined green, none of those cards shaped its entire color the way Counterspell shaped blue. For a long time, blue has centered around counterspell-based control decks, with card-drawing supplementing a creature-light, spell-heavy strategy. 

### I Hate You!

<table><tbody><tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/65/b4/65b44cec90bda938bbb278a79acba1f455a38a19b714807bea68c253b77de30d.jpg"></td><td></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/dd/0a/dd0a617788694ee0e82eed58b0746ab9dbdbdb6e709ee5390dcfb32556a6990e.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/96/f5/96f5d53e3ef958055c4fdf5bf835be99797bd18449a68a882c96ab3801bae612.jpg"></td><td><a><img alt="Pyroblast" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c8/49/c849d66c5873d6bc5dfafb6f3967fb330310a93c4b72f5c3e6ca2e511299c6b9.jpg"></a></td><td></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/a0/a9/a0a91e69ef087e17e689f758224146196f0a29fad934eb2639d2fb73d4cf3c75.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c0/e2/c0e24bff2fa206011911cb4b5cb20b4559cb82301a77e757b6e86de3c1ae659a.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/fa/ab/faab219b94a5d6a8405ff77aefe6f815ba2bba52fc447652a633eec85e227836.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Just as no card in **Magic** has done so much to define its color, no card in **Magic** has been the focus of so many hoser cards. The proud history of Counterspell hosers started in Alpha, with Red Elemental Blast. For awhile, this sort of spell was the main defense against counterspells. Much in the way Duress is now used to battle counters, Red Elemental Blast would be right behind a key spell, forcing the blue player to have two counters (or, at least, a Blue Elemental Blast, to counter the red one). Pyroblast in *Ice Age* and *Alliances*’ Burnout continued red’s tradition of going directly after the counters. *Mirage* brought with it Sirocco, which wasn’t super-effective, but showed a desire to punish blue players and Kaervek’s Torch, a Fireball designed to throw the math off on blue players. *Visions* gave green a good anti-counter defense in City of Solitude, which completely shut counters down. When *Tempest* debuted Scragnoth, players knew that the counter-hate had reached a whole new level. Cards that could not be countered absolutely took counters out of the picture. That’s how powerful counterspells are; even with strong hosers like *Urza’s Legacy*’s Defense Grid, the only way to stop Counterspell is to print cards that say “cannot be countered”. Also in *Tempest* was Boil, which improved on Tsunami to take advantage of blue’s penchant to tap out at the end of turn after having guarded an opponent’s main phase with counters. The clincher was *Invasion*, with several good spells that could not be countered. Blurred Mongoose was very much a smaller Scragnoth, while Obliterate and Urza’s Rage meant that, even if a blue player had complete control of the game, it could end on them in an instant – and there wasn’t much anyone could do about it.

### The Big Picture

<table><tbody><tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/65/b4/65b44cec90bda938bbb278a79acba1f455a38a19b714807bea68c253b77de30d.jpg"></td><td></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/dd/0a/dd0a617788694ee0e82eed58b0746ab9dbdbdb6e709ee5390dcfb32556a6990e.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/96/f5/96f5d53e3ef958055c4fdf5bf835be99797bd18449a68a882c96ab3801bae612.jpg"></td><td><a><img alt="Force of Will" src="https://i.ormos.uk/02/af/02af3898eaf3df8950fe7d34bee36b0488330045a33c1f2c7bccffbd394acb4d.jpg"></a></td><td></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/a0/a9/a0a91e69ef087e17e689f758224146196f0a29fad934eb2639d2fb73d4cf3c75.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c0/e2/c0e24bff2fa206011911cb4b5cb20b4559cb82301a77e757b6e86de3c1ae659a.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/fa/ab/faab219b94a5d6a8405ff77aefe6f815ba2bba52fc447652a633eec85e227836.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Counterspell is definitely a big picture card. With tons of spinoff cards, many of which have been as powerful and even arguably more powerful than the original. Mana Drain and Force of Will jump to mind as the biggest, best Counterspell variants. The Drain is Counterspell, plus mana acceleration and Force of Will is a ![0 Mana](http://www.wizards.com/images/Symbols/Symbol_0_mana.gif) mana cost, if you pay 1 life and pitch a blue card. The ![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) counterspell is rare – with only a couple to be counted. Ever since *Mirage* introduced Dissipate, lots of counters have moved into the 3 casting cost range. These include Exclude, Circular Logic, and Forbid as well as counterspells that have bled into other colors, like Undermine, Dromar’s Charm, and Absorb. There are a few counters that are strictly non-blue (like Illumination), but they are, as a rule, bad cards. Reasssignment counters, like Deflection and Misdirection add a little to the pantheon, giving blue a way to deal with Urza’s Rage, as well as other trouble cards. One mana counterspells also exist, but they are highly situational. Both Force Spike (which departed the core set at the same time as Counterspell, to much less fanfare) and Disrupt have been widely used as early-game counters. The ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) counters are some of the most compelling, as they have given decks that are not base-blue some countering power. Arcane Denial, Memory Lapse, Miscalculation and Mana Leak (coming soon to a core set near you!) have appeared in base white and black weenie decks, often in tandem with Winter Orb or Armageddon. In the higher casting cost counters, there are a few gems and some weak cards. The aforementioned Force of Will is technically a high casting cost spell, as are its nephews Foil and Thwart. Rewind and Dismiss give you counters with bonuses, the former is “free” and the latter is a cantrip. However, the high casting costs have been used to give some counters cool abilities, at the expense of making them unplayable or barely playable. Spelljack, Desertion and Fervent Denial fit this category. All three are cool, but you really don’t want to tap five or six mana to counter a Nantuko Shade.

Counterspell was always a cut above its variants. Now, in an environment without it, it will be interesting to see if blue counter-based control can make a go of it.
