There is no shortage of intelligence among competitive **Magic** players, nor is there any shortage of mistakes. However, do players analyze their mistakes intelligently? In the mind of any player on the brink of a decision, a cost/benefit analysis takes place. While the result of this process, be it a stellar play or a blatant miscalculation, is heavily scrutinized after the fact, it is the method itself which deserves the true consideration. Few players recognize that the irrational tendencies responsible for suboptimal plays are present in their post-play analytical process as well. One is truly reasonable only after recognizing defeat as a product of flawed character rather than poor luck.

For some, self-knowledge comes easy. For others, it costs thousands of dollars. Pro Tour-Chicago was to be the performance of my life. I entered the event with the supreme confidence that one can attribute to a victory in a Pro Tour Qualifier on the first try, copious discussion with Nick Eisel and Ken Krouner and a fortuitous week of playtesting with Team CMU in Pittsburgh. With a solid performance among my CMU brethren and the respect of many of my peers, I assumed that my apprenticeship was over and that now was the time to show off my skills. 

What transpired in Chicago was a rude awakening to the cold facts of being a tier-two player: impenetrable logic still eluded me. Off to a quick 6-0 start, I played the majority of the tournament in Top 8 contention. With the next great success on the line, however, I came undone faster than a bra on prom night. The following examples of my undoing should serve as guidelines to the aspiring **Magic** professional who is interested in making the pure play. 

### Round 7 vs.Osyp Lebedowicz, Game 3: Logic corrupted by habit.

Through maximizing my mediocre red-blue deck's ability to get to its Future Sight and the land necessary for its two Blatant Thieveries, I earned the privilege to face the table's best deck: Osyp's formidable red-green deck. I was happy to pull out a tight game 1 based on my foresight regarding consecutive topdecks I would need to win and was anxious to get to the much heralded 7-0 position. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/f7/c1/f7c168283a072ef6d2919ba0c2a71083b8879a419933bfa219794cb05ab69e04.gif)  
*If you kill all of your opponents creatures you can no longer protect your creatures with Meddle.*

I play first and start with a turn two Imagecrafter, which he follows up with a turn two Wirewood Elf. A critical decision is in my hands turn 3, when I'm staring at Lavamancer's Skill, Meddle, and Ascending Aven in my hand. So, do you skill the Imagecrafter and shoot the elf, or do you pass the turn and wait to Skill with Meddle backup? I know he has a considerable number of high casting cost spells, the casting of which will be facilitated by the elf and that he has two Shocks and an Erratic Explosion to prevent with Meddle. If I play the Meddle game, I can not cast the Aven on turn four, which will probably race him effectively if he removes the Skilled wizard and does not have the relatively unlikely second removal spell. Before I can finish these calculations (which I start making during his second turn so as not to be completely obvious), I realize that if I Skill the wizard and shoot the elf during the end step of his fourth turn, he could easily just not play another creature and a Shock will destroy me, as I will have no target for Meddle. So on my third turn, I immediately Skill up the Imagecrafter and kill the elf. He has the Erratic Explosion, as well as the Shock for the Ascending Aven and he beats me in a heavily lopsided game. Does anyone else see the problem here? 

Lavamancer's Skill does not read: "If your opponent has a creature that could be targeted with your Skilled creature's newfound ability, you must do so." I could have easily Skilled the wizard turn four and if he passed the turn, just not shot the elf down. Then I could have played the Ascending Aven face down and Osyp would have been forced to make a play. Never in testing had I wanted to not kill something with a Skilled creature and I did not even consider it as a possibility. He was holding Starstorm, so this was going to be a tough game regardless, but I never even considered what likely was my best option. Outside of the box thinking like this is paramount.

### Round 8 vs. Stefan Fries, Game 3: Logic corrupted by greed.

Stefan is playing a fairly traditional black-green deck, with only three spells and numerous high casting cost monsters, including the morphless Gigapede and Prowling Pangolin. My deck is black-white with the stabilizing power you would expect. I have sideboarded in my Starlit Sanctum, going up to nineteen lands, four of which cycle. Even with a Daunting Defender and a Gluttonous Zombie in hand, I decide to cycle a Secluded Steppe on the first turn, as I have three swamps in hand and therefore need only one more land. With nineteen lands in the deck, several turns to draw a fifth land and no low casting cost/morph creatures in hand, I deem this to be an acceptable risk with the reward being a better mana curve. Also, if I don't draw land, then I'm drawing spells right? Seems like an acceptable worse-case scenario.

This is entirely incorrect for several reasons. Stefan has a hard time applying much pressure to me in the early turns, even if I did little to nothing because of his high mana curve. The second white mana is still valuable for casting several of the spells in my deck and paying several of the morph costs. Also, I do not beat his deck by drawing more spells than him or by casting them more quickly. My victory condition involves locking up the ground with high casting cost spells with a high concentration of abilities and toughness, namely the Daunting Defender in my hand and the Grassland Crusader in my deck.

Essentially, when I asked myself this risk vs. reward question, I overrated the rewards and underrated the risks. I wanted to have not only the established victory condition, but also the perfect mana curve as well. To return to the actual game, I do draw several low casting cost creatures, but they include True Believer and Gustcloak Harrier. As should have been expected, his early turns are relatively uneventful and when he starts casting giant beasts, I am ill-prepared to deal with them. The first land I draw is Starlit Sanctum, which still comes early enough to put me in a position to win. However, I am punished for my mistake with an unintentional draw as I have taken enough damage that I will not be able to win in the allotted time thanks to my unnecessarily fragile control of the game. Cycling lands are invaluable due to their ability to prevent mana flood, but the decision to cycle them carries with it unique considerations every time. 

### Round 11 vs. Eugene Harvey, Game 1: Logic corrupted by arrogance and name recognition.

I am not a computer and neither are you. It is appropriately human to overestimate the importance of an idea based solely on the fact that it is one's own. At any given point in a game of **Magic** or any strategy game for that matter, the possible plays should be seen as a set of contingencies, each accompanied by the odds that the play will win you the game. More often than we would like to admit, however, we become attached to the first notion of the correct play that we determine. It is this arrogance that deters even the most logical gamers from the proper induction. Eugene exploits this weakness in the following classic example of irrational action:

He comes out of the gates this game with a morph, an Improvised Armor on said morph, and an Aven Brigadier shortly thereafter. I am holding Death Pulse but Eugene astutely remembers that I have it and does not block with the Brigadier. However, I topdeck Pacifism when I have to and nullify his Brigadier with it. Then I kill his freshly-cast Gustcloak Harrier with the Death Pulse. My next draw is a Brigadier-enhanced Dive Bomber and with it came the comforting realization that I had weathered the storm. Here's the situation at the moment of truth:

Eugene has three cards, the morph with Improvised Armor, another morph, a Snarling Undorak, and the Pacified Aven Brigadier. He has seven untapped forests and plains, several cards in hand and is at eight life. On my side are a True Believer, Battlefield Medic, Dive Bomber and tapped a Gluttonous Zombie. I have two swamps and a plains untapped, an Inspirit in hand and I am at 3 life. Eugene attacks with his two morphs and the Snarling Undorak. 

I have this game won as long as I see Eugene's out, which I figure he must have in order to make that attack. He is the National Champ, after all, right? I decide to play around the Vitality Charm that I know is in his deck by making the following play:

Inspirit the Gluttonous Zombie, block the Undorak with the Zombie. Block the morph with the Dive Bomber, and block the Improvised Armored morph with True Believer.

Sure enough, the morph is the Towering Baloth I forgot he had and I am left with only the Zombie and the Medic, which he totally trumps with a Crowd Favorites shortly thereafter. I was incredibly passionate about avoiding a loss to Vitality Charm and similarly confident that he had a direct way to win the game based solely on his skill level alone. Ironically enough, my play was completely incorrect even if he was holding Vitality Charm and without knowledge of the Towering Baloth. The correct play would have been:

Block Snarling Undorak with Dive Bomber, block morph with True Believer, block Improvised Armored Morph with Battlefield Medic. 

This play ensures that I do not die to a Charm (I can Inspirit the blocker of a pumped creature) and the real issue here is mana conflict. Eugene needs mana to pump the Undorak, flip over his two morphs, play a trick and play another creature. By making the above block, I force him to reveal a play so I can react accordingly. In this case, the threat was not greater than the execution because of his lack of resources. However, Eugene played the threatening role perfectly and I deservingly found myself at table two instead of at the greatest draft table of all time.

### Round 13 vs. David Brucker, Game 1: Logic corrupted by memory.

This was an epic match. With Top 8 on the line, Kai Budde and western Europe were rooting for their German countryman from the sidelines. I had unequivocally gotten over my Feature Match apprehensiveness the day before and I felt like the pressure was actually motivational. How wrong I was.

I am red-white this draft and David is black-green. With the traditional white defense set up, a Gustcloak Harrier slowly knocks him down to five. I'm tempted to cast the Improvised Armor I've been holding on the Harrier to kill David rapidly, but I know he has Cruel Revival and it is the right play to wait. Sure enough, he Cruel Revivals it while at five and returns a Spined Basher to his hand. I quickly make a note of the Spined Basher on my notepad. I now have free reign to armor up my Daru Lancer, and the only card worth worrying about at this point is the Primal Boost I noted during the draft. He is forced to play Wretched Anurid which, along with his Nantuko Husk and other assorted creatures, is enough to force my Daru Lancer to stay home, especially given the boost. Soon he casts Aphetto Dredging for three beasts, all of which I quickly note on my pad as well. When I immediately cast my freshly-drawn Glory Seeker during my turn, he sacrifices his Anurid to the Husk as he has fallen to two life at this point.

Passing the turn seems like a foregone conclusion when I look down at my pad and realize I know all four of his cards from the Dredging and the Revival, so despite it being against intuition, attacking with my armored Daru Lancer is the right play. Oddly, he blocks with his 4/4 Nantuko Husk and sacrifices a Pacified creature to make it 6/6. When he cast Primal Boost on the Husk, my face must have embodied sheer terror and confusion. He could not have Primal Boost. My ever-dependable notepad told me so. Then I get hit in the face with the cold realization that his seemingly mysterious tapped morph creature off to the side is the elusive Spined Basher he returned earlier. I had not made a note of Spined Basher becoming only a possible card in hand once that morph was cast. David comes back to win this game, and after relatively lopsided games two and three, I found myself eliminated from Top 8 contention, possibly with Kai cackling maniacally in the distance.

Many times, have I claimed to reach the elusive perfect play at PTQs and similar local events. After the invaluable experience of playing against some of the best the world has to offer, I have seen how arrogance, greed, habit, memory, and name recognition have been clouding my decisions all along. Only by being properly punished for my improper play have I learned from my mistakes. When playing for the Top 8, even the kind reviews of Ken Krouner can not help you. The only thing that can aid an aspiring tier-one player is the knowledge that while reason can not fail, the ability to recognize what is reasonable can.
