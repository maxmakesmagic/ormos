# What to Expect in Modern

Modern has not changed much with the release of *Core Set 2019*. Despite the printing of [Damping Sphere](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Damping+Sphere), which helps to fight Tron and storm strategies, Mono-Green Tron is still one of the most popular decks in Modern. There are also more aggressive strategies than in the past, with Humans and [Hollow One](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Hollow+One) joining Modern mainstays Burn and Affinity. Even if you expect an aggressive metagame, these four decks operate so differently that it is becoming increasingly difficult to build reactive decks. While there are White-Blue Control decks with Jace and Teferi, Modern players are favoring proactive strategies over reactive ones.

**Expect Ancient Stirrings**

By now there is no doubt that [Ancient Stirrings](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Ancient+Stirrings) is one of the most powerful cards in Modern. I wasn't too surprised to see even Affinity decks running a playset, even though that's not the norm—yet. [Ancient Stirrings](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Ancient+Stirrings) powers up Mono-Green Tron, Lantern Control, Red-Green Eldrazi, and of course Krank-Clan Ironworks. Combo master Matt Nass championed the KCI deck early on and was rewarded handsomely. He played three Modern Grand Prix between March and June, and his 3rd-place finish in [Phoenix](/en/articles/event-coverage/undated/grand-prix-phoenix-2018) was his worst result. He won [GP Hartford](/en/articles/event-coverage/undated/grand-prix-hartford-2018) and [Las Vegas](/en/articles/event-coverage/undated/grand-prix-las-vegas-2018-modern), claiming $22,500 and 21 Pro Points within three months.

**Expect Faithless Looting**

It's funny that a few years ago, [Thoughtseize](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Thoughtseize) would have been the number-one sorcery of Modern, and today, it's not even close to making my list. As it turns out, [Faithless Looting](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Faithless+Looting) is one of the best cards in decks built around it. [Careful Study](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Careful+Study) was already very Constructed-playable even during an era in which card advantage was generally valued over tempo and board presence. Adding flashback to a card that naturally enables graveyard strategies certainly didn't make it worse.

The Modern decks benefitting most from [Faithless Looting](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Faithless+Looting) are [Dredge](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Dredge), Mardu Pyromancer, and [Hollow One](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Hollow+One). In a way, Mardu Pyromancer and [Hollow One](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Hollow+One) can be considered two sides of the same coin, both black-red graveyard decks that quickly churn through many cards. Mardu Pyromancer executes this strategy within a midrange-control shell, while [Hollow One](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Hollow+One) is all-out aggressive:

[Hollow One](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Hollow+One) wins out over Mardu Pyromancer in raw metagame share, which matches the general Modern trend toward proactive decks. One big downside of playing [Hollow One](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Hollow+One) is the randomness of cards like [Burning Inquiry](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Burning+Inquiry) and [Goblin Lore](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Goblin+Lore). With two teammates to buffer a bit of variance, I expect [Hollow One](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Hollow+One) to show up in high numbers at PT 25A.

**Conclusion**

Modern is too vast a format to cover in half an article, but I managed to at least mention the decks I would personally consider for this Pro Tour. I would not want to play a control deck right now, and despite my love for [Bloodbraid Elf](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Bloodbraid+Elf), Jund has not been well-positioned for a while. For me, the big question for Modern is how dominant artifact- and graveyard-based strategies will be at Pro Tour 25th Anniversary, and what players bring to combat those decks.

# What to Expect in Legacy

First, let me tell you what not to expect: [Gitaxian Probe](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Gitaxian+Probe) and [Deathrite Shaman](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Deathrite+Shaman). The [recent banning](/en/articles/feature/2018/07-02-july-2-2018-banned-and-restricted-update) of these two cards, especially the latter, has shaken up Legacy in a major way. Four-color decks with [Leovold, Emissary of Trest](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Leovold%2C+Emissary+of+Trest) have almost disappeared. But Temur Delver and Death and Taxes decks are already making a comeback to fill the void in the metagame.

**Expect Lotus Petal**

There are fair decks that want to make their land drops, and then there are decks playing [Lotus Petal](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Lotus+Petal). If someone is willing to effectively discard a card for a single mana, they are valuing tempo over card utility in maybe the most extreme way possible. Hence, [Lotus Petal](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Lotus+Petal) decks are among the most aggressive combo decks in Legacy. Some of them happen to have [Force of Will](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Force+of+Will), but it's never an answer card, only a backup for the combo piece of choice.

[Lotus Petal](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Lotus+Petal) shows up in virtually all combo decks in Legacy: Sneak and Show, Reanimator, Ad Nauseam Tendrils, Turbo Depths, and [Goblin Charbelcher](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Goblin+Charbelcher). A classic Legacy deck that can theoretically win on turn one, but also in drawn-out late games, is Ad Nauseam Tendrils.

I expect a lot of decks with "unfair" mana at Pro Tour 25th Anniversary. Some teams, especially those without a true Legacy expert, could gravitate toward combo decks that promise quick wins. However, I'm always impressed how experienced combo players manage to position themselves in long games that shouldn't favor them from a strategic perspective. It's these games which ultimately make the difference.

**Expect Stoneforge Mystic**

[Stoneforge Mystic](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Stoneforge+Mystic) is back, and thankfully I'm not talking about Modern. White-Blue Stoneblade decks with and without red splashes are seeing play again, and Death and Taxes is back in force. The tempo gained with a turn-one [Deathrite Shaman](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Deathrite+Shaman) almost negated the threat of a turn-two [Stoneforge Mystic](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Stoneforge+Mystic), while providing the mana to circumvent the taxing effects of Death and Taxes. Now that the Shaman's reign is over, [Stoneforge Mystic](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Stoneforge+Mystic) is once again well positioned.

Miracles has been competing for the top spot in the Legacy metagame for a while, and—to my surprise—survived the banning of [Sensei's Divining Top](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Sensei%27s+Divining+Top) without much trouble. As difficult as it is to play control in Modern right now, Legacy has a pure control deck that will attract a lot of players.

The other [Brainstorm](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Brainstorm) deck I want to highlight is Delver, which was almost pushed out of the metagame by four-color Leovold decks before the ban. The most popular variant is Temur Delver.

This list makes me nostalgic, reminding me of the times when [Nimble Mongoose](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Nimble+Mongoose) was such a premier threat in Legacy that everyone feared "Canadian Threshold" decks like this one. There is something poetic about a Delver master operating on one or two lands for a full game, generating just enough tempo and resources to scrape out a win.

Delver decks, with their [Daze](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Daze)s, [Spell Pierce](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Spell+Pierce)s, and [Stifle](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Stifle)s, excel at punishing you for playing around the wrong cards. This means that Delver benefits disproportionally from the banning of [Gitaxian Probe](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Gitaxian+Probe). More hidden information means more opportunities to outplay your opponents.

**Conclusion**

Even more than Modern, deck choice in Legacy will be dominated by individual preference. I expect even more [Brainstorm](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Brainstorm)s than usual, because pro players like to be in control of their fate, and this might be their one chance to play [Brainstorm](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Brainstorm) at a Pro Tour. But that requires a certain confidence, because you will face the world's best players in Stoneblade, Miracle, or Delver mirrors.

Other good deck choices are a tier 1 combo deck, or Death and Taxes, which I would prefer over the more extreme Red Prison decks. Finally, we shouldn't count out Lands and Four-Color Loam. More decks with [Swords to Plowshares](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Swords+to+Plowshares) means that Marit Lage is not quite as scary as before, but some decks simply can't beat the value generated by [Life from the Loam](https://web.archive.org/web/20180802000000/https://gatherer.wizards.com/Pages/Card/Details.aspx?name=Life+from+the+Loam) recursion.

This concludes my overview of Legacy, and my preview of the Pro Tour 25th Anniversary metagame. It's a truly special event for all kinds of reasons, and I'm confident that the Constructed formats will do it justice. Apart from the deck choice alone, I'm most curious to find out how pro teams approached their testing and preparation, and if there is any kind of consensus on Team Trios Constructed.
