*Author’s Note: This article was written largely prior to Grand Prix-New Orleans. However, due to the possible apparent conflict of interests in publishing an article discussing a deck against which my deck for the GP was extremely strong, I held off on submitting this article until after the tournament. I apologize for the lateness of this information, but feel that maintaining my journalistic integrity was of utmost importance.* 

I have not done much **Magic** writing of late and, for this, I feel I ought to offer an explanation. I am currently in my senior year at Emory University and I am trying to both fulfill my various commitments there and still play **Magic** on the pro level. Because I still have a number of requirements to complete before I can graduate, I don’t have the leisure of going to school part time. Anyone who has attempted anything of this nature before can appreciate how difficult such a balancing act can be and, unfortunately, as a result, some of my activities had to take a back seat. One of these was my **Magic** writing. 

At the moment, however, I am on semester break and as such have some semblance of free time. Seeing as I’m currently in the snowed-in state of New Hampshire and I’ve played just about as much **Magic Online** as one human ought to, I figured I might as well use a bit of that free time to provide some insight into the current PTQ format: Extended. It’s been difficult for me to sit back and watch the commentaries written about the metagame and various decks without being able to bring my own perspective to print, so now I have that opportunity.

My view of the Extended environment has shifted a great deal since I first started working on it several months ago. At Pro Tour-Houston, I played Psychatog. So did just about everyone whom I normally test with. The problem was that these people I normally test with didn’t so much “test” for that particular event due to extenuating circumstances. I prepared for that event much less than I might have liked, but produced a deck which I felt was at least competitive in the format. My own result with the deck was quite poor, going 3-0 and then 0-3 and missing Day 2, due in part to several very significant mistakes. Others fared better, with Kyle Rose finishing 9th, Matt Linde and Gabriel Carleton-Barnes in the Top 32 and Ben Rubin in the Top 48.

After the event, I felt that, despite our lack of testing, the deck itself wasn’t so bad – it simply wasn’t sufficiently tuned. Our lack of Upheaval hurt us against Rock decks. Our lack of Brainstorm hurt us in midrange and control matchups. I’m still not convinced that Cunning Wish was fast enough for the field we expected in Houston, but the more the metagame shifts toward slower decks like Rock and Oath, the better it becomes. In the weeks following PT-Houston and leading up to GP-Reims – despite the fact that I couldn’t go to France due to conflicts with American Thanksgiving – I worked out a Psychatog listing which I felt was extremely powerful against the Houston field. It beat Reanimator quite easily, had a great deal of game against Oath and Rock in game one and almost never lost to either after sideboarding. The only real problem matches – Sligh and Suicide Black – weren’t really on the Houston radar. I expected a resurgence of the latter, but not necessarily the former, and prepared for that with a full complement of sideboard Smothers.

I suppose I have American tradition to thank, then, for my not getting mercilessly slaughtered by the crimson tide that washed over GP-Reims. Sligh was everywhere, as we all know now, and tore up the competition who’d been thinking in the same way I had. The Kastle-esque anti-control Rock decks and the Suicide Black decks were easy pickings for the swarms of little red men, who marched to a 1-2-3 sweep not unlike that which YMG accomplished in the Pro Tour itself. Alex Mack (incidentally, NOT of Nickelodeon fame) took down the other red decks with a deck suited to the mirror, eschewing the Goblin Piledriver rush strategy in favor of a more traditional Cursed Scroll/burn build.

As strange as it might seem, this is actually good news for someone who wants to play Psychatog. Traditional Sligh builds were never a problem. Firebolt and Cursed Scroll are simply too slow to compete with a Gush- and Fact or Fiction-fueled Psychatogs and it’s not as if there’s any effective way to get Mr. Teeth off the table with direct damage. The problem was Goblin Lackey and, from there, Goblin Piledriver. Often even Engineered Plague was too slow to shut down the rush of Mons Johnson’s favorite creature type. All of these direct damage-filled red decks will scare the Suicide Black decks away, as well, leaving the coast clear for Mr. Teeth to take a big greedy bite.

That having been said, however, without significant modifications, I would not play Psychatog in a major event at this time (author’s note – such as those modifications made by Team Togit, i.e. main deck Smothers, etc. that lead to Eugene Harvey’s Finals performance in New Orleans. Well done, guys). It is the only deck I have all of the cards immediately available for and it’s the deck with which I have the most experience in the format, but you still wouldn’t see me playing it in New Orleans, barring a major shift in the metagame or some significant new tech. The main reason, in fact, is not Sligh, nor is it Suicide Black, but rather a deck Brian David Marshall has done a great deal to profile on this site in his “Swimming to Venice” series: u-g madness.

When I first saw Jeff Cunningham’s deck at Pro Tour-Houston, I couldn’t help but laugh. We’d joked at the Invitational that everyone should play u-g madness in every format, but this, it seemed, was getting ridiculous. Jeff’s build certainly wasn’t optimal (as seen in his Feature Match against John Larkin, when he simply scooped to a Reanimated Visara, because there was no way in his deck to deal with it), but it was certainly a starting point and Jeff’s finish in the money showed that the deck was good enough to compete with the big boys.

Wild Mongrel, it seems, is officially the best bear ever. After defining an entire Block Constructed season, as well as making its presence strongly felt in Standard since it’s been legal, the world’s most ferocious hound has found its way to Extended. Sure, Wild Mongrel saw play on occasion in the pre-rotation format, but there he was just a little puppy, what with Swords to Plowshares, Force of Will and all sorts of other craziness. Now, however, he’s once again the cornerstone of one of the most potent decks in the format.

This is a bold claim, particularly given that no u-g madness deck managed to crack the Top 8 of either of the premier Extended events so far this season (Author’s Note: Jeff made it in GP-NO, but that was after I’d written this part). Amiel Tenenbaum came close, finishing ninth on tiebreakers at GP-Reims, but his is the only finish of note for the deck type in high level competition. What one must realize, however, is that PTQ seasons are hardly defined from the top down. The deck with the biggest impact on last year’s Extended was Miracle Gro (and, subsequently, its descendent Super Gro), which was first seen in Alan Comer’s ninth place finish at Las Vegas and then went on to tear up the PTQ circuit. Miracle Gro was an aggressive blue-green deck which abused free and undercosted spells to overwhelm the opponent while preventing his or her ability to effectively react. Sound familiar?

The fundamental strategy of u-g madness very much mirrors that of Miracle Gro, albeit on a less impressive scale – and thankfully so, for the sake of the health of the environment. Efficient, hard to remove creatures backed up by card drawing and inexpensive or free countermagic has been a recipe for success since long before Quirion Dryads started having terrifying growth spurts and it seems unlikely that this formula will cease to be effective any time in the near future. It’s only natural that such a deck would meet with success in the current Extended format and, given the power of Wild Mongrel in concert with madness cards, who better to lead the charge than everyone’s favorite hound since Jackal Pup?

And now it’s time to get up to date. The list above is the deck as played by Jeff Cunningham to a quarterfinal appearance at Grand Prix-New Orleans. Many other iterations of the deck have been seen, and have performed quite well, in fact, but Jeff believes quite strongly, it would seem, that these other versions, using cards like Merfolk Looter, are incorrect builds. The madness deck is, first and foremost, about beatdown. Kill your opponent before he can do anything about it – and if he tries, stop him with Daze or Circular Logic.

Jeff’s list takes this idea to the extremes. He has no tricky maindeck Ray of Revelation to Intuition for. He has no Merfolk Looters to draw out the game with. He has creatures, creatures, and more creatures, along with a healthy complement of countermagic to make sure they get the job done. Wild Mongrel and Aquamoeba come out of the gates swinging, and they don’t stop until someone is dead.

The most important cards to this deck are certainly Arrogant Wurm and Circular Logic. Three mana for a 4/4 trampling creature – which can be protected from removal for a single blue mana or simply returning an island to your hand – is along the lines of old Miracle Gro efficiency. Arrogant Wurm can’t even be killed by Pernicious Deed until he’s already taken a few bites out of the opponent, since his converted mana cost is five despite coming into play for only three. Nor can the Wurm be Smothered, making it one of the most resilient, undercosted creatures around. 

Many early versions of Extended u-g tried to take advantage of the Intuition/Deep Analysis/Roar of the Wurm synergy, but Cunningham realized that mana is at a premium in a deck like this. With only Circular Logic and Daze at its disposal, the u-g deck can’t try to get into a card advantage control battle with real control decks. As enticing as Intuitioning for Roars may be, it just isn’t fast enough to get the job done. That mana is better spent playing another Wild Mongrel or pumping your Rootwalla.

Jeff’s sideboard, as well, pays attention to this principle of maximum mana efficiency. For instance, Cunningham chooses to use Ray of Revelation rather than Naturalize. While, at first glance, it seems that this is one of those Intuition tricks, but with only a single Intuition in his deck, it is evident that Jeff’s primary concern is actually the cost of G to flash back rather than ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif) to cast. In Extended, every last mana counts.

The rest of his sideboard mirrors this concern. Submerge is a perfect example, providing the u-g deck with exactly what it wants – time to attack at no real cost to do it. Gilded Drake is the obvious answer to Reanimator, but also half of a devastating combination with Waterfront Bouncer, allowing the u-g player to take control of every creature his or her opponent plays. Last, but certainly not least, is Powder Keg, which provides an answer to early creature rushes from Red Deck Wins, as well as taking out annoying constant damage sources like Grim Lavamancer and Cursed Scroll.

U-g is an extremely powerful deck, it seems, in whatever format it decides to rear its ugly head. The power of Wild Mongrel in combination with madness cards is a force to reckon with whether you’re playing Standard, OBC or even Extended. Between efficient – even undercosted – threats and cheap countermagic, u-g is the ultimate in aggro-control. If you’re looking to beat down at your local PTQ and red just isn’t for you – or if you just want to pull out a deck you know well from the last Constructed PTQ season – give Jeff Cunningham’s deck a try.
