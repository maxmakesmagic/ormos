Last week’s poll was very close, with both the Lions and Mother of Runes taking home over 3,000 votes. The Lions narrowly took the race, most likely receiving a boost from their reappearance in *Eighth Edition*. Many players believed that the versatility of Mother of Runes was a much better value for the mana, considering that she could almost singlehandedly run a game against red and green creature decks. But beatdown ruled the day. It’s been along time since we’ve seen what Savannah Lions can do in Standard. It’s about time for them to return and lead white’s weenie barrage.

This week’s poll covers three of *Arabian Nights’* best. First is the amazing City of Brass. The longest lived of the three candidates, City of Brass has given players around the world an easy, sometimes painful method of accessing any color their decks may need. Useful in multicolor decks as well as two-color decks without access to dual lands, the City has seen the most play of the three. Juzam Djinn is one of **Magic’s** poster children and remains, to this day, the most expensive (in actual dollar cost) creature in the game. At 5/5 for 2BB with a negligible drawback, the Juzam has influenced the design of many cards, as well as teaching players that not all drawbacks are deal-breakers. The final card is Library of Alexandria. Long considered the final part of the “Power 10” (the Moxes, Black Lotus, Time Walk, Timetwister and Ancestral Recall comprise the Power 9), the Library was the first land that let you draw cards and, provided you play by its rules, was a personal Howling Mine. It was also one of the first cards to have a hoser designed specifically for it (Strip Mine, released in the very next expansion, *Antiquities*). 

### Remember Savannah Lions?

These bad boys debuted in Alpha, setting an unmatchable standard for beatdown creatures. None of the other one casting cost creatures in Alpha could match the Lions pound for pound (though most of them could trade with them, due to the Lions’ par-for-the-course 1-toughness). What set the Savannah Lions apart from other one casting cost creatures was its ability to beat down. While the other good one casting cost creatures in Alpha were keen to help build a mana base, they were vastly inferior to the Moxes and Black Lotus and didn’t see much play until those cards were removed in Revised (Third) Edition.

White, moreso than any other color, was quickly set up with a good mana curve on weenies, as well awesome support cards that black, its closest rival in the weenie race, could not quite match. White included Crusade, which was a huge boon to all of its small creature decks (and which black matched with Bad Moon). With a Crusade, even the smallest of white’s creatures could be fearsome monsters, especially when it was present in multiples. There’s nothing worse than Wrathing away several dangerous creatures, only to have them replaced by a couple of four-power guys that only cost one or two mana each. On top of this was Armageddon. At the time, it was white’s unique spell, allowing it the ability to freeze development of the board in favorable positions. This was particularly powerful in weenie decks, as they were most capable of creating a favorable board position in the first few turns, making Armageddon a natural turn four play. Additionally, the access to Disenchant gave the white deck a native defense against nasty cards like Nevinyrral’s Disk, which would otherwise wreak havoc on creature- and enchantment-based offenses.

With all those pieces in place, white still had to rely on its creatures to enable the strategy. Savannah Lions were the number one guy coming down, ready to attack for two (or three with a Crusade) on turn two. Sure, they had backup, but they were the ones doing the dirty work.

### When They Were Legal…

The problem the Lions ran into, more than anything else, was a set of Standard environments that were not altogether friendly towards white weenies. While the strategy was played to some success, it was shot down by decks that accomplished the same goals more efficiently and without weaknesses against certain cards (like Gloom) that doomed white weenie. The Lions appeared in early versions of Erhnam-‘Geddon decks, but were replaced by the more versatile Llanowar Elves or Birds of Paradise, both of which had a lot of synergy with both Armageddon and Balance. One of the most successful decks running Savannah Lions was Scott Johns’s Type I deck, the Zoo.

This deck has multiple avenues to victory. The first line of attack is the four Black Vises. A single turn one Vise was often deadly enough to ensure victory, lowering an opponent’s life total significantly. Multiple Vises, especially against slower opponents, could be a death sentence. The damage the Vises could inflict was multiplied in this deck by access to fast mana. If you’ve ever played against the Vise, you know the serious difference between turn 1 Vise, turn 2 Vise and turn 1, Vise, Vise powered out by mana acceleration. Turn 1 is when you get the most damage through. Against a slow deck, the Vises, by themselves, can win the game. Some decks just could not get rid of their cards quickly enough. Add to that pressure eight one mana, two power creatures in Kird Apes and Savannah Lions and the pressure is doubled. When you pile on the third threat of the incredible amount of burn the Zoo can generate, in the forms of Lightning Bolt, Incinerate and Psionic Blast, it is easy to see how this deck could be a powerhouse. Any two of these three components could make for a successful game and, when you also consider that your opponents will occasionally miss a step, almost assuredly costing them the game, it makes quite a lot of sense that this deck was very dangerous. All of these threat avenues are backed up by the usual Type 1 suspects, providing enough punch to refill spend hands, tutor for needed threats or reset the entire game.

### It’s Back!

As one of the most exciting inclusions in the soon-to-be-released *Eighth Edition*, Savannah Lions are generating a lot of buzz. With blue looking to take a back seat in the near future, it’s quite possible that weenie decks will flow into the Standard metagame to combat the new wave of combo/control decks and high-mana cost decks that the last couple of blocks have thrown into the mix. The inclusion of White Knight in *Legions* and the introduction of Silver Knight in *Scourge*, plus the return of Glorious Anthem are all signals that R&D wants to put white weenie back on the map. Will *Mirrodin* further boost the strategy? We’ll have to wait and see.

### The Big Picture

Savannah Lions may have been the first, but it was not the only two-power one mana creature. Ironically, white never received another decent one, while the other four colors all received at least one. After the Lions, though, they all came with drawbacks, some (Scarred Puma) more severe than others (Mtenda Lion). Green naturally received a good portion of these guys, from the first variant, the extremely shifty Ghazban Ogre to the echo-laden Pouncing Jaguar. Portal’s Jungle Lion has never been tournament legal, but its minimal drawback (can’t block) has caused high-level players everywhere to glance at it with jealous eyes. The Jaguar and Wild Dogs often found themselves in the same Stompy decks, paired with Rancors to make them even more lethal. Red ended up with the majority of the mediocre-to-bad versions, because red’s creatures often come with drawbacks (Ironclaw Orcs established this way back in Alpha). Some of the red variants are completely unplayable, but that doesn’t mean they all are. Red got the best Lion replacement in Jackal Pup and decent 2/1s in Goblin Patrol and Goblin Cadets, both of which were playable in the right decks and metagames. Black received a strong pair in the *Tempest* block in Sarcomancy and Carnophage, but little else. Sarcomancy isn’t technically a creature, but it does create a 2/2 for ![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif), so it passes that test. Carnophage is better, with a knockoff of the Juzam disability. With both of these creatures appearing at roughly the same time as Dauthi Slayer and Dauthi Horror, along with Cursed Scroll, Dark Ritual and Hatred, they were key cards in Suicide black. Even blue got into the action with *Stronghold*’s Spindrift Drake. While not as strong as red’s, green’s or black’s entrants, the Drake did see play in some decks at the time, never actually emerging as a real contender. The raw power of Savannah Lions (and Jackal Pup) has made the playable two power, one mana creature a rarity that can define a format.
