To the rest of the Pro Tour: my humblest apologies.

To Baby Huey, and Jonny Magic, and Nico, I have no excuse. There was a parting in the clouds, an opportunity, a chance. After I wrote the 2002 Year in Review a couple of weeks ago, Kai sent me a message on IRC. He was angry. He was mad. He’d won an Invitational and a Pro Tour and yet I’d had the insolence to suggest he’s had a down year, merely because he’d fallen below 100 pro points for the first time in a while, falling in the process to depths the rest of us can only dream of attaining. I lit a fire and Kai sat right on top of it.

Strange how his is the only butt left unburned.

Pro Tour-Chicago has come and gone and Kai once again reigns supreme. The new Sunday streak is up to 5-0, and, this time, he had to run as tough a gauntlet as ever, surviving the toughest draft table ever assembled on Saturday before toppling Jensen, Finkel and Herzog in succession to claim the title. Oh yeah, and the big check.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptchi03/647.jpg)Ironically, Kai’s eventual victory likely can be attributed to the man he most wanted to avoid, Bram Snepvengers. The Dutchman, responsible for Kai’s lone Top 8 loss in Nice, had Kai openly cheering against him, as the German feared his unusual draft tendencies and what they could do, were the German seated beside him. Bram won his final round match against Bill Stead, placing himself to Kai’s right in the final draft, but worried about his first-round-opponent Herzog’s deck, he drafted the unconventional green-white in the hopes of extending his survival to the semis. This left the door open for Budde, who drafted a power-packed black-red deck with Rotlung Reanimator, Cabal Archon, multiple Phyrexian Husks, Sparksmith and more. Together, deck and player paraded to victory.

Enough about Kai though. *The Sideboard* told you he won days ago and it’s old news. Here’s a closer look at my thoughts on the weekend:

### The Biggest Match of All

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptchi03/655.jpg)With no offense to Nico intended, the biggest match of the tournament took place in the semis, and I’m not talking Herzog vs. Stern. Ever since his win at 1998 PT-NY, Jon Finkel‘s name is the only one aside from that of present king Budde to be mentioned in the ‘best of them all’ debates. The dethroned monarch, who’d fallen on hard times, in danger of falling off the gravy train and out of the **Magic** world for ever, earned himself one more shot at the brass ring in Chicago, defeating Fabio Reinhardt in the quarterfinals to earn himself a date with the German Juggernaut.

The match was a solid one, with Finkel’s final-game mana flood putting to rest any debate between the two, but the stark contrast between the competitors was apparent in their demeanor. Kai was chatty; Jon was silent. Randy Buehler’s insightful assertion that Kai would want to win a hard-fought match while Jon would likely be perfectly happy with the mana screw victory painted very clearly the differences between the two men.

More impressive than the players themselves was the crowd reaction. Other games stopped, the room became focused and WotC almost ran out of headsets for one very simple reason: this was history in the making. It’s not often that one sees the two greatest practitioners of any craft go head-to-head in such an important match. For those of us lucky enough to be there, it won’t soon be forgotten.

A lot has been made about my choosing to play the child-like beast deck in the Masters, so a quick explanation seems to be in order. With 10 minutes to go before Masters, I still hadn’t chosen a deck, but my meanderings brought me to the conclusion that there would be little to no Reanimator or Slide played in the tournament. Beasts, originally designed by Brian Fox, has a solid chance against blue-black, especially before some moron who hasn’t tested it much removed Gigapede from the board just before the tournament. I think Psychatog is the best deck in the format, but I hadn’t played it much and didn’t want to face a sea of mirror matches. All in all, this was a pure, hardcore metagame choice, and who knows, if Jeff Cunningham hadn’t discarded on turn 2 of game three against Neil Reeves, I might have made the final. I doubt I’d be playing this in a **Magic Online** 8-man (where the bad matchups are commonplace), but it was a good call for the Masters. Thanks Brian.

### The Best Draft Table Ever

Before Finkel-Budde, the most compelling moments of the weekend had to come at table 1 of the fourth draft, which the expert consensus suggests was likely the strongest draft table of all time, at least as far as sanctioned play goes. In order to begin any such debate, Budde and Finkel must be involved, which they were, as were Billy Jensen and Mike Turian, who are definitely among the top 10 drafters of all time.

What really made this table so compelling was that the ‘lesser lights’ weren’t really lesser at all. Nico Herzog is a long time Pro Tour combatant with a 2nd place finish (2001 Worlds Team Event), a European Championship (1999) and Worlds Top 8 (1999), two strong Team PT finishes and a couple of National Championships. Dustin Stern is a veteran of the draft circuit who caught our attention with consecutive Top 8 finishes in American GPs a couple of years ago, while rookie Derek Bruneau, who started 7-0, seemed to flourish under pressures that would crush most aspirants. Most impressive to me though was Eugene Harvey, who may have drafted the smartest draft at the table.

Sitting at 10-1 going to the last table, Eugene was slated to play the only other 10-1, Kai, in the 12th round, with the two agreeing before the draft to draw into the Top 8. This left Eugene with enough security with regards to Sunday play that he could turn his attention towards helping CMU teammate and housemate Turian. With Bruneau declaring himself blue-red early in the draft and Finkel going green behind him, Eugene settled into white-black with strong table position, with he and Mike the only white drafters at the table, each coupling plains with swamps while sitting directly across from one another. Eventually, with Budde deciding late on white as his second color and the card pool distinctly favoring red and blue drafters, Turian would be felled, but not if Eugene could help it. 

The pick of the draft came with Eugene taking Daru Healer over Inspirit in the second set of packs. Noting the three drafters between he and Turian were blue-red (Bruneau), black-green (Stern) and blue-red (Herzog), Harvey took the significantly weaker card, sending the instant to teammate Turian, who picked it up sixth. When this unfolded, I’m pretty sure I saw a little smile spread across Eubroken’s normally silent face. It was a brilliant move. That night, on the way to dinner, Mike could be heard yelling through the streets “I was trying to draft clerics you hate drafter”. One can only hope he was kidding.

Woe is Gab Tsang. Sitting in seat three, his draft had started well, with Wirewood Savage and Spitting Gourna his first two picks behind an apparently white-black player in seat 1 and a blue-red drafter in seat two. He opened his pack to find himself staring at either a second Savage or the mighty Exalted Angel. What would you pick? I said Angel, because it was early and he was sitting behind the non-white player, but it’s definitely close and I asked a number of pros and the opinions were split pretty much down the middle. Gab took the Savage, choosing consistency over power, not knowing that had he taken the flyer, he’d have had chances at two more of them. Instead, American Ruger Hebert ended up with all three along with an Insurrection. Jens Thoren beat him in the 12th round to ensure his place on the gravy train, which was looking shaky as his twin Top 4 finishes last year are about to fall off the PT point standings.

### A New Day

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptchi03/card_bloodmoon.jpg)In an unanticipated move, Wizards of the Coast announced this weekend that *Eighth Edition* would come with a redesign, one that has been met with a lot of negative response. For those of you who are complaining, I have one thing to say: get over yourselves.

**Magic** is a game, not a series of pictures and just because the pictures change, there’s no indication the game will follow. Graveyards, life totals, hand sizes and the like aren’t going away, and those of you who are complaining are experiencing fear of the unknown. There is comfort in that which we know, and I understand that this is something different, but with the popularity of **Magic Online**, the game is starting to reach players it never had before and this redesign only goes to inspire them to play more. Stop thinking with your hearts and look at things with your minds for a moment: a more aesthetic redesign can only lead to a wider acceptance of this game. How exactly is that a bad thing? I’m guessing those of you who insist on maintaining your diligent complaints after this reality check are the same ones who argued Apprentice was better than **Magic Online**.

I opened pack 1 to see lady Visara staring back at me along with three other strong black cards. Ruud took Snarling Undorak, Thompson grabbed Swat, Menno a white card, Myrvig a black card, Wolfman made an obvious blue-red statement, Noah grabbed the last of the quality black and Seppo got the wheel of Pearlspear Courier and Daru Cavalier.

Ruud opened pack 2 and saw Krosan Tusker along with Lavamancer’s Skill and the often bomb-y Callous Oppressor. The question here is what the right pick was. Ruud elected to take the Tusker, with the Oppressor going all the way to Peter setting up a blue-red war with Wolfman, who got the Skill. In chatting afterwards, my contention was that Ruud should have taken the Oppressor and ditched green altogether. Here’s my argument:

In Rochester draft, the goal of each drafter should be to get as many cracks at rare bombs and other first-pick caliber cards by staying as far from neighboring colors as possible. If we look at the first pack, the real question becomes where does Ruud want to be? With Wolfman looking to go blue-red, it was more likely than not that Noah would go black-green behind him, taking archetype preference into account. Noah could conceivably go white, but his second pick of pack one was green, signaling his potential direction and with Seppo leaning white behind him, its unlikely Boeken was going to fight for *Onslaught’s*... well… white-headed step child. 

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>Toikka then had to make a choice: red or blue?</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

With Noah therefore assumed green-black, the white player Toikka then had to make a choice: red or blue? With both colors two seats in front of him, Seppo was definitely going to lean towards the mountain, whose card pool is just a lot deeper than blue’s, at the same time putting himself into one of the two kill colors (red and black), thus adhering to the rule many drafters have stuck to that suggests one must be either red or black to thrive in this format. If we assume Seppo is going red-white then, where does that leave me?

Well, as I didn’t want to be fighting a losing battle for white or red, I likely would be either blue-black or green-black, not wanting to leave the best card in the format in my sideboard. Blue-black is generally unpopular and there was a blue player three seats ahead of me when, more often than not, the blue player wants to be directly across from the other blue player at the table. This means its more likely than not that I’d end up going green-black, likely the second most popular archetype in the environment. In other words, with Wolfman sitting directly across from him as the only blue mage thus far and at the least a calculated possibility that the man in front of him, an experienced drafter, would understand that it’s the man on the right who controls each drafter’s fate, I think Ruud should have ditched the green, cutting his losses while he could, and gone blue-red.

Ultimately, I think it comes down to this: the difference between Rochester and Booster is that in Rochester, you can see what those around you are doing and take advantage by playing around their drafts. Yes, losing that Undorak would have hurt, but the question is this: would his resulting picks from the remaining fifteen packs in which he’d be drafting directly behind me be improved enough to make the one card worth dumping? More often than not, the answer is yes. 

Have a good week.
