At recent Pro Tour events and in online discussions, I've been asking a lot of top players the same question, "How often are your *Odyssey* draft decks just two colors?" Quite often the answer I get is, "Usually not."

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>Draft games are often decided in the first few turns, and spending even a turn or two looking at spells you couldn't cast can be fatal.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Part of my practice for PT-Nice has been experimenting with multicolor drafting, both online and in tournaments, and my results have been disturbingly good. In one notable draft, I didn't have a single close game despite playing four colors. It felt like an IPA draft where my opponents were running playable cards in two colors while I'd devoted a few slots to mana-fixers but had filled the rest with much higher quality spells. Is 5cG dead, or is it still a viable archetype? And, for those of you who aren't completely insane, what about just 4cG or running splash cards?

There used to be an axiom in draft that most good players followed almost without exception: draft two colors. Occasionally we would splash a third color, but the explanation was usually either that we'd received an incredible bomb or that the draft had gone poorly and there was no choice.

There were good reasons for this. Draft games are often decided in the first few turns, and spending even a turn or two looking at spells you couldn't cast can be fatal. Some colors required an intensive commitment that made the idea of a third color almost impossible. Add to this the value of signaling; by taking a solid card in your color over a better one in a different color, you improve the chances that you and your neighbor will cooperate. The cost of adding a third color typically outweighed the benefits.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/dc/1a/dc1af564bf281391e89dd343263be5ec2b728603fcad9ff1d9f3289c9eca38ff.jpg)*Invasion* Block threw this out the window. Common mana-fixers, powerful "Domain" cards, and abundant gold cards in both allied and then opposing colors forced drafters to diversify their mana base and run cards in three, four or even five colors. 5cG became one of the most popular draft archetypes.

With *Odyssey*, 5cG would seem to be gone. But is it? Not quite. True 5cG is likely to be rare, but *Odyssey* block drafters, particularly those drafting green or blue, should avoid limiting themselves to just two colors.

Before looking at multicolor drafting in the current set, let's look at what made it viable the first time around... in Urza Block. 

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/86/2c/862c68387c6f625d6b000433ba198e2fb1db80cf5a850a997ce8098666cb729a.jpg"><br><i>A great cycling card.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

First, there was cycling. Expunge, Veiled Serpent and Pendrell Drake were all powerful cards that could be turned into cantrips for two mana. This meant that you could run them without committing many land slots to supporting them, making the trade-off between card quality and mana consistency favorable. Cycling lands made this even easier: I lost track of the number of drafts I had where I supported a cycling splash card with cycling lands. If I drew them, I played them... if I drew only one, I turned it into something else that I needed.

Another important factor supporting splashing was the existence of mana fixers. Green had two commons, Yavimaya Elder and Yavimaya Granger that could fetch basic lands from your deck, as well as Fertile Ground that accelerated and diversified your mana.

Finally, Urza Block had lots of powerful commons that only required one mana of their color. No one would splash Expunge if it cost ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif) instead of ![2 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif). Powerful commons that didn't cycle but were fairly easy to cast included Arc Lightning, Blanchwood Treefolk, Congregate, Falter, Heat Ray, Path of Peace, Sanctum Custodian and Symbiosis... an incomplete list, and that's just from the main set.

We can probably identify the three most important criteria for multicolor drafting from this analysis:

![](https://i.ormos.uk/de/d3/ded3b19815d33536916e5eb968bbcdfb4f3676c3831018366f3383779a8f7bac.jpg)

First, you need good mana fixing. It's not enough to have spells that help you get multiple colors - they have to do so in a way that makes the slot worth it. This is a question of cost and utility. Lay of the Land was good because it cost just one mana; at ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif) it would have been horrible. Rampant Growth is good at ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif) because it puts the land into play, meaning that a turn-two Rampant Growth lets you get a mana jump on your opponent that can more than make up for not playing a threat on turn two.

Next, it must be possible to benefit from spells even if you can't cast them. In Urza Block this was cycling, but the mechanism isn't important - only the likelihood and utility matter.

Finally, the set must have a high number of powerful spells with only one colored mana required for casting them. It's no good drafting a deck that can cast spells with ![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif), ![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif), ![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif), ![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif) and ![White Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif) if the only spells you are able to draft cost ![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif), ![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif), ![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif), ![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif) and ![White Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif)![White Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif). With draft archetypes, it is essential to look at the odds of getting the pieces you want. Here we have to look at commons and to some extent uncommons.

So how does *Odyssey* block draft stack up?

![](https://i.ormos.uk/e8/81/e881cf5f0674488901a8051f716fad1b6e1077f812b96ec5c4aeb087e3755e7d.jpg)

The mana fixing isn't amazing, but it isn't bad either. Diligent Farmhand is a very solid 1-drop, often applying a bit of damage or chump-blocking before doing a Rampant Growth imitation. Four mana matters a lot in *Odyssey* Block, and the Farmhand gets you there on turn three. Rites of Spring is one of those cards that doesn't look great at first glance, but proves its value in play. Thinning your deck and getting close to threshold is pretty good on its own, and with flashback and madness you sometimes get the benefits without any card loss. Far Wanderings is a card where the casting cost is all wrong, but which is sometimes good anyway. At three mana, you really want to be casting a creature or removing one of your opponent's creatures. The early game matters too much for a turn-three Wanderings to be good most of the time. To make things worse, *Odyssey* block draft is full of good things to do for four mana, so the ramping effect of Far Wanderings (accelerating you from three mana to five) is much less useful than Rampant Growth would be.

At threshold, however, Far Wanderings can be very powerful. I've found that in straight u-g decks with a couple of Cephalid Scouts, Far Wanderings is sometimes worth a slot, since it is mediocre early but often great late. The Scout gets me threshold but eats up lands. Wanderings gets me three more lands, helping me cast the spells I'm drawing and gradually turning into three more draws via the Scout. Having a powerful splash card in the deck is often enough to have Wanderings make the cut.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/3e/91/3e91593214e27a85a375c5ecb54bd1e92f8332f8cbc142c5b0a8c8e322acc8be.jpg"><br><i>Odyssey lands can act as one-shot Gemstone Mines.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

*Odyssey's* various sacrifice lands can help support a splash as well as gaining threshold. I once splashed Shower of Coals in a u-b deck (along with Temporary Insanity and Pardic Collaborator) with only two mountains because I'd drafted four u-b sac-lands. I didn't have any mana issues in three rounds (although I certainly could have) and won two games outright with Shower. If you're only splashing one card, a sac-land can be as good as a basic land to support it, without hurting your overall mana base.

*Odyssey* Block gives players ample ways to benefit from spells they can't cast. Looting effects, Rites, and creatures like Wild Mongrel and Skywing Aven all contribute to minimizing the danger of holding dead cards in your hand.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>Keep splash options in mind.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Finally, *Odyssey* Block has a large number of easy-to-cast spells, making splashing valuable. *Torment* hurts this a little, with a number of black cards like Faceless Butcher and Crippling Fatigue requiring a heavier color commitment, but good common splash cards include removal (Ghastly Demise, Afflict, Patriarch's Desire, Firebolt, Flameburst, Chainer's Edict), utility creatures (Hallowed Healer, Chainflinger, Nantuko Disciple, Cephalid Looter, Gravedigger) and powerful mid-to-late game offensive creatures (Rabid Elephant, Mystic Zealot, Dwarven Strike Force).

So how should we draft more than two colors in *Odyssey*?

First, the sad news, for those of us who love *Invasion* Block because we can start off as 5cG drafters before opening the first pack: *Odyssey* isn't *Invasion*. Don't sit down with the expectation that your deck will be running more than two colors, let alone four or five. Be open to the possibility, but don't start off by grabbing the best spell in every pack and assume you'll be able to cast them.

Keep splash options in mind. It may not make sense to plan a multicolor deck, but it should be in your arsenal of options. That means taking mana fixers a bit higher than you might if you knew you were going to be just two colors and actively looking for powerful splash cards.

Value splash cards higher if you're drafting green or blue, and much higher if you're g-u. Green gives you the mana fixers and blue gives you card-drawing (which improves deck consistency and improves your chances of finding the right mana) and gives you all of the looting effects that let you discard cards if you can't cast them. If your deck is g-u it will very likely reduce the risk of splashing considerably. And, since green and blue are colors with little removal of their own, a g-u deck can often be strengthened considerably with removal from another color.

Evaluate splash decisions based on the rest of your deck. A fast r-g deck would rather not splash at all, since it wants to come out fast and consistency is one of its best weapons. If it were going to splash, however, Ghastly Demise is likely to be better than Patriarch's Desire. R-g already has ways to kill small utility creatures, and would much rather have mana-efficient ways to remove a fattie. U-w, on the other hand, has a stronger need for removal as a single powerful utility creature can give it fits. U-w typically gains threshold quickly (making the Desire better), and a lot of the creatures it can't handle are black (making the Demise worse). Add to that the very real possibility of using Patriarch's Desire over again with the help of Auramancer, and the Desire's value becomes clear.

Draft Temporary Insanity. I'm not kidding here. If I'm drafting g-u I consider Temporary Insanity to be a wonderful first pick out of *Torment*. You'll be able to cast it, you'll be able to borrow almost any creature with it, and it fits wonderfully with a tempo-based aggressive control deck like g-u.

Practice in the drafts that don't matter. Weighing splash decisions and figuring out how much help your mana needs isn't easy, and rules are hard to come by. I'm experienced enough with the archetype now that I know that I can run Kirtar's Wrath as my only white card in g-u and still find room for Ghastly Demise (don't laugh, I've splashed Wrath twice with great success, each time in a four-color g-u deck). You may lose a few drafts at your store until you get the hang of it, but when you get passed an off-color bomb in the Top 8 of a PTQ, you'll have a weapon in your arsenal that can take advantage of it.

And that's perhaps the best lesson of all... 5cG or 4cG or splashing... they aren't strategies you should set out with, but options you want to be able to take with some knowledge and experience. A year ago I wrote an article on drafting 5cG in *Invasion* Block as Barcelona approached. It wasn't my favorite archetype, but I'd spent a lot of time learning how to do it so that I could make the necessary draft picks and deckbuilding decisions correctly if that's what the draft offered. I didn't use that particular weapon until the last table, but when the chance came I was able to make the most of it and 3-0 the table, earning the right to get savagely beaten by Dan Clegg on Day 3. Hopefully some of you can use it to 3-0 a Top-8 draft and earn the right to play on the Pro Tour.
