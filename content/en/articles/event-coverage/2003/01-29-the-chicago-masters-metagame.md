Last year’s Standard-format Masters Series was the debutante ball for everyone’s favorite – or, perhaps, most hated – Atog. A full fifty percent of the Masters competitors in San Diego played a deck built around Mr. Teeth, including three of the Top 4 and both of the finalists. Since then, Psychatog has been a powerhouse on the tournament scene, taking titles in everything from Regionals to Nationals to World Championships. With the rotation of *Invasion* block from the Standard environment and the loss of such staples as Fact or Fiction, Repulse and Nightscape Familiar, many thought Psychatog’s reign had ended with the coming of *Onslaught*.

Reports of the ‘Tog’s death, it seems, were greatly exaggerated. If San Diego was Psychatog’s debutante ball, Chicago was its surprise birthday party. Twelve of the thirty-two Masters competitors played some version of Psychatog, including all four Gateway winners. Half of the Top 8 decks were ‘Tog and the semifinals saw this ratio remain unchanged. In the finals, however, suddenly Psychatog’s toothy grin was nowhere to be seen, with Ken Ho and his updated version of his Osaka deck taking on Franck Canu with three color Squirrel-Opposition.

What happened? How did Psychatog come back out of nowhere to make such an impressive showing? Is it really that good? And – perhaps most importantly – if it IS that good, what did Ho and Canu do to beat it – and how can you do the same?

First things first – yes, Psychatog is still good. Combine efficient countermagic, efficient card drawing, efficient creature kill and perhaps the most undercosted creature in the history of the game and it’s going to be hard NOT to get a deck that’s at least playable. Sure, there’s no more Fact or Fiction, Repulse, or Nightscape Familiar, but there’s still Deep Analysis and Chainer's Edict and now there’s Smother. Rather than playing acceleration and bounce, the most successful Psychatog decks now play more removal to deal with early creatures, winning the mid-late game against aggressive decks with ease thanks to their card-drawing spells and Psychatog itself.

Neil Reeves was one of the two Psychatog players to make the semifinals, and his decklist seems to be the better choice in large part due to a single card – Compulsion. With the shift away from pure control to a more creature-control-oriented strategy, Psychatog decks are more likely to end up with dead cards in hand in other control matchups or against combo decks like Mirari's Wake. Compulsion solves this problem, burning through useless removal spells and fattening up the graveyard to feed a hungry Psychatog. Many other competitors chose to employ additional creatures such as Shadowmage Infiltrator, but given the creature-kill density of the decks in the format, Compulsion seems almost strictly better as a card drawing tool against other control decks.

It seems that Psychatog still has it all. Card drawing, counters, creature kill – all the pieces of the puzzle are still in place, even if they did get a bit scattered and had to be reassembled anew. Why is it, then, that Mr. Teeth didn’t end up on top of the heap this time? Sure, Psychatog decks lost a lot in the rotation, but so did everyone else. How did Franck Canu and Ken Ho wade through a field of Psychatog with ease?

If I had to answer that question for Ken Ho with a single word, that word would be Compost. The newly creature-kill heavy Psychatog decks are necessarily skewed a lot more toward black than their pre-*Onslaught* counterparts, with some – like Neil’s – going so far as to have fifteen black removal spells between deck and sideboard. That’s a lot of cards that say “draw a card” if you have a Compost in play. While previous Psychatog builds hardly paid any attention to Compost at all, modern versions of the deck fear few cards more from a creature based strategy. Like Jeff Cunningham said in his mock Masters tournament report – “2x Compost, 2x Scoops – just like Raisin Bran”.

Thankfully, however, I get more than one word to explain Franck and Ken’s success. Ken’s deck isn’t particularly unusual – in fact, it’s so similar to his Pro Tour-Osaka deck that it’s nearly comical. Ken’s deck beat Psychatog with a combination of early pressure and a lack of reliance on madness outlets. While many versions of u-g madness might end up stranded with Arrogant Wurms in hand in the face of the barrage of early countermagic and creature removal coming out of opposing ‘Tog decks, Ken’s deck has only two Wurms for cards that require a madness outlet to survive a turn. Circular Logic is clearly much more efficient when played for its madness cost, but it can be used immediately upon playing a Wild Mongrel, as can Roar be discarded right away.

This consistency makes Ken’s Standstills all that much more effective. Neil Reeves explained the matchup quite succinctly with a comment he made during his match against Ho in the semifinals – “You attack me; I try to kill your creatures. If any of your creatures live, you win”. Standstill makes this analysis just that much more accurate. Ken’s plan is to get to a board position advantage, plop down a Standstill and wait until his opponent does something while he chips away at their life total. All of the creatures in Ken’s deck are big enough threats that this plan works with impressive regularity – and if something DOES fall through, there’s always Upheaval to set everything straight. With Deep Analysis and Concentrate, the card drawers of choice now that Fact or Fiction is gone, it’s even possible to fight through an Upheaval against a Psychatog deck that taps down low on its own turn to dig for answers. There’s got to be some sort of poetic justice in that.

If Ken Ho’s deck is well-tuned against Psychatog, Canu’s deck is a Psychatog-killing machine. Franck’s deck has nearly every card a Psychatog player hopes not to see. Duress, Cabal Therapy, Opposition, Squirrel Nest, Deep Analysis, Wild Mongrel, Quiet Speculation – all rolled up into one tight little package that eats ‘Tog for lunch. He even has Llanowar Elves and Birds of Paradise to Accelerate into all of them and Wild Mongrel to keep the pressure on while he does. What more can you ask of a deck?

Indeed, if I were to build a deck just to beat Psychatog, Franck’s decklist is probably not far from how it would look. Modern Psychatog decks are particularly vulnerable to Squirrel Nest and Opposition, with Teferi’s Response and Recoil having rotated out and Aura Graft rarely seeing play. The loss of Repulse makes Call of the Herd a legitimate threat once again rather than simply an annoyance to cantrip away. Even Smother is annoying for a Psychatog deck to play against, giving Franck both a cheap and easy way to punch his creatures through, as well as the ability to kill a ‘Tog played after an Upheaval at instant speed by floating mana, should the need arise.

Generally, however, that need doesn’t arise, because between Duress and Cabal Therapy, the Psychatog player is hard-pressed to keep anything in his or her hand, let alone set up an Upheaval. This is especially true if Canu’s deck manages to get a Squirrel Nest into play, which provides an endless supply of creatures to sacrifice to Cabal Therapy. After tearing the opponent’s hand apart, winning becomes nearly incidental – there’s not much modern ‘Tog decks can do against an Opposition or Squirrel Nest that manages to hit play. Wild Mongrels and Call of the Herd tokens make the window for the ‘Tog player to piece some semblance of a hand back together remarkably short and Quiet Speculation and Deep Analysis let Canu’s deck keep up on the card count.

Things get even more lopsided after sideboarding. At best, Psychatog decks get to bring in Ghastly Demises and perhaps a few copies of Engineered Plague against Squirrel Nest. Franck, however, has a monster sideboard, complete with Phantom Centaurs, Static Orbs, and additional copies of Quiet Speculation and Cabal Therapy – and even Ray of Revelation, if he’s really concerned about those Plagues. Unlike the Psychatog decks of old, for which Centaur was merely an annoyance due to the presence of Repulse, modern ‘Tog decks have no way – barring Chainer's Edict and Cunning Wish for Hibernation – to remove a Phantom Centaur from play. Just force one into play with the barrage of discard and, chances are, four turns later your opponent will be scooping up his cards.

With all these advantages, unfortunately, come a number of weaknesses. Between rounds of the Masters Gateway, I played my r-g deck against Gabriel Nassif, who was playing the same deck as Franck, or at least something very similar. The disruption-heavy, big creature-light build that serves so well against Psychatog is at a loss here, with Elves and Birds dying to burn spells left and right, stranding uncastable spells in the Opposition player’s hand. The matchup improves considerably after sideboarding, with Phantom Centaurs and the Quiet Speculation-Roar plan making things less hopeless, at least, but the deck’s low land count and the vulnerability of its mana creatures to damage-based removal make aggressive decks with burn a difficult matchup.

Despite these problems, either Franck’s or Ken’s deck would be an excellent choice for the current Standard environment. Ken’s deck is in desperate need of enchantment removal in the sideboard, as his trouncing in the finals at the hands of Canu displayed and Franck’s deck might be less effective in a field without such a high concentration of ‘Tog, but both have the tools it takes to win in the current metagame. Take them to your local FNM or JSS and enjoy kicking Psychatog in that big toothy grin over and over again.
