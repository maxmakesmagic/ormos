You never really know how well you are going to do in an upcoming Constructed tournament. You could spend weeks playtesting, only to find out that there is an ultra-powerful deck out there that destroys yours. You could play the best deck and hope to outdraw and overpower the rogue decks set out to beat you, or you can hope to play a rogue deck and face the matchup you want often enough to carry you into the Top 8. Or one of the best decks for the tournament could simply fall into your lap.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>I had no deck and no hotel room…</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

This is pretty much what happened to me at Grand Prix-Detroit. I showed up in Detroit early Friday morning having done about the usual amount of preparation for this tournament – I had no deck and no hotel room to stay in. In fact, I played less than a dozen games in this format since Pro Tour-Venice. Upon arriving I placed a few phone calls to see who I could room with only to discover that none of my usual roommates bothered to make the trip. 

Forced to get a room on my own at $120 a night and getting a few hours of sleep, I ventured into the tournament area where I put together a version of an Astral Slide deck with some help from Valentin Moskovich and went on to playtest it against his goblins archetype for a while. The results were disheartening. At Pro Tour-Venice, the Astral Slide-goblins matchup was a cakewalk. This was no longer the case. With so many good toys for the red mage to tinker with, the matchup was pretty close to even. 

I really did not want to play goblins and Slide was not looking quite as good. Paul Rietzl, the only other Your Move Games player in the tournament, to the rescue. He told me earlier in the day that his friends Lucas Glavin and Chris Chin designed a very powerful rogue deck, but could not disclose it to me. They needed to put together an extra copy of the deck for their friend John Clougherty but did not have the cards. Paul offered to share a deck with me in exchange for lending them the cards. I really did not expect the deck to be good – most of the time when someone claims to have broken the format the night before the tournament, they drop out by round six. However, I had no problem lending them the cards and so accepted the offer. 

After several hours of playtesting up in their room, I ended up playing the very deck list Paul showed me. Here's what it looked like:

In a field of 30%+ goblin decks, this strategy was a silver bullet. The deck annihilates goblins and zombies and has an edge over Astral Slide – which was a significant majority of the archetypes present in Detroit. The one bad matchup is monowhite control, and even that isn't terrible – you are looking at something like 40% win percentage. Maher's winning red-white control deck is a slightly worse matchup as well, but we did not know about it prior to the tournament.

The key card against goblins is Form of the Dragon. If you survive long enough to cast the Form, the only way goblins can win – literally – is by drawing three Shocks. If you look at the [Detroit deck lists](/en/events/2003/gpdet03/all-day-2-decklists) you will notice that goblin decks run no other burn spells. Carbonize is a bit slow and Chain of Plasma is downright weak in a weenie creature deck. Thus, unprepared for the Form, goblin decks' only out is to draw three copies of their only burn spell. Mathematically, this draw is so unlikely that I do not hesitate to cast the Form even if I am still at twenty life points. At this point, they are on a four turn clock at most. Usually, it only takes two to three turns as you no longer have to waste your direct damage spells on their creatures and just burn them out.

The trick is staying alive against an ultra-aggressive deck until you can play a seven-mana enchantment. Fortunately, it is not very hard in a deck featuring twelve burn spells, four Gempalm Incinerators that are fueled by your opponent's own goblins, and four Lightning Rifts. As you are able to cycle a significant portion of your deck (there are a total of 24 cards with cycling), it will not take very long to draw a Rorix or a Form. Although the deck is light on victory conditions, this is not a serious problem in a format almost completely devoid of countermagic.

Perhaps the only actual threat in goblin decks is the Goblin Goon. It isn't easy to take one out early on with monored. This is why it is important to kill Goblin Prospector and Goblin Warchief on sight. Usually, you should still be able to win even when a pair of Goons hit play – they just aren't fast enough and you will be able to drop the Form before Goons kill you. When Paul Rietzl claimed a 90% win percentage against monored, I found it very difficult to believe, but testing proved him right. If you plan on trying out this deck, play a bunch of games in this matchup yourself and you will be convinced too.  
 After sideboarding you have to assume that your opponent might be bringing in Stabilizer. Thus you take out the Rifts even if they are pretty good in the matchup. You also take out Lay Waste and one Form (two is plenty and they might have more ways around it after sideboarding). You bring in your Thoughbound Primocs, Sparksmiths, and a Menacing Ogre. If you suspect that your opponent is boarding in multiple Threatens, you might want to replace your Bladewings with additional Menacing Ogres.

Perhaps the only "bad" card in the goblin matchup is Lay Waste. It's still decent and even good with Rift in play – but it becomes your best friend when facing off against Astral Slide or any other type of white control. A timely Lay Waste could be crippling against Astral Slide – which wants plenty of mana to take advantage of its own Lightning Rifts. The matchup will often deteriorate into a Rift race, but it's in your favor because of all the extra burn. In fact, it is quite possible to outrace a turn two Silver Knight. You really want your Rorix to hit once. Once again, Lay Waste is very helpful here. You are more likely to catch your opponent without Wing Shards mana or unable to cycle and activate their Slide for a turn if you destroyed one of their lands on turn four.

After sideboarding, you bring in Misguided Rages, Primocs, and Ogres taking out Shock, Form of the Dragon and Incinerators. With eight land destruction spells, you should be able to hold your opponent off from casting their ultra powerful cards such as Akroma's Vengeance or Akroma herself, while sneaking in damage with your assortment of creatures.

You pursue a similar strategy against monowhite control, but it's less likely to work well there. There are too many creatures you just can't deal with in that deck – from Akroma and Dawn Elemental down to Silver Knight. Form of the Dragon can easily save you game one if they only have one Dawn Elemental in play, but it won't be as good post-sideboard. On top of that, monowhite control might actually board Stabilizers while you do not have to worry about that against Slide decks.

You have to rely on being able to slow down their mana enough to get in damage with Ogre and Rorix. Temple of the False God is very helpful both here and in the aggro matchups, providing you with an opportunity to summon a turn five Rorix or cast a turn six Form. 

To give you a good idea of how the deck performs in a competitive field, here is a recap of my matches from Grand Prix-Detroit.

Rounds 1-3 – Bye.

### Round 4 – Ken Ho – B-R Form

Josh Bennett covered this match on *The Sideboard* (read the coverage [here](/en/events/2003/gpdet03/round-4-feature-match-ken-ho-vs-alex-shvartsman)). I was somewhat rattled to face essentially a mirror match, but was glad because "Bad Form" has a significant advantage in this mana-hungry matchup thanks to the land destruction elements. Although Bennett felt otherwise, I see this as a very favorable mathcup as Ho's black splash does not bring enough to the table. Ken played very well and managed to make the match pretty close, but it worked out for me in the end.

### Round 9 – Mark Herberholz – Astral Slide

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i> I certainly qualified for Bad Play of the Week in game three.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Game one worked out real well for me as I drew three of my four Lay Wastes and was able to slow Mark down enough to finish him off with a Rorix, with him unable to remove it with Wing Shards or Akroma's Vengeance.

He surprised me when he chose to play second for game 2. Mark later defended his decision by claiming that "one should always play second in control on control matchup". Obviously there are no control elements in my game against Slide. I kill his lands and deal damage to him as quickly as possible. If he recovers in time, he will win. If he does not, I will win. 

Mark's lack of judgment did not help me much however. He was beating me down with Silver Knight, and I eventually died while holding three Forms of the Dragon in hand and enjoying my six mountains in play. 

I certainly qualified for Bad Play of the Week in game three. Mark was developing with Lightning Rift and four lands in play. I decided to go for the quick kill, dropping Form of the Dragon as he did not yet have enough lands to kill me with Lightning Rift for several turns. What I did not realize is that "reset to five life" effect triggers and resolves while still leaving an opponent opportunity to damage me. Thus, Mark was able to cycle at the end of my turn bringing me down to three life points, then untap and cycle twice more to finish me off. The moral of this story is – even if you pick up a deck the night before the tournament as I had, better take care to understand your own card interactions fully.

I was certainly happy with a fourth-place finish. Chris Chin and John Clougherty finished 17th and 18th – earning about $1,000 each in amateur prize money. Between the five of us playing the deck, we took home over 12% of the prize pool.

I would like to take some time out here to award some serious slops to Gary Wise. In his coverage of GP-Detroit [here](/en/events/2002/ww/wise-words-pittsburgh) Gary talks about rivalry between Your Move Games and CMU-TOGIT which, to the best of my knowledge, exists in his mind only. Further on, he compares team performances while failing to mention that every single YMG member he names in the article did not actually attend. In fact, I was the only player wearing a dragon shirt there. 

I can certainly appreciate writing about relationships between players and teams – and even fueling rivalries that may exist – for benefit of the reader, but Gary was way out of line in this case.

Back to the business of talking about Form of the Dragon. Yes, I would definitely play this deck again. There are only two changes I would make. First of all, I would replace one Solar Blast with one Rorix. A third Rorix would further improve the matchup against white decks. Second, I would certainly consider replacing Sparksmiths with another sideboard card. I am not prepared to suggest anything specific, but it seems like you really do not need them to win the matchup – so if you need room in the ‘board for whatever tech your playtesting comes up with, these are the slots that can be emptied out.

Once again, thanks to Lucas Glavin, Chris Chin, Paul Rietzl and John Claugherty for giving me a chance to play this deck. I have not had this much fun playing Constructed since using Withdraw in Miracle Gro at Grand Prix-Houston – being a step ahead of the metagame is what makes Constructed worth playing.
