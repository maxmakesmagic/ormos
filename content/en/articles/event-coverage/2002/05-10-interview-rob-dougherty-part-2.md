This is part two of my interview with Your Move Games owner and perennial pro player, Rob Dougherty. Part one is available [here](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/05-09-interview-rob-dougherty-part-1).

**Wachter:** Since Pro Tour-New Jersey where you had your first Top 8 you've had a lot more Top 8s, especially this season. People don't really mention you in the same league as a Finkel or Kai obviously, but you're also not often mentioned as being on the same level as a Turian or a Zvi. Do you think you're underrated?

**Dougherty:** Not really. I mean there may be players who are overrated, but I certainly wouldn't categorize myself in the Kai or Finkel category...

**Wachter:** What about the Turian or Zvi category? 

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>If I'm playing a format where deck design is very important, I'll probably do well.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**Dougherty:** I might categorize myself with that group although I basically feel that I am a specialist, in that my strongest area is deck design. If I'm playing a format where deck design is very important, I'll probably do well. If I'm playing a format where deck design isn't important, like Standard when no new sets are involved, then I'll play well. I'll play at a Pro Tour level, but not necessarily a "best player in the world" level. I certainly considered myself significantly overrated when I was the #1 rated Composite player in the world. 

**Wachter:** When was that?

**Dougherty:** Actually, it still might be now. 2195 was my rating coming into this event, and I lost to my teammate (Justin Gary) in the first round of the Masters, so my rating will be going down from there. I believe that as of now I may still be there, and that is definitely an overstatement of my skill. 

**Wachter:** Your Move Games has always had a very close playtest group, as opposed to other stores where people often form their own little independent groups. Why do you think your store specifically has that going for it?

**Dougherty:** Probably because I'm the owner who's actually on the team. It's important to me to try and be inclusive for practicing for events. My goal is to have anyone who qualifies through the shop... you know, any of my players who qualify, I want to try and work with them if I can. Sometimes we have so many qualified players that it gets unwieldy. I think the fact that a representative of the shop, in this case the owner of the shop, is actively involved in the team, and it makes the community tighter. 

**Wachter:** Your Move Games seems to have most of its success in Constructed Pro Tours. Why do you think your Limited success hasn't been on the same level?

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/ad/27/ad27eb78e7ee9d9bc2e81cd2981dce5e1dacc1ceddcbd2fff73cab0301b59518.jpg"><br><i>Your Move Games brought Entomb to the next level in New Orleans</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**Dougherty:** I think we have better deck designers than most teams. We have access to better deck designers than most people on the Pro Tour do, so it gives us an advantage in Constructed events. Whenever you go to a **Magic** event, you have as many advantages as you can put together; you play, you get lucky, you don't get lucky, your performances will vary, but it's all about maximizing your chances for success. In a Constructed event, you tend to have the largest diversity of deck powers. You'll get the most favorable matchups in Constructed events where your opponent's deck has almost no chance against your deck, or vice-versa. In Limited, the playing field is much more even. I actually enjoy playing Limited much more than Constructed because I find the games themselves to be much more challenging. Frankly, I do better sitting down playing a random match of Limited than a random match of Constructed because the Constructed that non-Pro players play is a Constructed where the field is pretty much known, and the best decks possible are available on the internet. There's very little deck construction skills involved in that, but when the format is brand new and there's room to make decks no one has ever heard of before, then you can really get a leg up on your opponent.

**Wachter:** How does your team test for Pro Tours?

**Dougherty:** We've used a variety of methods to varying degrees of success. Some of our best events we've been working with insane pro player Tom Guevin, who is a pretty good organizer of people. He's got good management-like skills, and if he's on the team, he often breaks up people into groups and various people are doing R&D, various people are handing off decks to other people who are then taking the new ideas and trying to tune them and playtest them.

**Wachter:** So it's a big operation.

**Dougherty:** Yes, a big, complicated operation.

**Wachter:** What do Dave Humpherys and Darwin Kastle bring to the table? Is there something that each of them is specifically good at?

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/WORLDS2000/images/227.jpg)**Dougherty:** Kastle is a really good deck tuner. He's probably best in a known environment. If the environment is old and it consists of a set number of known decks, Kastle is the person to break it. He can look at the field of set decks and figure out an unorthodox deck which is playing with janky cards, which just happens to kick the crap out of the three decks that the environment is made out of. If someone comes along with a rogue deck, there's a good chance they'll smash it, but if they're playing one of the known decks, he's going to win. He also has very good mana intuition. He's very good at just sort of figuring out how many sources of each color are necessary by intuition, that sort of thing. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/Worlds2001/763.jpg)Humpherys is obviously an amazing player; I would say he's the best of the three of us as far as straight play skill goes. He does a lot of homework. He'll be the one to figure out what's going to be out there in the field, he'll dig up the tournaments in every country and figure out what decks are out there, and give us a good idea of what the competition is. Obviously he's a good deck designer as well, but I don't think he has a specific deck design strength. He's more universally good in deck design, and not concentrated in any one area. 

**Wachter:** So that means your strength is the opposite of Kastle's.

**Dougherty:** Yeah, my strength is taking a brand new format, looking at it, and coming up with powerful decks and powerful concepts. Basically, my strength in deck design is best before the first tournament. 

**Wachter:** Are there any lesser known players on the team that contribute?

**Dougherty:** Well everybody at the shops contributes. People are very excited to give ideas for decks and that sort of thing, so the whole community... specifically, I'd say one of the lesser known players who contributes a lot is Chris Manning. He was a student at Harvard and plays at the shop a lot. He has a lot of really wacky deck ideas. If you're drafting and you're playing an insane combo, people will say you're "Pulling a Manning." He just loves wacky combinations of cards, and he's given the team a lot of interesting ideas over time.

**Wachter:** What advice would you give to someone looking to test a Constructed format?

**Dougherty:** If the field is somewhat known, build copies of every deck in the environment, test your deck versus a gauntlet. Play in a lot of tournaments, or simulated tournaments. Like, get your test team together and play round robin tournaments with both the decks you created, and the decks that you know to be in the field. Certainly give a lot of emphasis to the sideboard. A lot of people will work forever on their main deck and then work on the sideboard the night before the tournament. Working as you would for a tournament from very early on where you're working on the sideboard and the whole two out of three match scenario is very important.

**Wachter:** And for a new format?

**Dougherty:** For a new format, it's important to get a lot of people working for a little bit of time independently so you can figure out what other people are going to figure out to get an idea of what the metagame will be like. Figure out which decks are obvious, and which decks are ones that you just happen to be thinking of. Then, you can do a little bit of testing versus what you guess the metagame to be, and go from there. And obviously, the internet is becoming more important. Unfortunately for me, new formats are becoming like old formats where people are posting the ideas they get and the information on what decks are going to be there is becoming available ahead of the tournament. So doing the same kind of research you would do for a known field is important as well.

**Wachter:** What goes into wearing a Your Move Games shirt? Is it an honor, or advertising, or both?

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/GPVAL01/974.jpg"><br><i>The shirt.</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

**Dougherty:** I hope a little bit of both. Clearly there's an advertising element. Anybody can buy a shirt, but the people I give shirts to wear, which is basically the minimal level of sponsorship... the free t-shirt for coming to the event, people who I like. So, I hope that they play with some level of honor and I'm certainly not a big fan of the rampant cheating that goes on in professional events, and I certainly would never sponsor someone who is going to be cheating at an event.

**Wachter:** So is wearing the shirt something that's earned then?

**Dougherty:** Yeah, I mean I don't like to think of it like that, but there are people who I would prefer to not wear the shirt. It's not a pure advertising thing. If everyone would wear the shirt there would be people who I would wish wouldn't.

**Wachter:** Have you ever thought about replacing the "poorly drawn pigeon" logo?

**Dougherty:** (laughs) No, I actually love the logo. Basically, the keys to a logo are simplicity and recognition. The dragon logo is very recognizable. It conveys the right information of "shop that carries fantasy games" and it's got a good recognition factor. So I think it provides everything that a logo should, including some nice rivalries with New York.

**Wachter:** Why does Your Move Games keep losing the Grudge Match to Neutral Ground?

**Dougherty:** (laughs) Partially because the good players don't play in Boston, in the Grudge Match, like Dave Humpherys, Darwin Kastle and Justin Gary. Obviously the pool of players we pull from, from the random customers are very good, and the Grudge Matches to date have been close. But in two of the three finals our player was a pretty severe underdog, and for the random **Magic** excuse I'll throw in that the random deck pairings have not gone well for us. 

**Wachter:** Between a Pro Tour victory and countless individual Pro Tour Top 8s, is Your Move Games the best team in the world?

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/9b/35/9b352f8d96418a47ae7cb128c5a9ef6d7653d6344219a648b9094165f494da31.jpg"><br><i>Rob kicks back with teammates Humphreys and Kastle.</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

**Dougherty:** I would say currently we are the best team in the world, partially in that we are the oldest team in the world. You could easily put together a team that consisted of better players than the team we've got, but if you're talking about the team as a whole and not just the three player draft team (Dougherty, Humpherys, Kastle) we have players who have been on the team since back in the West Coast Legends (Pacific Coast Legends) day. We've been a team forever and we'll probably continue to be a team forever, so we've got a lot of consistency.

**Wachter:** How do you feel about the Pro Tour coming to Boston?

**Dougherty:** Fantastic, obviously. It's very exciting for the local **Magic** community. It'll be a great opportunity for the players in the region to come see the event, and as the local premiere event organizer it's very exciting from an event running perspective. So, it'll be a ton of work for me, but very exciting.

**Wachter:** ...A Star Trek wedding?

**Dougherty:** Ha ha, ah yes, the Star Trek wedding (laughs). Yeah, what can I say, my wife and I are really big geeks. Surprisingly enough, it was her idea. 

**Wachter:** Really? So it wasn't like you had to convince her to do it?

![](https://i.ormos.uk/c7/03/c7036a07b9226423583b7a45c18699c20b6879e47a5088a99c87d6dbc956df52.jpg)**Dougherty:** Exactly. And, she was actually a little upset about Alex's (Shvartsman) article on the Sideboard which indicated I was able to get her to agree to it, when in fact the opposite was true! But yeah, we're both big Star Trek fans, and we both don't like standard weddings. To me, the concept of spending $80,000 on a giant wedding where you prioritize your friends in categories of "invited to the wedding", "not invited to the wedding", "best man", or "random guest" just didn't excite me. So we wanted to do something small that was fun. I was actually thinking about doing something like a picnic and inviting anyone who wanted to show up, and have a cookout and a really low-key thing. She and I had gone to Vegas before for the Star Trek Experience and had a really good time, and she looked online and saw that they did wedding packages, and was really excited about it. So who was I to argue? It seemed like fun.

**Wachter:** Do you think you still have things left to accomplish in Magic?

**Dougherty:** For me? Oh God yes! I mean obviously Magic is a continuing challenge. I don't care how many Top 8s you've made or how many events you've won, you're always going to want to win the next one. I've never even been to the Invitational... hint, hint, so obviously I have yet to win an Invitational. I have yet to win an individual event, although I've had high finishes. I've yet to make the Top 8 in a Limited Pro Tour. So, there's a lot of ground left for me to cover, and even once I've done all those things, the challenge just continues. That's one of the fantastic things about the game of Magic, is to have a tournament structure that brings all the best players in the world together. Who wouldn't be excited to play a game they love against the best competition the world can offer? That's just fantastic.

**Wachter:** And you can make money off it too.

**Dougherty:** Well sure, making money off it... but quite frankly to me, it's more about the challenge. I mean it's great that there's money there and don't get me wrong! I like getting money, but for example I would find it very exciting to win the Invitational, and there's zero dollars involved in that. To me, that would be just as exciting as winning a Pro Tour.

**Wachter:** Plus you would get to make your own card.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/e0/69/e069657962b79b8fec4aa2e4392cfb4d861551d1bc0a7564598aa0aee13300b1.jpg"><br><i>If Rob did win the Invitational, he would be the second member of YMG with a card. Darwin Kastle is featured above.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**Dougherty:** Oh, sure, making your own card. But even without making your own card... just to be able to be in an event with the best players possible, and to be able to excel at that. That's really exciting.

**Wachter:** Are you one of the world's best players?

**Dougherty:** It depends on how big of a group you're looking at. I think I'm among the best. I've been invited to a whole lot of Masters tournaments, and theoretically they're the best 32. It's almost impossible to rate players precisely. The nice thing about Kai is that he's someone everyone can easily say they're not as good as. It's hard to rate players exactly, and frankly my play level alters a lot by how much attention I'm paying to the game and how much preparation I've done for the event. I think that's true for everyone. So, it's hard to categorize people exactly, but I'm definitely up there somewhere.

**Wachter:** What advice would you give to players who are looking to bring their game to the next level? 

**Dougherty:** Concentrate on the mistakes you're making in the game. One thing about Magic is that it has a random element, so it's really easy to make excuses. Like, if you talk to someone about why they lost a match, nine times out of ten it'll be mana screw, it'll be the cards they draw, those sorts of things. You can't do anything about the random elements in the game, but believe me, you're making mistakes. Everyone makes mistakes. I make mistakes, every player makes mistakes. Figure out what mistakes you're making, and correct them, and that's going to go a long way. Basically, stop blaming the game for your losses, and start concentrating on the mistakes you made, even in games you won, and try to bring up your play.
