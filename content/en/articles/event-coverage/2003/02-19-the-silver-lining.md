![](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/8e/b78eeeac205d206f91ee01c7f2929458343f990c2d2d0e3a076951e600530cf9.jpg)With the current Pro Tour Qualifiers using *Onslaught-Legions* Sealed Deck throughout the Swiss, the aspiring professional player must first make several key determinations before he can expect to achieve victory. Firstly, he must determine the comparative quality of the new creatures while reevaluating *Onslaught* spells due to the shortage created by *Legions*. Secondly, he must determine what deck archetypes will wax and wane based on what *Legions* has to offer. An appreciation for this new metagame is vital both in focusing on the most powerful combination available to you and in anticipating that very combination from others. When presented with a card pool containing consistent distribution of quality among colors, an understanding of what archetypes you are likely to face will lead you to the correct foil and therefore the correct color combination.

In order to introduce these applied principles of comparative quality and archetype focus, below I examine the Top 8 decklists from a recent sealed deck Pro Tour Qualifier in Atlanta, Georgia. Despite the hindrance of the random card pool, seven of the eight decks employed an eerily similar strategy, with the one rogue competitor using a successful foil. Through inspecting their card valuations, learning from the focus they had in looking for overall deck synergy, as well as deducing the sealed deck metagame, we can gain a tangible edge throughout the season.

I will first list the entire sealed deck, followed by the decklist. This will enable you to first attempt your own construction and then compare that to the successful build. The decklists are in order of Swiss standing.

Jeff wisely abandoned white and green for this aggressive red-black deck. He opted against using the aforementioned highly splashable blue trio in an effort to keep the deck fast and consistent. The two-color build also ensures optimal use of his zombie tribal cards, namely Deathmark Prelate, Boneknitter and Infernal Caretaker. One interesting omission is Frenetic Raptor, the drawback of which is actually a potential boon against green decks. Skirk Outrider also seems to be a natural fit given his five beasts (six if he had chosen to play the Raptor). It seems clear that both the Raptor and the Outrider should have made his deck, replacing Fallen Cleric and Crown of Fury. The crown does not seem appropriately suited to a deck lacking Skirk Commando, Snapping Thragg, or creatures with provoke.

Despite my disagreement with these two omissions, analyzing Jeff’s success is still a productive undertaking. Cutting the Raptor kept Jeff’s mana curve a little lower and this effect on deck synergy is undoubtedly important. While I would have removed Fallen Cleric and Crown of Fury, they are somewhat of a package deal: Jeff needs to keep his zombie count high to use a tribal card effectively. Black easily has the best splash cards, with Cruel Revival as a potent removal spell and Dirge of Dread answering the creature stalemates we will come to expect in *Legions* sealed. White has a lot of good creatures and the Armor, but they don’t add up to the raw power provided by red-green. Bart’s deck could be improved slightly by trading the third swamp for the sixth mountain as, with Explosive Vegetation, he should have no problem finding a black source by the time he needs to have these late game spells at his disposal. The Sootfeather Flock morphs, so that should not pose a problem either. I would also support swapping Shaleskin Bruiser for Flamestick Courier, which would lower an already top-heavy mana curve and assist in the goblin tribe threshold needed for Sparksmith, Goblin Sledder and Gempalm Incinerator. An important sideboard consideration for this deck is Wirewood Pride, which would be a valuable addition to the spell count against a green deck with about four elves (of which one can expect many in this environment).

Decks with red cards dominated the sealed deck portion of the tournament, with all eight competitors using red in some fashion. Black-red and green-red were clearly the most successful archetypes, with seven of the eight basing their deck around these colors. Also noteworthy is that the three players to reach seventeen points all played two color decks. Despite the fact that Andrew Gaffner, Brandon, and Bart could have improved upon the specifics of their splashes, they all limited them to just a few cards. Only Cole Stewart’s deck, which played cards that would be veritable trash against decks based in white or blue, successfully played the paper role against red-green and red-black rock.

This tournament’s results imply predictability. Red-green and red-black comprise the majority of successful decks. Furthermore, one should expect high creature counts and creature stalemates and should be prepared to win under these conditions. Game-breaking spells are the obvious cards to look for in your deck to combat this problem. The other quality consistent throughout these decks is focus. Lowering one’s color commitments and optimizing unity of creature type are both pivotal. Engulfed by the dark cloud of a random assortment of cards in an unknown environment, victorious competitors relied on a higher knowledge of sealed deck and environmental theory to carry them through. It is as encouraging as it is important to recognize that despite the specter of a few mistakes, these players managed their understanding of focus and the metagame to achieve what many more came to get: a Top 8 finish.
