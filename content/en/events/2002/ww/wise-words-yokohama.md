### 

Chapter four of the Japanese Pro Tour story is in the books. We could wax poetic about the beauty of Asia, the history of Yokohama and the like, but I got off the plane twelve hours ago, am seriously jetlagged, moving into a new apartment today and have too much info on the goings on of the weekend to fill these pages with that drivel. Let’s get to it.

### The Champ

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptyok03/672.jpg)

For years, Mattias Jorstedt has been attending Pro Tours, landing Top 16s and other solid finishes, but without the all-important Top 8 on his resume, he’d gone overlooked. Now, graduated from University and a card-carrying member of one of the planet’s better teams, Mattias has done something no other Punisher had done before this weekend: win a Pro Tour. Tomi Walamies had two finals, Jens Thoren one, and Anton Jonsson always seemed on the verge, but Mattias, after two near misses with Top 8 finishes in Houston and Venice, finally did it in Booster Draft, a format I’d guess he’d admit isn’t as close to his heart as the 60-card variants. 

This is a blatant out-and-out case of good things coming to those who wait, and there can be no doubt Jorstedt has taken his game to an entirely new level. My guess is that getting over the Top 8 hump in Houston has allowed Mattias to shed some psychological bonds, as reflected in his recent results. From those results, I think you’ll agree that the rest of us need to get to therapists as quickly as possible. 

The win leaves Mattias only twelve points behind Kai Budde in the Player of the Year race. Of course, when reminded of this, Budde was quick to point out that meant Mattias “only needed to Top 4 Worlds”, and that only assumes Kai will crash and burn at what’s going to equate to a hometown PT… probably a bit of a stretch. Still, I can’t help remembering Darwin Kastle’s “I don’t see why people think there’s still a Player of the Year race” comments to Kim Eikefet before Bob Maher’s leapfrogging him with a 2nd place finish at Worlds to take the honors. Speaking of Bob…

### The Master

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptyok03/673.jpg"><br><i>Maher and Nassif</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

The final Masters is in the books, and Bob Maher - whose major successes have all come with either Tinker or Extended - combined the two, Metalworking his way to $25,000. Bob, who is finally close to finishing school, has been contemplating a step back ever since the end of year payout announcement came down. Defeating Gabrial Nassif in the final, I have to admit, despite obviously being closer to Bob in light of our recent team up and seven-year friendship, a little part of me was definitely cheering for Nassif in that it would have just been so cool to have Enchantress win that big a tournament. Nassif’s deck was based off the only such deck to win a major tourney of note, played by Motokiyo Azuma to victory at Grand Prix-Hiroshima this past January.

Like Jorstedt, Nassif’s been on a roll of late, his finish in the Masters coming on the heels of his Venice Top 8. Earning his finals berth on Thursday night, Gabriel started the Pro Tour 1-3, but found little sympathy from his colleagues. Perhaps the best quote of the weekend came when he told me “this sucks, no one feels sorry for me!” I think he’ll survive with his $15,000 check.

### The Greatest Draw of All

I’ve always liked Antonino DeRosa. Brooklyn born, “Antonio” moved to Italy as an infant, only coming back here a few years ago for schooling purposes, learning English when he did so. Until recently, the big man and I had only been passing acquaintances, but our new team up has allowed me to get to know him a little better and fully appreciate the smile he brings to my face every time we chat.

This weekend, in an act that fit his character to a tee, DeRosa negotiated one of the great draws in Pro Tour annals. Starting his tournament with a 0-4 first draft, he was mathematically eliminated from Day 2 before sitting down for his second, where he proceeded to get off to a 0-2 start, a fact which, not too surprisingly, led to his throwing his deck in the garbage. One problem: he forgot to drop from the tournament. 

Seventh round pairings were posted and Antonio was paired against Florian Leroy (“Leroy Florian”) of France. DeRosa arrived at the table and the following conversation occurred:

Antonio: *Hey, do you want to draw?*

Leroy: (confused look)

Antonio: *Come on, let’s draw and go do something.*

Leroy: *Sure, why not?*

He got his one match point of the tournament. The sad thing is that the seventh round may have featured his best chance to win on the day.

### Quake!!!

![](https://i.ormos.uk/e6/36/e636e64646a780304bc2129a5742cdd88649d3d47c59711240975eec3a20cdd6.jpg)

At 2:30 AM Monday morning, Mike Turian, Eugene Harvey, Eric Froehlich, Paul Sottosanti, Ben Stark, Ben Seck and I were playing poker in my 22nd floor room while Bill Stead and Osyp Lebeidowcz watched, when the room suddenly started swaying back and forth. After Osyp accused us of shaking his bed, I suggested it might have been an earthquake. We all looked at one another, stunned, just for a moment, before a round of yells and high fives filled the room. Super cool experience.

  

### You Got a Game Loss for What?

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/60/13/6013a47c64ebe5543350ad88771422c8bad3dacff975d0719b3fc9d030669988.jpg"><br><i>Burn!</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

If anyone on the Pro Tour is going to get a strange game loss, Peter Szigeti has to be the primary candidate, but this goes above and beyond. Playing round fourteen against Tomohiro Yokosuka, Peter was about to lose game three, when an overly cautious turn by the Japanese player gave the verbose American one last turn to topdeck. Peter drew, stood up, wiped the just drawn card across his posterior and slammed the lethal Searing Flesh he’d drawn on the table with a yell for the win. The problem is the judges saw him do it and decided a game loss penalty for unsportsmanlike conduct was warranted. The result dropped PTR from 29th place to 57th, costing him over $1,000 in prize money and two Pro Tour Points.

When I asked Peter about it afterwards, he was remarkably calm. “I deserved it” he said among a large circle of onlookers. 

### A Little Bit of This, a Little Bit of That

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>The world seems to have learned how to draft.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

One thing Yokohama will be remembered for is the diversity of the Top 8. Five continents were represented this time around, with Tsuyoshi Ikeda and Masashi Ooiso representing Asia, Jose Barbero South America, Richard Hoaen and Jon Finkel North America, Ben Seck Australia and Jorstedt and Benjamin Caumes doing the honors for Europe. Equally astounding was Finkel’s being the lone American in the Top 8 of a Limited Pro Tour. The world seems to have learned how to draft.

Aside from the small inner circles of the other competitors, the Pro Tour seemed universal in its hope that Seck would take the honors when Day 3 started. Long Australia’s most recognized player, Seck has been fighting the good fight for years, winning two Grand Prix in the process. It is his demeanor, not his accomplishments that has made him one of the Tour’s most popular denizens. Seck eventually lost in the Quarterfinals to Ikeda, but just seeing him on camera was uplifting. Good job TBS (The Ben Seck).

### Bridging the Gap

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptyok03/714.jpg"><br><i>Potential Rookie of the Year, Ooiso</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

One pattern making itself obvious in Yokohama was the way Japanese players are finally integrating themselves with the rest of the Pro Tour community and vice versa. Time and again, the Japanese were seen drafting with the French, drinking with Your Move Games or gaming with Mike Pustilnik, and with the Masters win by PS2 in Venice and the twin Top 8s by Ikeda and Ooiso, it appears Japan has finally grown from its one-time status of the beaten up little brother of the Pro Tour. I expect the recent success is a sign of things to come.

### The Rookies

Poor Craig Krempals. Coming into Yokohama five points back in the Rookie of the Year Race, the Team TOGIT member had his strongest tournament to date. Going into the final round, it looked like a draw was likely going to be good enough to get him into Top 8, so when Mattias Jorstedt offered, Krempals eagerly accepted. Then lightning struck.

For Krempals to miss Top 8, both Ooiso and Finkel (playing in his eleventh Top 8, by the way. The old man is far from done) had to win their matches. When that happened, Krempals still had a chance at 8th, depending on how the tiebreaks between he and Rochard Hoaen played out after Hoaen drew with Seck. Here’s how it ended up:

If it ended there, it would be merely crushing, but Krempals woes were heightened by the fact the man he trailed in the Rookie of the Year race was none other than Masashi Ooiso, he of the 2nd place finish. In other words, for his strong showing, Kempals missed out on the glory and cash Top 8 can provide, all the while losing ground in the Rookie race to the tune of sixteen points. Unless World Championships is won by a newbie, Ooiso is going to take away this year’s honors.

### Oh Canada

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptyok03/753.jpg)

Okay, okay, I’m biased, but it has to be said that my home country is enjoying a resurgence to heights unseen over the last half decade. Rich Hoaen’s Top 8 finish (which he arrived at by going undefeated in three drafts with black-white clerics, by the way. His record with other decks? 1-3) coincided with 2002 National Team member John Stern’s near-missing with his final round loss to Ooiso and Mark Zajdner’s Top 32 placing to follow his GP-Boston Top 4.

Not since the days of Terry Borer, Eric Tam and Paul McCabe have so many Canadians found their names among the Top 50 in the Player of the Year race. Elijah Pollock, Steve Wolfman, Dave Rood, Gab Tsang, Jeff Cunningham and I all sat above twenty points on the season before Yokohama, with their performances there bringing Hoaen, Zajdner and Stern to 25 and 19 points respectively (Zajdner’s and Stern’s ratings will likely qualify them for World Championships, bringing them both to threshold status). National Championships looks to have as competitive a field as can be remembered, especially with old-timers Terry Lau and Pete Radonjic making Magic Online-fueled comebacks.

One interesting no-show in Yokohama was Ryan Fuller. Disqualified in Venice due to marked sleeves, Fuller’s status as far as suspension goes is still being debated by DCI investigators, and according to Chris Benafel (Who, by the way, appears to have finally found a team in Bill Stead and Zajdner), Ryan decided that a trip all the way to Yokohama in the face of no Masters payoff and perceived DCI persecution wouldn’t be worthwhile. While I can understand his frustration at being disqualified from Venice, if, as he insists, his markings were unintentional, I’m of the opinion that you have to be on your best behavior when you come back from suspension. The verdict on whether Ryan will decide to make a serious return is still out.
