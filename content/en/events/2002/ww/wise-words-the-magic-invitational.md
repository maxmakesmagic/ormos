### 

As I begin writing this, I've been home for 27 hours and am finally starting to recover from the Invitational, the goings on surrounding it and the lack of sleep associated with both, so it seemed the time was right to sit down with my trusty laptop and put my residual recollections about the Invitational on paper.

### The Participants

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/17/f2/17f25f2cdbfbc1d240bcebff6998f97082fee44c22b28d800963aebd54ee60fc.jpg"><br><i>Get well, Zvi!</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

All in all, I think the world has to be pretty happy with the eventual sixteen players in this year's tournament. Yes, the omissions of Mike Turian and Ben Rubin were unfortunate, as was Zvi Mowshowitz's (very legitimate) decision to drop from the tournament because of health concerns, but the remaining group not only had a good time, but also did the tournament justice by not only playing **Magic** well, but having a lot of fun while doing so.

It's interesting to note that while not all of the participants really prepared for the tournament, the two late replacements, Scott Johns and Olivier Ruel had more trouble than most. Scott started out with a 0-6 day one before rebounding to go 6-3 the rest of the way, while Olivier finished last. For what it's worth, it was obvious from the get go that the Frenchman was there to have fun, with dozens of stuffed animals surrounding him as he played for the right to make his card, a ![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) casting cost enchantment that would make all penguins blue. It would have been interesting to see if his winning the Invitational would have spawned a new **Magic** race, but now we may have to wait until next year to find out.

Of course the biggest deal with Olivier's performance is the possibility that it may spawn a Pro Tour Champion. The last three Invitationals have seen their sixteenth place finisher go on to win the next Pro Tour, with Steve O'Mahoney-Schwartz winning PT-LA 1999, Potato Nation winning New York 2000 and Zvi finishing Tokyo as the last man standing, so it has become almost traditional to expect the player who earns that elusive 16th place slot to flourish soon thereafter. If he does so, Olivier will likely be attending next year's Invitational in Buenos Aires.

Meanwhile, at the top of the standings, Kai Budde was at it again, winning yet another tournament, it seemed, almost effortlessly. Universally being regarded now as the best player in the world ("I don't think you can argue that" said long time Finkel supporter Chris Pikula), Kai breezed through the Invitational, likely spurned on by his 0-8 start a year ago. Interestingly, the streak Kai's started at the Invitational in Sydney, where he turned his tournament around finishing 7-0 and kept on winning, finishing well at his next major tournament, GP-Frankfurt, before winning Chicago, Barcelona, New York, London and over $100,000 to go with them.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/MI01/795.jpg"><br><i>Finalist Dan Clegg</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

Most memorable about Kai's performance this year was the way he won the final. Facing Dan Clegg, Kai jumped out to a two matches to none lead before Clegg evened the score, leaving but one format, 5-Color to play. Before the final match, Kai told me that his chances of winning were less than one in five, with his deck virtually incapable of defeating fast creature decks like Clegg's, but he won the first game, taking enough in winnings that if he were to lose a Jewelled Bird in each of the next two, it would be enough for the win. That's exactly what happened, with Kai top decking Tinker, sacrificing Fellwar Stone to get a Jewelled Bird the turn before being beaten by Ritual, Ritual, Hatred. Clegg said after 'It's too bad. I think I really would have appreciated it more than Kai', who seems to win at will these days. Regardless of that perspective, winning the Invitational is a monkey off Budde's back. You can be sure he'll enjoy the victory.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/MI01/973.jpg"><br><i>Dance Dance Fujita!</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

Perhaps the most memorable moment of the weekend came before the tournament even started. With the Invitational tour consisting of visits to two wineries, more than one player (not to mention reporters and WotC staff) found themselves a little inebriated. In high spirits after a day of sailing, petting cheetahs and drinking, we retired to an African restaurant where we were served a meal of traditional African dishes and music. At the end of the meal, while our own personal band played, some of the waitresses started dancing. Tsuyoshi Fujita, who was sitting next to me, motioned that I should get up and dance, but when I countered that he should do so, one of the waitresses noticed and pulled him up on stage. Instead of standing there unsure of himself, the first ever Japanese player to make a PT Top 8 erupted in dance, thrashing his head, swinging his hips and outmanuvering the women surrounding him. Despite the language barriers involved, it was obvious that the bond **Magic** provided made Tsuyoshi one of the players as well as one of the guys.

Meanwhile, it was Scott Richards who was busy proving that the representatives from **Magic's** developing continents belonged at the Invitational. If not for an unfortunate error in the final game of the final round that cost him his match against Kai Budde, it would have been Richards, not Dan Clegg, who would have faced the German Juggernaut in the final. As it was, Scott, who traveled to the event with this year's version of Ben Seck, James White, finished in third with a 10-5 record, meanwhile finally making himself familiar with the Pro Tour stars with who he's long shared tables without sharing equal glory.

### Helpful Souls

As great as the devalued rand (currency) and the very fact of being in Africa were, I think I can speak for the entire group by saying the trip wouldn't have been nearly as much fun if life there weren't made so easy by the WotC staff and tournament staff attending the event. From Tournament Organizer Carl Crook to Invitational godfather Mark Rosewater to South African distributor Peter Coode, a lot of help with regards to transportation and luxury came from up top.

Just as invaluable to the players were those people who helped us with the little things: Natanya, Dennis, Emily and Mark, who drove us to dinners and tours, Andrew Monteith and Andrew Mitchell, local semi-pros who showed us the sights and provided cards to a few of us, Cutting Edge Collectibles, who provided what cards they could to those who needed them despite having brought those very cards for sales purposes and the aforementioned James White, who not only provided cards but good times for a number of the players. If I forgot anyone, I apologize, but trust me, your efforts were appreciated.

### The Tournament

![](https://i.ormos.uk/20/4f/204f39dbedaa08343214b94f4d95d55d789bbbb40ea1eb2888988e4bd082905c.jpg)In the context of the tournament itself, a few interesting things did happen. Perhaps the most memorable match of the tournament, Scott Johns clash with Scott Richards in 5-Color provided two memorable moments: First, Johns won game 1 when he cast Dream Halls, the center of the deck he played in honor of ill teammate Zvi Mowshowitz. Cycling through his deck with a number of card drawers, cantrips and tutors, Johns finally found the two-card combination he'd been looking for throughout the format, finishing Richards off by casting the one Atogatog in his deck and then winning the game with his sole Coalition Victory. 

Winning that game and the next, Johns was feeling very confident that his match, which was going especially long (a constant problem throughout the tournament was the length of the untimed matches, with Day 1's six rounds taking fourteen hours) was locked away. Richards, down by $24 worth of cards, needed to cut to a Mana Drain, power blue card or Mox in order to keep the match going, and with all other competitors done their fifth round matches, there as a collective sigh when he did just that, pulling Mox Pearl from the Godzillan's deck and going on to win the match as a result.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/b4/59/b459ca638020614291e56e2e98a5ac63da35025f6944a5b7869b82104f8a6721.jpg)The most interesting deck in Standard, the Invitational format that will likely most effect future metagames, had to be Tsuyoshi Fujita's. Based loosely on Domain, Fujita's deck used cards like Rampant Growth and Harrow to attain the full domain while also accelerating the deck's mana, cards like Concentrate and Allied Strategies to draw cards, and Early Harvest to keep piling up mana to the point where he would burn his opponent's out. Counter-based decks would have given him problems if not for main deck Orim's Chants and sideboarded Mana Shorts. The deck's only true downfall was how weak it was against Meddling Mage, with a second turn Mage naming Ghitu Fire forcing a concession. Still, it was a lot of fun to see in action and play against, and it could eventually lead towards the formation of an important archetype.

By the way: the best deck name floating around the Invitational? Peekula, which combines the *Odyssey* catnip Peek, which allows a player to look at their opponent's hand and draw a card, and Meddling Mage, Chris Pikula's card, which becomes much more effective when one knows what their opponent is playing, let along holding in their hand.

By the way, Pt. 2: If you're wondering, I would choose to play the deck Kai played in Standard if I had it to do over again. I tried it afterwards in gunslinging and it was a lot of fun to play, not to mention really strong.

That's about it for this week. Thanks again to the entire South African **Magic** community for their enthusiasm, helpfulness and participation in my week and a half there. I had a great time and look forward to the day when I can revisit. To the rest of you, as per usual, I wish you a good week.
