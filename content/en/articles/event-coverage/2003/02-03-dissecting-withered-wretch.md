I actually wrote a different "preview" article about Withered Wretch for *Sideboard* Magazine, long before I saw the rest of the *Legions* spoiler; this article is more about looking at the card in context, the kinds of decks that can play with it and, perhaps more importantly, the kinds of decks that will be affected by it.

The most obvious thing about Withered Wretch is that it is a fairly efficient beater: two-power creatures for a mere two mana have been the standard for clocking opponents since Black Knight and Elvish Archers inspired the first aggressive strategies, close to ten years ago. On its face, Withered Wretch is an efficient attacker. On the other hand, if it were only a two-power beater for two mana, we would all pass on this card, except perhaps on principle of numbers redundancy, because black already gives us a number of two-drops that are either more synergistic with the color (Mesmeric Fiend), more devastatingly powerful (Nantuko Shade), or just plain bigger (Wretched Anurid). Obviously, the reason this guy is inspiring any articles at all is that his additional ability is so relevant in the current Constructed formats:

*1: Remove target card in a graveyard from the game.*

Let me put it to you this way: for the past two years, Rob Dougherty has, basically, beat up the rest of the Pro Tour by finding Nether Spirit with his Entomb. I'm thinking that, come this year's Extended tournament, he might have to find a new deck.

At first sniff, Withered Wretch smells kind of like black's Spellbane Centaur. He's hanging out in the black creature locker room, about as big as the guys in his same mana cost, looking to get some play time in the big deck. Sure, he's not the heaviest hitter in the world, but he's really good at messing up opponents' strategies... well, some opponents' strategies, anyway. Against your average opponent, he might just be the sixth or seventh guy you have on the team. You call him up when Nantuko Shade needs a breather or Dauthi Horror fouls out. He's not a first-string player, but he's in the lineup because he's good for a couple of points a game. But, like any player who catches the coach’s eye enough to make the lineup at all, Withered Wretch is capable of having the big game… He happens to get the hot hand against opponents playing the *Odyssey* Block mechanics.

There is currently no consensus as to what the best u-g strategy is in Standard. You have the vanilla madness decks, with very consistent deck kernels based around the same basic cards, possibly adding Quiet Speculation to optimize Deep Analysis and Roar of the Wurm, perhaps adding maindeck spoilers like Ray of Revelation or Moment's Peace. You have threshold-based decks like the one [Levy played](/en/events/2002/worlds02/2002-world-championships-standard-decks-g-m) to an excellent record at 2002 Worlds, accumulating large graveyards to make Nimble Mongoose and Seton's Scout look like all-stars, setting up more consistent Wonder offenses and recouping threats over the long game with Genesis. You have flashback/threshold hybrids, like the one [edt played](/en/events/2002/champs2002decks/2002-champs-decklists-108) at this year's Michigan States, which are elegant decks balancing the power of Grizzly Fate with the versatile defense of Cunning Wish. And, of course, you have the standouts from the recent Masters in Chicago: Canu's three-color Squirrel Opposition deck and Ken Ho's Standstill/Upheaval remake of... well... Ken Ho's other Standstill/Upheaval deck.

The thing is, by this year's Regional Championships, the u-g field may narrow a great deal; we may have a consensus deck (I think the madness deck, probably without Quiet Speculation), purely because Withered Wretch constricts the other strategies. The major advantage of Levy's Worlds deck is its heavy component in the cheap deck manipulation category (Careful Study and Mental Note) fueling deck velocity. Playing Levy's deck is like playing Alan Comer's 1997 Turbo Xerox deck, but instead of moving through the deck to ignore land and pick up permission, you are discarding Wonder and drawing more cheap attackers. Levy's deck, on its face, gets the most from *Onslaught*: Flooded Strand and Windswept Heath. This makes an already solid strategy more consistent. On the other hand, look at how many cards it plays - even giving up card economy - just to set up that fat graveyard: Breakthrough, Careful Study, Genesis, Mental Note, Roar of the Wurm, Wonder... add to that the list of cards that are pretty unspectacular without seven cards in the ‘yard: Nimble Mongoose, Seton's Scout, Werebear... an unchecked Withered Wretch is a house against this kind of deck. Though it is fairly unspectacular in terms of efficient mana cost-to-power ratio, though it has a minimal effect on traditional card counting, Withered Wretch turns the Levy deck's best card into expensive Llanowar Elves. I mean Llanowar Elves are pretty tight, but I don't see anyone paying two mana for them.

Now look at a deck like the one edt played at Michigan States. I'm not sure if this deck is more powerful or more beautifully tuned. When I first saw it, I wondered why it used the inherently limited Grizzly Fate instead of the infinitely powerful, Psychatog stopping, game controlling, universally accepted, Squirrel Nest. Then I realized that Grizzly Fate tokens don't lay down and die for a cycled Slice and Dice, that even without Opposition, a Fate with threshold has the offensive power of a Deranged Hermit and that few cards have quite the same potential to play simultaneous, immediate offense and defense. At the same time, Grizzly Fate is not vulnerable to the primary mirror match (or Cunning Wish) strategy of Aura Graft and few decks can beat an Opposition chained into a successful Grizzly Fate. Unlike the comparatively slow development of Squirrel Nest, Opposition into Grizzly Fate may well mean, for an unlucky opponent, one turn of getting permanents tapped down, followed by a Fate flashback and a swing for 8+ damage in the same, following, turn. Ouch.

Against Withered Wretch, Grizzly Fate might just be a more expensive Acorn Harvest. Also ouch.

For any number of builds, Quiet Speculation becomes a liability. Where some decks use this card to turbo-charge threshold or Circular Logic and all u-g decks use the card to enable cheap card advantage through Deep Analysis and Roar of the Wurm, against Withered Wretch, basically everyone is going to have to be more strategic about when they cast Quiet Speculation. Either short-term threshold or immediate flashback is going to have to be the base goal... otherwise u-g decks can be looking at a huge loss of card economy. At the same time, holding back on Quiet Speculation has problems of its own. For one thing, once-beautiful mana curves will hit stumbling blocks. For another, this card, designed to hit the graveyard anyway, may become prey for Duress and Cabal Therapy... only it will not change zones on top of three flashback spells. This is a sad fate, indeed, for a card that once prompted Pat Chapin to suggest the banning of Roar of the Wurm.

And none of this really speaks to the central u-g graveyard card: Wonder. Where once Wonder was the key to both winning the mirror and racing any number of decks, that most popular of incarnations may be of little help in flying over the next generation of black ground forces, instead asking for the tap of a single swamp.

Of course, Withered Wretch has any number of applications in other matchups. It can stop a Wake deck from repeated Time Walks (Moment's Peace style, though a dead opponent may well keep Time Stretch from ever happening); in Extended, an open mana or two can rain on the parade of Benzo and Oath players alike. It can screw up the Turbo Land combo engine and erase the Angry half of the Hermit deck... heck, it can erase the Sutured Ghoul while it's at it. Yes, Withered Wretch, for such a small cost, certainly can be quite a monkey wrench in the machinery of many decks in many formats. The disincentives it creates certainly are everywhere... even in the development of black decks who might play it.

As I see it, there are two disincentives to Withered Wretch from the other side of the table. The first of them is: what kind of deck will want to play Withered Wretch? The dominant black deck in Standard is black control, descended from the Osaka black decks, made great by the swamp-friendly *Torment* expansion. These decks are all about raw power. They destroy hands, wipe the board of creatures and erase graveyards. These decks do not want to play Withered Wretch. If they play creatures at all, they will either be game-enders like Visara the Dreadful or Phage, the Untouchable, or imitation blue cards, like Undead Gladiator. If these decks delve into the pool of two-drops, they will first go for Nantuko Shade, a game-ender in his own right, or Mesmeric Fiend, making their hand destruction redundant while giving them an early creature. These powerful decks will never really want Withered Wretch... they are apt to destroy their own forces with Innocent Blood and Mutilate, after all, and if it is the graveyard they are after, they can opt for the most powerful bin cleaner of all: Haunting Echoes. I do not know that these decks are interested in a 2/2 for ![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif), even as a sideboard card.

Therefore, in Standard, Withered Wretch will have to be played in a new, as yet unrevealed, archetype. Likely it will have to be faster than the OBC Pirates! deck (especially as Braids isn't so hot against control these days), probably alongside other fast weenies and 8-12 maindeck hand destruction, possibly as a sidekick to Graveborn Muse. I am perfectly willing to believe that a fast black deck, chock full of Duresses and Cabal Therapies, playing maindeck Chainer's Edicts, Smothers or Faceless Butchers, fighting all zones equally aggressively with Withered Wretch, will be a beating against some of the established archetypes, at least maindeck. The problem is that as problematic as this deck might be for the rest of the world, no u-g deck is going to lie down for it, as long as it sees Withered Weenie coming. 

What does Withered Wretch really have to say about the core of the madness deck? If players want to go the way of Ken Ho and play with Standstill instead of Quiet Speculation, is there much effect at all? Ken could very easily rebalance his Wurm count away from the Roars and towards the Arrogant fellows, to reduce Withered Wretch liability even more, or increase his Aquamoeba and Merfolk Looter count, retiring his hardworking Werebears. As good as the black cards are, if green really wants to fight back, there is nothing black can do to keep it from abandoning its central strategy for games two and three, simply overloading on anti-black sideboard cards. 

Which of these is a good thing? 

*   U-g diversity is largely stamped out because of a black bear.
*   A new black archetype is developed or resurrected.
*   U-g strikes back, compromising (other) sideboarded matchups to strengthen the matchup against black.
*   U-g decks abandon their diversity to win sideboarded games, ironically to maintain their game one diversity.
*   The weenie forces of darkness drag down the old guard of control as u-g decks become stronger against them, too.
*   Non-black decks become stronger against u-g over three game matches as a result.

Which, if any, will actually occur?

Besides all of this, I don't know that a monoblack weenie deck would be very good at all against the dominant board control strategy of today's Standard, the deck Zvi Mowshowitz chose for the Master's Gateway: Astral Slide. Black weenies, for the most part, can't withstand a single blow from a Lightning Rift and can't Duress a Secluded Steppe. On the other hand, if they are built fast enough, with the right balance of hand destruction and creature kill, these decks might just rip Psychatog to shreds.

I have been criticized for predicting too much environmental shift when seeing new, potentially exciting cards. Nevertheless, Withered Wretch reminds me very much of the Mirage Tutors before my first Pro Tour, PT-Dallas, way back in 1996. Before that PT, long before Foreshadow or Predict existed, my good friend Brian Schneider emailed me, saying he had never before have considered playing Ray of Erasure maindeck, but that it might be quite effective as a foil to the Tutors; many players agreed that this might very well be a legitimate templating choice. At the same time, at the end of that PT, it was mostly established, Tutor-free decks that made up the Top 8.

By Regionals this spring and the Extended Pro Tour next fall, we will see what kind of effect Withered Wretch has on Constructed. Though it seems to wipe out two-thirds of an entire block's unique mechanics, it is also pretty vanilla against decks that are not graveyard reliant or don't scoop to vanilla bears. At the very least, I think that this zombie cleric will have a ripple effect in the sea of Standard, changing how decks other than itself interact with one another. Will you choose Slide, the deck that the Masters ignored, watching the u-g decks around you fall or over-prepare for black? Will you under-prepare u-g, hoping to not get paired against black more than once? Will you choose black, trying to harness the new cards, hoping to catch opponents with overpowered (but six-month old) decks off-guard, or just play spoiler? It certainly will be interesting. 

I remain convinced that Withered Wretch will have an effect, possibly a dramatic one. The question, then, becomes... at the end of the day, will Withered Wretch be on top?
