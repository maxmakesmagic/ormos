### 

The *Onslaught* sacrifice lands may not be the best dual lands *ever* - that title probably still belongs to Alpha's original ten - but this cycle may be the first to rival the effectiveness of the ubiquitous "pain" lands in Constructed deck design. 

### Dual Lands throughout the Blocks

![](https://i.ormos.uk/ba/68/ba6893e0cb17d24f65f4db39eb5721361ae0ee51eb5729b3a098088d2eaaa771.jpg)The pain lands, first introduced in *Ice Age* but then appreciatively remodeled for opposing colors in *Apocalypse*, are the measuring stick against which dual lands have been measured since the original rotations of old favorites like Tropical Island and Underground Sea. While players may have initially been apprehensive to pay a life to tap for colored mana (at least when they first appeared) the Ice Age models have nevertheless held a strategic leg up on every sort of dual land printed since then… at least until *Onslaught's* cycle. Coming into play untapped, the pain lands were able to produce colored mana immediately, every time.

This single, incredibly important, design difference has made the pain lands the first choice dual land for almost every appropriate archetype in almost every format in which they were legal (at least, that is, in formats where the original dual lands were not available). Remember the Fires of Yavimaya decks that dominated Standard a year and a half ago? While the ostensibly superior Invasion come-into-play-tapped lands were available, players chose to run pain lands instead. Why? Shivan Oasis can't tap for a turn-one Birds of Paradise.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/cf/23/cf23f1c4bfbcd217cf5d76fea28bbc8b2d68df2e7e1f8148706e0696e06ce9e6.jpg)This is not to say that other dual lands have not had their place. Coastal Tower and Salt Marsh especially represented the *Invasion* Block variety in competitive deck after competitive deck. In general, though players would rather tap these lands for colored mana than their Ice Age equivalents, the speed issue prompted two-color decks almost always played a full compliment of pain lands before going to *Invasion's* slower-but-better models.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/5a/c3/5ac31ecf199f6aed0c9869e6419556071d618a96b7ca05f5e5523eb479897d40.jpg)Like the pain lands, *Odyssey* Block's "filter" lands also hit the board untapped. Though player adoption of these lands has been slow, they have nevertheless worked their way into a tertiary role in stabilizing some mana bases. Like the *Ice Age* lands, the filter lands are, for the most part, effective the turn they come into play; like the *Invasion* Block lands, and probably leading to their relatively limited appearances in Constructed decks, these lands lack any potency on turn one, and sometimes make for awkward taps in the midgame.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/32/38/3238ddd5d0d0039586b1401fad67c6fabf046f9948a09559a7ffb95b00adbc53.jpg)The other major cycle of lands, and perhaps the most important for the discussion of the *Onslaught* models, is the *Mirage* come-into-play-tapped sacrifice lands (Bad River, Flood Plain, Grasslands, Mountain Valley, and Rocky Tar Pit). These lands had an extremely low early adoption by players... but for no good reason. In 1996, when *Mirage* debuted, first-turn plays and the mana curve were a lot less important to two-color deck design than they have become over the last half-decade. Instead, even Necropotence decks - decks in which both consistent mana development and player life totals were important resources - played with the inferior Ice Age "sleepy lands" (Lava Tubes, et al) and Cities of Brass! It was only after his Top 4 performance at Pro Tour-Dallas that Chris Pikula decided that his b-r Necropotence deck could have been improved had it played with Rocky Tar Pit.

Over the years, especially in Extended, the *Mirage* sacrifice lands have become staples of deck design, primarily because of their ability to search up the original dual lands. Bob Maher's Ped Bun Oath deck used this style of land to make his three- and four-color mana bases look smooth and easy, as well as to make "random cantrip" Brainstorm look like a star.

### The Children of Two Strong Parents

The obvious way to look at *Onslaught's* dual lands is as an update to the *Mirage* lands... but with the flavor of *Ice Age* rather than *Invasion*. The *Mirage* lands had the same disability as Elfhame Palace and Urborg Volcano. While they were good, they still came into play tapped. While they had specialty roles in decks like Bob Maher's, they did not tend to show up en masse in quicker Extended decks, whose first turn plays were more central. Instead, the *Onslaught* lands - Polluted Delta, Wooded Foothills, Bloodstained Mire, Flooded Strand, and Windswept Heath - ask for a one-time investment of one life. In return, they give players whatever color they need in the short term, and can produce that color of mana the turn they come into play.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/85/ad/85adaee95fcff59153504acc852ddd4bddba25193b648c1487ac593b6e81eee4.jpg)

If we look at how much more pervasive Brushland and Karplusan Forest have been than Elfhame Palace and Shivan Oasis, it is easy to predict that the *Onslaught* lands will see play even in aggressive decks.

### Designing Brainstorms

![](https://i.ormos.uk/65/ec/65ec8fe2397e538a97cf353e4bf161be10dcc134a2146c44642bf86d40e18798.jpg)Brainstorm, most recently seen in Standard by way of the *Masques* Block, has historically been held back by one thing - the "reshuffle" condition. While Brainstorm has been extremely effective in control decks since Erik Lauer's Thawing Glaciers Counter-Post in 1997, without the ability to shuffle one's library after casting it, Brainstorm sometimes proves deceptively ineffective. In Extended, Brainstorm has fueled both Draw-Go and Oath of Druids decks. It has allowed players to shuffle away their dead-draw Silver Bullets and aped Ancestral Recall when mingled with the Rebel chain.

As should at this point be obvious, in Extended, the *Onslaught* lands fill a void for this staple card left by the recent departures of Thawing Glaciers and Flood Plain.

### Everybody Loves Basics

![](https://i.ormos.uk/6c/9a/6c9a2f3c844e659c290dbe53e17b7ccc90bd7423fc260a40fb26fbdc3936f4f4.jpg)Though nobody likes to adjust his life total when tapping for mana, the *Onslaught* models have a couple of decent upsides in this regard. Unlike the *Ice Age* dual lands, after their initial payments, the *Onslaught* lands never ask for another life point. The mana they produce comes from good old basic lands. If they are not hosed before they are used, the *Onslaught* sacrifice lands aren't going to get hosed. They are next to invulnerable to the powers of Back to Basics, Dwarven Miner, or Wasteland.

If these lands have any drawback, it is the drawback of all basic lands in the long term. While they offer a ton of short-term versatility, and while they ask for very little after that first life point, unlike most of the dual lands before them, the *Onslaught* lands can tap for only one color of mana as the game goes progresses. Nevertheless, I think that this drawback is a small one when compared with the ability to pay for either a first-turn Birds of Paradise or a first-turn Firebolt.

The *Onslaught* sacrifice lands represent a general upgrade to their very playable *Mirage* predecessors, and should see as much Constructed play as the original pain lands. This cycle is a great payoff for players, and shows off the kind of ideas and rededication to multicolored play that Randy Buehler recently talked about in [Tending the Land](/en/articles/latestdevelopments/2002/08-02-tending-the-land) over at [MagicTheGathering.com](https://web.archive.org/web/20041204111919/http://www.wizards.com/default.asp?x=mtgcom). These lands are good... I'd love to see mana of this caliber show up year after year.
