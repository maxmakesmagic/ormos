Another Pro Tour has come and gone with its share of events, news and gossip of interest, so this week's Wise Words looks at a few of those tidbits. 

### The King of Europe

![](https://i.ormos.uk/b2/73/b273ec50ddfc99df20409c10121ba3c48a29f07f8531f7e5c6ebff5fd526d267.jpg)One of the great benefits of writing for the *Sideboard* is that I get a better vantage point on a number of important matches than anyone. I've sat next to Kai Budde as he's won more than one Pro Tour, and then when his Sunday streak ended against Bram Snepvangers in Nice. I've sat next to destiny's Pro Tour winners, National Champions and Continental Champions, and yes, I even sat next to Ryan Fuller when ABU won the Tokyo Masters. It been a lot of fun for me to sit on stage, gauging the reactions of eventual winners and losers, but I can't remember having a better time up there than I did while watching Eivind Nitter.

Years ago, at PT-LA 4, I sat down for my final round match against a young, wild looking kid from Norway and proceeded to beat the snot of him, not because I played brilliantly, but because he was already in the defeatist mindset thanks to a very emotional approach to the game and an 0-2 start to the draft. He seemed nice enough, if not a little scatterbrained, but the way his body was sprawled on the table, his cards slammed in disgust, I figured him for one or two Pro Tours, three at the most.

Around a year later, I was having a conversation about European **Magic** with Sigurd Eskeland when the Big Viking informed me in no uncertain terms that there was a kid from Norway who would be the next big thing on the Pro Tour, and upon hearing the name, I vaguely remembered the guy from that match. I told Sigurd I doubted the validity of his claim, but he insisted. This was my introduction to Eivind Nitter.

Since that time, I've gotten to know the 23 year old a little better. Eivind definitely wears his heart on his sleeve, but in doing so seems so transparent that his bluffs can be next to impossible to read. When he's winning, he celebrates with the exuberance of a child in a way that can grate on the hardest of nerves, when he isn't, he shows his displeasure in his body language, his facial anguish and his cries of displeasure. In short, Nitter so bluntly exhibits every thought that you can't trust any of them at the table, because it could come back to bite you in an instant.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/5e/dc/5edc6da4e9f7f8656c436592049d0a7a97db5c212e1d1d59efebab2e24cea61c.jpg)My favorite publishable story about Eivind came from this year's European Championships, where he faced Wolfgang Eder at table one in the ninth round. Drawing first, Eivind kept a six-card hand full of four-casting-cost spells with no land, proceeded to cast two Bog Downs and a Flametongue Kavu in the first six turns, reduced Eder to no cards in hand and no non-land permanents in play, and then, two turns later, when Eder cast Confound to protect a just-cast critter, Eivind reared back his head and roared "You're so lucky!", forcing a laugher-enforced stoppage to play for a minute or two. 

It's this emotion-on-top approach and natural comedic timing that makes Eivind one of the best players to watch in the game. He cracks jokes, chats it up and entertains in countless ways, always maintaining a loose attitude to the game. In the final, he started staring at something on the ceiling, and when I finally joined him in trying to figure out what was going on, he started laughing, simply because there was nothing there.

And that's the essence of Eivind. He has fun in everything he does, in his snake skin boots and belt, seemingly acting on his 'does it feel good?' instinct at all times and making it work for him. With his win in Nice, he becomes the first player in history to be reigning champion of both Europe and the European Pro Tour, and winning two tournaments of that stature in one year is a pretty remarkable accomplishment, even if the aforementioned Mr. Budde has dulled its importance some. Don't let his laid-back demeanor and youthful exuberance fool you: this win is not a case of lightning striking twice. Instead, it is the validation of his earlier successes. 

### Teams Update

![](https://i.ormos.uk/d6/4c/d64c9b841fdd6c0ef6f2fbca7949c9ef24f862e23267f9e685019a45dc084d56.jpg)It's around this time each year that the loose ends of the Pro Tour scramble to tie themselves up for Team Pro Tour purposes. While established teams like YMG (Rob Dougherty, Dave Humphreys, Darwin Kastle) and Phoenix Foundation (Kai Budde, Dirk Baberowski, Marco Blume) have had a high degree of success, teams of 'mercenaries', those players who couldn't find suitable team ups in their Pro Tour affiliations, have had their moments also, as evidenced by the Swiss success of Illuminati (Zvi Mowshowitz, Alex Shvartsman, Justin Gary) at this season's Pro Tour-New York, and as a result, there can be a lot at stake for pro players, especially those fighting for Masters berths, when it comes to locking themselves into team ups for a tournament that's still over four months away.

The biggest news to come out of Nice as far as Masters was concerned was the DCI's announcement confirming that there would no longer be rating-based invites for the Masters. I was part of the last crop of rating-invited players, so I think I can certify with some authority that the rating invite system was an unfair one, with the extra pro point invites being handed out for singles events being much more equitable. 

With a pro point-based invite system impossible for the teams Masters, the new system will work as follows: the top two teams from the previous team Masters (Phoenix Foundation and Panzer Hunters) and top two teams from the previous Teams PT will receive invites, as will the top two teams from the Gateway tournament and the winners of this year's two team Grand Prixs: Nagoya and New Jersey.

Much has been made of the lack of a European 'qualifier', but in reality, it's hard to blame anyone for this void. Past Grand Prix numbers have suggested that individual events are far more popular in Europe than teams, and as a result, WotC Europe decided to take their one Boston qualifying GP and move it into the Nice qualifying season before the Masters decision had been made across the ocean. These decisions must come months in advance thanks to the investments GP planning require, and that meant the European plan was locked into place by the time the American branch was ready to look at the Team Masters situation.

So, with the Masters developments coming down as they did, a chain reaction was set off that saw those teams trying to qualify for Masters on rating like YMG, suddenly left without that responsibility. That means that those teams will likely remain intact, in turn meaning that those players who thought they might fit into those teams' alternative plans are now left to find themselves new team ups.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/Worlds2001/831.jpg)Two developments of a more personal nature came to the front in Nice to head the Boston news front. The first of these was Mike Turian's decision to end his Potato Nation affiliation. Turian is considered by many to have a better understanding of the team format than anyone else on the planet, and with Scott Johns taking a step back from the game to take care of his infant son Blake while I chose to live an ocean away, it seemed to Mike that it would only make sense to get things a little bit closer to home.

Turian will now be working with Eugene Harvey and Osyp Lebedowicz (pronounced Lebedovich). Harvey, a fellow member of team CMU, moved in with Turian last year and the two have played **Magic** together more and more as of late, especially with Andy Johnson's retiring from Pro Tour life and Aaron Forsythe's move to Washington. Eugene, a New Jersey resident, wanted to play with Osyp, who made his first Top 8 at Osaka, in part because they'll be able to practice together over the summer.

The other development, pending U.S. Nationals and Worlds, is the apparent end of Antarctica. With both Jon Finkel and Steve O'Mahoney-Schwartz failing to make Top 128 in Nice, both players fell off the gravy train, meaning that barring a last-second flourish at U.S. Nationals, neither will be qualified for Worlds, leaving their long-standing team up with Steve's brother Dan in trouble if only for the lack of pro points needed to qualify. What the two titans plan to do for Boston is unknown at this time. Here's a look at a few other team situations that have emerged over the last couple of months:

![](https://i.ormos.uk/19/78/1978a8ad86e938271b567f41b67f464bb8e8024896894d8fc1c9af7e1a1658eb.jpg)Rubin/Kibler/Ho - Fresh off his Osaka victory, Ken Ho, who Rubin insists is the best Limited player of this bunch, has decided to team up with one of the world's best deck building tandems.

BJG - Gab Tsang and Brian Hacker have been teaming up for everything from Pro Tours to two-on-two side drafts for years, so it should come as no surprise that they'll be doing so again this year. This time around, they've decided to team up with Jeff Cunningham, the Top 8 finisher from this season's PT-San Diego, who ironically enough has just moved away from Tsang's Toronto, where he'd been studying film for a year.

The Ruels - Questions had been asked as of late as to whether Antoine and Olivier would be teaming up with old Black Ops partner Florent Jeudon, but they put those questions to rest, assuring me that the team up would be maintained. Jeudon had moved away from the game, but recent rededication has paid off, most notably with his second-place finish at Grand Prix-Antwerp.
