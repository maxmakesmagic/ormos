For me, the *Odyssey* Block format is over. I played and won my fourteenth round match at Grand Prix-London, finishing ninth after throwing away rounds twelve and thirteen, and while I was upset at myself for making the errors I did, ninth didn't upset me. Instead, it made me think about this format, one I panned before Day 3 of Worlds, and how in its late stages, it might be getting... dare I say it... good.

I can't remember a Constructed format where little mistakes have been punished so thoroughly. In game one of my eighteenth round match vs. Jin Okamoto at Worlds, with plenty of mana on my side of the table and no cards in either of our hands, I drew a swamp and decided to hold it instead of playing it like one should in a match up where your opponent's actions aside from the occasional Skeletal Scrying really don't matter. Jin, his Edicts removed from the game by Haunting Echoes, drew a card and passed the turn, then I drew Skeletal Scrying for six, played a land and said "go". Jin drew, played a swamp and urged me on, and I played a swamp and my last two Shades with seventeen mana left. Jin drew, cast Mutilate for nine and, after I'd saved one Shade, cast Mutilate number two, leaving me no kill methods and a loss. If I'd played that land, I'd have won. I won a game last weekend when my opponent made the same mistake.

See, there's a lot of power in OBC, and the fact you have to be able to compete with turn two Mongrel or turn four Roar has to be frustrating deck builders around the globe, but there are four colors in this format that are producing quality decks in all manner of combinations now, and while red's being undermanned wastes the sheer power of Violent Eruption and Fiery Temper, the rest of the good stuff in *Odyssey* Block is now finally being allowed to interact in a way that wasn't approached in Osaka and Sydney.

Before you keep reading, do go and take a look at the coverage of [GP-London](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/08-31-2002-grand-prix-london-coverage) and [GP-Cleveland](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/09-06-live-coverage-of-2002-grand-prix-cleveland), if only to see the diversity of those fields. At Worlds, decks other than blue-green variants and monoblack were being played, but even the number three deck in the field, Punisher's blue-white deck, was only being played by ten players, and only one other blue-white deck made an appearance at the tournament. That's far too little diversity for an environment with any hope of being recognized as healthy.

London was a different story. With both Tomi Walamies and Arvi Limpadanai going 6-0 at Worlds with non-blue-green/monoblack archetypes, there were suddenly all kinds of possibilities, with the common belief being that while the big two would still be prevalent, Punisher white weenie, white-green madness and even blue-black Upheaval decks geared to beat black to smithereens would all make their presences known. Even that better-informed outlook didn't prepare us for the reality though.

With the completion of London, the number of viable archetypes may have climbed to double digits, creating as open a metagame as we've seen all year, and when combined with the need to do even the little things right, a parity has been created that is seeing the same players succeed time and again because they know their decks better than their opponents. Don't get me wrong, there's definitely a degree of luck involved, especially in the black mirror and the blue-green race to Wonder, but as a whole, this environment has surpassed its present Standard counterpart in both skill and diversity. It's just a pity that it has come this late in the game.

Me, I'd still be running monoblack control, if only because that deck's been good to me. Okay, that's not the only reason. The other decks rely on solid mana draws while black doesn't have to. Once you have your mana, it's still less draw-dependant than any other deck in the environment with bunches of Tutors and Pact making one draw as good as another, and thanks to those same Tutors, the deck really does have answers to anything available to it. My London configuration left the maindeck vulnerable to Solitary Confinement, a suitable gamble, but a Death Wish, Mirari (used with Rancid Earths to take out the green mana sources needed to return Genesis to the hand) or the one maindeck Coffin Purge I abhor can all be used to break that deck's back. In other words, the cards are all available to make the deck do what you want it to. All you have to do is figure out what specifically you want it to do. Well, that and know how to play the deck better than most: going into any tournament in this format without knowing your deck is suicide.

### The Votes are In

Around this time of year, the debates always rage over who should be going to the Invitational and on what criteria their invitations should be warranted. Before the votes were tallied, the popular consensus was that Zvi, Finkel and I would likely be going to Seattle, and thanks to Brian Kibler's ascension to the DCI slot, a fourth opening could be filled by any number of players.

This will be my fourth trip to the Invitational, each one coming courtesy of the vote, and I've fielded my share of pointed fingers and dirty looks by players who felt they were more deserving of the slots. For a while I even agreed with them. Now, I'm not so sure.

See, the Invitational is supposed to be for the community, not just the participants. If those four voted slots were intended for the next highest ranking players in the Player of the Year race, then there really wouldn't be much point in there being a vote, and the results over the years seem to have set the criteria. Sure, being a good **Magic** player is a boon to anyone's chances, but making a contribution and leaving a mark on the public consciousness really seems to be the decider.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/MASTERSBAR01/695.jpg)

Jon Finkel has had an atrocious PT year. To have fifteen pro points over a full season during which no tournaments were missed means that at best Jon could have had only one finish in the 33-48 range with no GP rewards. Once considered the greatest to ever play the game, Jon now ranks second on that level, but I'm sure even he would tell you that right now, he isn't one of the top sixteen players in the game. Fortunately, as far as the Invitational vote goes, that doesn't matter. 

Jon's contributions to the game are finally being rewarded by its followers after years of being unable to pay him back for his contributions. Each year, Jon would qualify for the Invitational be it by World Champion, Player of the Year, returning Invitational Champion or pro point status. In the meantime, his name has rested on the lips and hearts of all gamers with dreams, emblematic of what one can do as a **Magic** player. His name was placed next to Richard Garfield's on WotC products, his face beamed to the world as a Pro Tour icon, and now he's being remembered for it.

Zvi is just Zvi, with his usual forty pro points and articles for thirty web sites, and I'm not going to dwell on my selection, but Dave Price's induction is an interesting one. Dave told me in Sydney he didn't expect to be voted in this time around. Dave won a Pro Tour four years ago, in doing so teaching the world to beat down with little red guys and then point accompanying spells to the head, and at the same time he was making major communal contributions, writing about his experiences as the man climbing the infinite hill that has been his quest for Pro Tour stability. By his own admission, his **Magic** writing has slowed to a standstill and he still has to qualify the hard way on occasion, but its the way he does that which has kept him close to the public heart, attacking with creatures in a way that the youngest gamer feels they can emulate, and fighting the constant struggle that long-time gravy trainers no doubt have forgotten. He's the true people's champion.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/MI00/982.jpg)

With all that said though, Dave didn't get in for any of those reasons. Candidly, Dave got into the Invitational because no one has stepped up to take his place. There's no doubt in my mind that if he were eligible for the vote, Brian Kibler would have toppled the PTQ king, but other than Kibs, there hasn't been a major pro who has emerged as a voice in the community for some time. Chris Pikula designed a card that's no longer legal in Standard as of November, Tomi Walamies built a deck for a soon to be obsolete Extended environment that became synonymous with his name and Mike Long is, well, Mike Long, but these guys are still finishing high on the ballot virtually by name recognition alone. In other words, if the Invitational is really a big deal to some of the pros out there, start writing, winning, designing or just doing something memorable. Get deep enough into the voter's memory and you could be getting the free trips for a long time.

Each year, as the new ballot comes out, I plan to write an article on who I would vote for only to have the window of opportunity for such an article slip by while mired in post-Worlds work, and this year was no exception. I was going to suggest that Mike Turian and Rob Dougherty were the two guys who likely wouldn't get invited who really should have been. Both are good players, sure, they're two of the best, but more importantly, Mike has been writing for years and is now the site editor over at [www.grimmoire.com](https://web.archive.org/web/20020911000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.grimmoire.com&origin=sideboard_ww_20020911), while Rob has worn every hat that tournament **Magic** can offer, from that of competitor to writer to T.O. Guys like these, like Price and Zvi and Kibler, Alex Shvartsman, Geordie Tait, Rune Horvik, and hosts of others do more than their share by dedicating that time not used playing to help further the game in other ways. If that's not deserving of votes, I don't know what is.

I guess the point is this: as with any game where the spectator is given a say in who gets to play, this isn't a contest of skill but instead one of popularity, popularity based on name recognition. More often than not, that name recognition comes from the prolonged exposure that accompanies contribution to **Magic's** internet community, and that means anyone with an internet connection and the ability to get to the Pro Tour more than once or twice can make the choices that will eventually put them into the position of familiarity with the public that can get them into the Invitational. 

The guys who get voted in, as has always been the case, did so because they can play the game, sure, because they write articles, okay, but most importantly, Dave Price, Jon Finkel, Zvi Mowshowitz and I are more than just names on a page, as most of the Pro Tour competitors are, to the people who care enough to vote. Maybe the voters sense we're trying to do more than just play a game: we're trying to build one as well. For that, I feel vindicated in saying this: I need a vacation.

That's about it for this week. Before I save and send this off to my editor, I want to get in a quick thanks for everyone who deemed me worthy of their votes for the Invitational. Getting voted in remains one of my proudest achievements in this game, and I must admit, it makes the effort all seem very worthwhile. See you in Renton. Have a good one.
