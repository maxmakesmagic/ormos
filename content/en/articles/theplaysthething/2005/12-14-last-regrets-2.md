There's nothing like dying to make you wonder if you made a mistake. Looking over the examples that have been submitted lately, most of them have one thing in common: The player submitting them ended up losing a game that he suspects he need not have lost. It is great that they're looking at what happened and wondering what they could have done differently, but there are two things worth noting before I begin tackling the examples. First, it's not just the last decision that is worth looking at but those before it. These players have managed to get themselves into a pickle, and I'm curious if they had a way to avoid being put to the decisions they now have to make where they generally are not going to have any good (or at least, not going to have any safe) answers. Your fate is not always sealed by your last decision but by the decisions that led to that decision. 

The other thing worth noting is that you shouldn't let the outcome of the game determine whether you look back and examine it. Those games where you end up taking these risks but winning anyway are worth looking at as well, but I don't often hear people say 'My play would have lost to a Shock, did I do something wrong?' unless their opponent did in fact have the Shock. 

Now, on to the examples:

* * *

You're down to four life and your opponent is actually ahead of you on cards. At this point in the game decks as controlling as this one (Buehler's deck had the most counterspells and least amount of offense of any competitive deck I can think of) expects to at least have generated some card advantage. Right now your main advantage is your pair of <a>Stalking Stones</a> where your opponent has extra basic lands. </p>&#10;<p>The key piece of information in this example is that your opponent is playing a red burn deck. If your opponent was playing a deck full of red creatures, you could consider plays that brought you down to one life point. Against a burn deck that is suicide. You're turning every burn spell in your opponent's deck into an automatic win, and without even the ability to pitch a card to <a>Force of Will</a> (since that would kill you) you have very little chance to win even if you manage to stabilize. If he can stop you from blocking or can burn you out from four life then you had even less hope. </p>&#10;<p>It might cause you to wince but you have only one play. You must activate the <a>Stalking Stones</a> and attempt to block <a>Bloodrock Cyclops</a>. If he burns the <a>Stalking Stones</a> then you have lost nothing. Most scenarios where he burns you out were also unwinnable any other way for the reasons described above. You need to take a chance. </p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

That's exactly what the player decided to do, but he started doubting himself after his opponent played an unexpected card.

* * *

> Well, I tapped all of my Islands and a Stalking Stones to make my other Stalking Stones a 3/3 artifact creature so that I could trade with the Bloodrock Cyclops. It turns out that my opponent had a Shatter conveniently located in his hand. So, I lost.
> 
> Now, my question is, is it smarter to save your Force of Will in this situation to hard cast it if your opponent plays a threat the next turn? But, this will also let the Cyclops swing for three, putting you at one life. So, let's say I save the Force of Will and my opponent tries to play a Goblin Lackey on his next turn. Do I counter the Lackey or save my Force of Will for a possible spell like Lava Axe or Beacon of Destruction? Another possibility is to play my Disk, but is that too slow in this situation?

* * *

That's the thinking of a man who just lost, but suppose you did agree to go down to one life and your opponent tried to cast Goblin Lackey. Would you cast Force of Will?

<table><tbody><tr><td><p>If you don't counter <a>Goblin Lackey</a> then you're going to have to activate <a>Stalking Stones</a>, untap, activate another <a>Stalking Stones</a> and double block. That means you either have to draw another land and prey your opponent has nothing at all – no burn, no other creature this turn, no <a>Shatter</a>, no nothing – or draw a card that gets you out of it. This deck's core design doesn't have anything that will get you out of it, so your best chance of survival is to counter the Lackey. That is a strong argument for not holding back <a>Force of Will</a> at all. </p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>
