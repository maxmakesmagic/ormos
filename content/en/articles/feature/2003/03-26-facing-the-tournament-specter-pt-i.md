### A Constructed Event Primer

* * *

With the upcoming free tournament on March 22, I thought I'd write up some tips on getting prepared to play. I'm not a former Pro Tour player, and I'm not the best deckbuilder in the world, but I have been in the seat of trying to prep for tournament action. Since I was a relative **Magic** newbie at the time (just off a five-year hiatus), I've got a decent perspective of what new players will face if they decide to dip their toes in at this event.

This has nothing to do with someone playing <a>Shahrazad</a>. What they're talking about is what decks (and versions of decks) you're likely to see in a given tournament.<p></p>&#10;<p>While you can walk into a tournament with a well-built deck and take home the top spot, knowing the decks you're likely to face can make the difference between winning and losing. ### Netdecking Around The World

Saying "netdeck" is much like kicking a hornet nest. This is one of the most polarizing topics in the **Magic** scene, and players get vocal on both sides of the "do or don't play them" issue. I'm not going to put my two cents in on either side, except to say that they are useful and you shouldn't ignore them.

If you haven't run across the term "netdeck" before, it refers to decklists that you can find on most **Magic**-related websites. Originally, posting decklists was a way for players to know what decks were doing well at tournaments and so they could test against the best decks out there. Soon, however, players began building those decks for tournament play without even modifying them (called "netdecking"). Nowadays, part of the **Magic** community believes there's nothing wrong with this (play proven decks) while part is against the idea (build your own decks). The argument is more complicated than that, but I'm just going to say you should pay close attention to netdecks.

Specifically, pay attention to decklists you find in articles about tournaments: what decks were prevalent and which of those won? Since everyone on ***Magic** Online* by default has access to the Internet, you can bet many of your opponents will see these decklists, too. And a good percentage of those players are likely to use those decks. These decklists are on the 'net for a reason--they have a *proven* record.

If you have the cards, play copies of those decks. You'll learn a lot about deck construction, how those decks work, and what they can and can't do. Playing the decks show you why they win and what makes one deck better than another. Even if you don't take one to a tournament, you'll be forewarned when you run up against one.

You also want to play *against* copies of netdecks. Just because your red goblin-Sligh deck pounds players in the **Casual Play** arena doesn't mean it has a chance against a well-built R-G beats deck. If you don't play against the best decks, you won't learn whether your deck can beat them or what you can sideboard to level the playing field.

### Plays Well With Others

One constant in top Pro Tour competition is that the players don't work in a vacuum. Most of them are on teams or have loose collaborations that allow them to play, plan, build, and get better. When it comes to seeing all the angles or coming up with a new idea, a group is better than an individual.

If you're a member of a ***Magic** Online* clan, chat with and play against other members. You can also work with players on your **Buddy list** that you play against on a regular basis. If you come up against a friendly player that is obviously better than you, ask for advice. While she or he may not give away all secrets, you'll probably learn something that'll make you a better and more competitive **Magic** player.

### Conclusion

Keep in mind that there's more to being a good **Magic** player than having the right cards. A Tier I deck with all the goodies can still lose to a mediocre deck if it's not piloted well. Know your deck inside and out, and try to know your competitors' decks just as intimately.

Overall, consider the March 22 event a chance to practice for the future. It may be just your thing, or you may not like it at all. In the end, what's the harm in finding out?
