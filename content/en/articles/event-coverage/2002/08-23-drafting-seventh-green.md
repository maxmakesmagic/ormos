Please read the first three installments in this series before proceeding with this (final) article of the set.

If I had to rate the colors in *7th Edition*, green would be second only to blue. It's got the best combat creatures, works well with literally every other color in the set (though green-white is often a bit of a stretch). Here is an in-depth look at this color's common cards.

**Canopy Spider**  
 This is a poor man's Giant Spider in every sense of the word. If you can get your hand on a pair of Giant Spiders, you will not be wanting this card in your deck. If you can't, you will almost certainly want to play this one instead. With superior creatures, evasion is green's Achilles' heel and often the only viable way for other decks to defeat you. Green has a number of weapons to deal with the problem - Hurricane and Squall among them. Giant Spider is the best possible answer as it is capable of blocking every single common flying creature in the format and then surviving to tell the tale. Canopy Spider is not nearly as good - it can block 2/2's, which comprise the majority of the 7E flying contingent, but it cannot take them out - which means a pair of flyers will still be able to do damage. In short, you may not want this card in your deck, but you may need it if a better alternative is not available.

**Fog**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 I generally do not play this card, though one cannot call it useless. In a trick-light deck, Fog is a mediocre but viable way to counter your opponent's combat tricks. There may be some compelling reasons to add Fog to your deck occasionally, but buying a turn of respite should never be enough of a reason in itself. Just like a lifegain spell (see Stream of Life explanation), this card will usually do nothing for your long-term strategy and only forestall the inevitable.

**Giant Growth**  
 I really cannot make up my mind as to whether Giant Growth or Gorilla Chieftain is the best green common in the set (Giant Spider comes in a close third). Green has very few trick cards worth playing, but you can literally never have enough Giant Growths in your draft deck. If I could somehow get eight of them, they would still all make the cut. For just one mana (which means casting it will not usually slow down your development that turn) you get to win almost any creature combat that is likely to take place in the game. You can take down Sea Monsters and Redwood Treefolks that are opposing you. It even makes your Bears very good instead of very mediocre. If you set out to draft a green deck in this format, be sure to get your hands on several of these if you can.

**Giant Spider**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 Yet another card you really want multiples of in your deck. Randy Buehler feels this to be the best green common (followed by Giant Growth and Chieftain, in that order) One of a few ways your opponent has of beating the green deck is to either stall the ground or tempo you out, both involving attacks with evasion creatures. An untapped Spider thwarts that plan as often as not, buying you time to play even larger monsters and overwhelm your opponent's army. There is no common I hate to see more when playing a blue-white deck.

**Gorilla Chieftain**  
 A 3/3 creature you cannot easily kill, it is definitely among the best Limited creature cards in the set. Few common creatures have a toughness higher than three (especially if you look outside of green) and so Chieftain is often unstoppable. It also fits the mana-curve extremely well, sharing the four mana slot only with Giant Spider.

**Grizzly Bears**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 Over the course of these several articles on *Seventh Edition* draft, you have already figured out that I hold Bears in very low esteem when it comes drafting this format. Ironically, the original Grizzly Bears is worse than its black and red counterparts. The reason is the overall creature quality available in your colors. If you are drafting green, you have access to far more quality creatures than if you are drafting red, decreasing the chances of those Bears making the cut. Having said that, you still have to watch the mana-curve and play some two-drops. So if you fail to get some Seekers of Skybreak or quality two-drops in your second color, a pair of Bears may often make the cut.

**Llanowar Elves**  
 Another important strong point of green in this set is its numerous mana acceleration cards. Top among those are the Llanowar Elves. Not only you are paying only one mana to accelerate, but you get a random 1/1 creature in the process - a creature you can use later in the game to trade in combat for something reasonable, or just buy you time chump-blocking a large monster. Most importantly though, it will help you bring out Spined Wurms and Redwood Treefolk before your opponent knows what to do with them.

**Lone Wolf**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 While its uncommon and rare counterparts (Pride of Lions and Thornwood Elemental) are very valuable in draft, Lone Wolf's ability just isn't attractive on a 2/2 creature. It's like having a 2/2 creature with trample, but worse. At least you can Giant Growth a trampler to kill the blocking creature and still spill some damage over. In Lone Wolf's case, the best you can do is bolt your opponent for five when you combine it with Giant Growth. I practically never play it in draft, unless things have gone horribly wrong and I really have to reach for that twenty third card.

**Monstrous Growth**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 Just think of how good this card would be if it were an instant! Unfortunately, it is not, and it sucks. So you cast it on one of your creatures. At this point your opponent has two options - either to absorb about 5-8 points of damage and call it a day, with you losing card economy - or block and trade his weakest creature for your Monstrous Growth. With the choice being up to him, Growth rarely manages to impress. My dear wish in 7E draft is to face Lone Wolves and Monstrous Growth every first round.

**Rampant Growth**  
 A solid card that is both capable of accelerating your mana, and making sure you aren't color-screwed. It is especially valuable if you are splashing a third color for a removal spell or two.

**Reclaim**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 Somewhat playable, but surely isn't exciting. You have to have numerous cards in your deck that are definitely worth giving up two-for-one card economy on to get back from the graveyard - which is what you are doing when casting Reclaim. In most cases it simply will not be good enough or necessary enough to play.

**Redwood Treefolk** This is one format where bigger truly is better - and green has a near monopoly on huge common creatures. Although Redwood Treefolk is not a great first pick, it is the kind of card you will want to draft very early - within the first four-five picks to get it at all. Only slightly weaker than Spined Wurm, you will want one or two of each in your deck.

**Regeneration**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 As is the case with most creature enchantments, it is not worth running the risk of two-for-one card economy loss to get your hands on Regeneration's ability. As with most other cards, there will be a time when you get some amazing mileage out of it - but those times are not going to happen often enough to justify adding it to your deck.

**Seeker of Skybreak** Although it has lower toughness, its special ability sets it far above Grizzly Bears. There are many utility creatures Seeker works well with - from Llanowar Elves, to Prodigal Sorcerer, to Angelic Page. Since you want two-power two drops on your mana curve anyway, Seeker is the best candidate since it does not become a useless card several turns into the game the way Bears without good special abilities often do.

**Shanodin Dryads**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 A mediocre sideboard card against other green deck, it's about as good (bad?) as Plague Beetle. Nothing to write home about, or pick above 12th-15th - but VERY occasionally you might choose to board it in, in order to help break a likely stalemate.

**Spined Wurm** It's big, which means it's good. On its own, Spined Wurm is bulky and expensive and - chances are - will just trade in combat with Giant Cockroach. However, tricks like Shock and Giant Growth make it very, very good - making it devastating for an opponent to try and double-block your large creature.

**Squall**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 If you did not draft Giant Spiders, you may want to maindeck one of these. It is also a very good sideboard card you will bring in against any deck with a lot of flyers. 

**Stream of Life**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 Like other life gain spells, it is not worth playing. Gaining a few life points (or even as many as eight) is practically never worth spending a card on it. Suppose you are facing a couple of 2/2 creatures and draw a Stream of Life. You may gain 5-6 life points, but you did nothing to stop your opponent from attacking every turn - the way you may have by drawing a 2/2 creature of your own and trading it off in combat instead. Doing that would save you enough life points to make up for not gaining life in that situation. On the other hand, if you fail to draw a blocker or a removal spell for several turns, you will certainly lose more life to the extra attacking 2/2 creature to more than make up for the Stream of Life gains. The rule of thumb is - if a card does nothing to affect the game situation, you should be very careful before choosing to play it. And whereas a case can be made for cards like Lava Axe as finishers in fast weenie decks, life gain cards do not deserve the same consideration.

**Trained Armodon** You just cannot go wrong with a 3/3 for ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif). The double green in its mana cost may be somewhat annoying, but it's well worth the risk. Most of my green draft decks feature two or even three or four Trained Armodons as I tend to pick them very highly. While Bears do not do the job, cheap Hill Giants usually do.

**Tranquility**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 A solid sideboard card to deal with Pacifism and a handful of other good enchantments in the set, but is never worth maindecking.

**Wild Growth**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 It does the same thing as Llanowar Elves except that you do not get a 1/1 body in the bargain. On the plus side, your opponent is less likely to be able to destroy it than he would a creature. However, that does not make up for all the benefits of getting an extra creature as your mana source. Wild Growth is playable, but you would much, much rather have Llanowar Elves, Wood Elves, or even Rampant Growth.

**Wood Elves**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 Playable but really unexciting, Wood Elves will not help you get your second or third color the way Rampant Growth might, nor will they accelerate you early enough to matter. They help get out a 5 casting cost creature on turn 4 - but fall short of matching up to the other mana acceleration options in the set.
