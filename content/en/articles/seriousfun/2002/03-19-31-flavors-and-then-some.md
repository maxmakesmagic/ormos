“Wuv Muffin.” Does that name ring a bell? (Please, if it’s because of your personal life, send your emails to editor@wizards.com instead of to me; I don’t want to know.) To **Magic** players, the bell *should* be ringing. Wuv Muffin is an *Odyssey* card that will probably see play in Block Constructed formats this year.

Need more detail? It’s a 2/2 for two mana that allows you to discard a card to give it first strike. And it’s white. That’s right, Patrol Hound.

I remember playing at the Minneapolis Grand Prix just a few short months ago (and this is *not* boasting… many of you know that anyone can play in a Grand Prix, and I did so, horribly). After compiling an amazing record in the early rounds (see parenthetical in last sentence), I decided to give my competitors a break and check out the side events. Since I’m better at Limited than Constructed formats, I at least felt that it would help me preserve some dignity.

Anyway, this was a week after the *Odyssey* Prerelease, so *Odyssey* events were available. I’m playing in my second or third draft of the day, and my opponent drops down a card and announces, “Wuv Muffin.”

“Pardon?” I come back at him. All I see is a white creature that’s strictly better than a Grizzly Bears, so I can understand some affection for the card in limited; but still…

“Wuv Muffin,” he repeats. “See?”

![Wuv Muffin = Patrol Hound](https://www.wizards.com/global/images/mtgcom_daily_aa12_pic1_en.jpg)

He turns the card around on the table and points to the flavor text. I read it, for the first time.

*To the camp, it was a fierce and loyal protector. To the sentry's youngest daughter, it would always be her "Wuv Muffin.”* 

That’s pretty cute, right? And so, more detail-oriented **Magic** players have taken to calling the Patrol Hound the Wuv Muffin; and so now, if you hear it at your next PTQ or Prerelease, you know what they’re talking about.

But flavor text is about more than inside jokes.

### FLAVOR TEXT, YOUR STATE GOVERNMENT, AND YOU

As I mentioned in my opening article on this site, I work for Minnesota’s state government, which means I work for Jesse Ventura. (No matter what you think of him, wouldn’t Jesse write some great flavor text for **Magic** cards?) More specifically, I work for the Department of Economic Security, which is the department that deals with the unemployed, as well as some employed groups who may be looking for certain kinds of training or job placement. (I can feel everyone’s eyes glazing at just *that* level of detail, so I’ll stop there.)

But here’s where I’m going with this: in my two years working in state government (I’ve had other careers before this one), I’ve learned a shocking fact: *not all bureaucrats get along*. For example, there are those who work in our department who look less favorably toward those in, say, the Department of Transportation. The rap we give those guys over there is: they’re all engineers, they have no feelings, they don’t care about people -- just potholes, and so on. And we’re right.

Of course, at the same time, folks over there look at us and think we’re too wishy-washy, we have no focus, we can’t measure what we do like you can measure bridge integrity, and so on. And they’re right, too.

The point is, we’re the kind of people who would write flavor text on our **Magic** cards, if we could misappropriate enough funds to do so. *They’re* the kind of people who would *not*. You want people like us caring about and talking to people, and you want people like them making sure your bridge won’t collapse. Flavor text is not a priority for them. (“Aaarghh, honey, we’re all going to crash and…heeeey, niiice haiku etched there on the crumbling lattice-work!”)

Flavor text is an art more than a science -- it is, after all, writing. Together with the card title and (where appropriate) creature type of the card, it is the only chance Wizards has to tell the card’s story, and give it its place in the **Magic** universe.

One of the first card texts I noticed, since I began **Magic** when *Fifth Edition* was current, was Ley Druid. The card didn’t make much sense to me at first: why should a guy with a fancy hood be able to untap an entire land? When I read the bit about taking energy from the land and returning it when needed, it made a good deal more sense -- and green as a color made more sense to me, as well. This sort of text also helps set up the “druid mechanic,” which tends to be tending and untapping lands (opposed to elves, which produce the mana on their own).

The next flavor text I can remember noticing is Ihsan's Shade, which coincidentally is also the first creature with protection from white I ever saw. (Since I was playing a white weenie deck at the time, I was rather miffed. Thus the cushy spot in my memory.) Hearing about Ishan’s betrayal helps us understand why *this* creature, as opposed to some other random black creature, may be particularly problematic for white to handle.

The list can go on, but if we all think back hard enough, I’m sure we can identify two or three cards where the flavor text really helped us get into the flow of a card more closely. And it still works, even when we’re veterans…

Certain card mechanics just make more sense, even to seasoned players, when we see the flavor text. For example, the “Phyrexian illness” creatures in *Urza’s Legacy*: Phyrexian Denouncer, Phyrexian Debaser, Phyrexian Defiler, and Phyrexian Plaguelord. The four stages of illness described reflect the way the mechanic grows stronger over the four creatures. Sure, makes sense. And it makes thinking about the cards, in limited in constructed, easier. After all, all those “D” words are hard to keep straight. Isn’t it just easier to ask, *“Which stage of the disease will be most useful to me against this creature?”*

![The Phyrexians: Denouncer, Debaser, Defiler, and Plaguelord](https://www.wizards.com/global/images/mtgcom_daily_aa12_pic2_en.jpg)

So the art of flavor text has a purpose: to help newer players understand the nature of the game, and to help all players understand the storyline (and reasoning) behind new mechanics.

Or, getting back to the parallel with government, “It’s flavor text, and it’s here to help.”

### THEY PUT THE WORDS RIGHT IN OUR MOUTHS

Of course, it’s not all about understanding the game or mechanics better. Often, the flavor text just gives us a really good starting point for rubbing in a key play against an opponent.

What true blue mage hasn’t, at least once his enemy, asked upon casting Rethink, “Are you *sure* you want to do that?” Or pointed out, upon activating Empress Galina, “Down here, you belong to me!”

What hot-blooded red mage hasn’t used the word “toast” in homage to the late, great Jaya Ballard when using Incinerate or Pyrokinesis? (My favorite Jaya Ballard quote is on Inferno.)

What dark, moody black mage hasn’t cast Hatred and made some reference to flaying skin off of bones? Or remarked at least once, upon casting Bottomless Pit, “Does this come with the place?”

And hey, what insipid white mage hasn’t, upon casting Soul Warden, gone out to the local animal shelter and taken care of all the poor little souls he can find? (Hmmm. Actually, this last one might just be more my imagination of what self-righteous white mages must do, than anything I’ve actually seen or heard.)

The fact is, echoing the flavor text helps us get in the spirit of the game, and may even impact the play decisions we make. Rather than just attack some random person with a Craw Giant and be done with it, we would rather find the guy with the 1,000 squirrel tokens (or, if we don’t have a Fault Line or Sandstorm in our hand, the guy with the two pathetic Brown Ouphes instead) and shout in a throaty voice, “Ho ho ho! Midgets! You think you can stand in my way?”

### THE FOUR FLAVORS, AND HOW TO USE THEM

For those of you who are just waking up to the fine possibilities of flavor text in your decks, let me run through the four types of flavor text, and how each is best used. (These divisions are my own unofficial musings; I’m sure the good staff at Wizards use a highly technical system of storyline plot points, biochemical weapon countermeasures, and sentence diagramming to handle flavor text.)

**Flavor One: “Rocky Joke Road.”** These can be painful puns, as on Root Greevil; or more subtle Ben-Franklin-style aphorisms like the one on the *Mirage* edition of Boomerang.

Please, folks, repeat these jokes with extreme caution. There are no proven, certified comics on Wizards staff (Rosewater’s respectable Hollywood background notwithstanding), and it’s reeeeally easy for a joke written by a card geek to sound like…well, a card geek when *you* say it. After all, buster, you *are* a card geek. So repeat Lava Burst’s text, if you must. Just don’t expect it to impress that cute co-ed who just stopped by your table at Caribou Coffee to find out what that “strange card game” is all about.

Now that it’s just us card geeks, there are some genuinely funny flavor text moments captured in the wackiest, card-geekiest set of all, *Unglued*. Check out Ow and Censorship for a chuckle, and Look at Me, I'm the DCI and Flock of Rabid Sheep for virtual Monty Python moments.

Lexivore is a clever tribute to several cards in the *Mirage* block (including Coercion, Jamuraan Lion, and Harvest Wurm). And The Cheese Stands Alone is borderline pornographic; I have no idea if that was intentional or not. It’s funny without the double-entendre, anyway.

Again: the co-ed won’t understand why you’re laughing. And believe me, *it’s not her fault.*

**Flavor Two: Storyline Swirl.** There are several subgroups in here: the quotes from characters as on the *Urza's Destiny* Temporal Adept; the clear depictions of plot lines as with Distorting Wake; and the “miscellaneous” descriptions like the one on Diving Griffin, which may or may not involve the storyline. (Who the hell are the Kipamu?)

I’m not a huge fan of the *Weatherlight* storyline, so I have to admit that this set of cards was the hardest to warm up to. (I still like saying “Now you will pay for your treachery!” when I slap down a Pernicious Deed, but Freyalise means little to me as a character.)

The *Odyssey* storyline so far already feels a bit more lively, not least because the characters seem more multi-dimensional, just off information the cards provide on their own. That’s probably why I find the texts of Afflict and Price of Glory somewhat compelling -- and the texts of Hand to Hand and Jilt somewhat less so.

**Flavor Three: Plain Ol' Description.** This captures all of those cards that have “neutral” text, independent of any story line or mention of any characters, races, or places. Recent examples would be Zoologist, Overeager Apprentice, and any of the *Odyssey* spheres, like Sphere of Reason.

Honestly, these are my favorite. Wizards staff write really nicely when they’re not trying to cram in jokes or storyline. (Lest I sound patronizing, I would quickly say that I don’t believe I could do any better.) Vanilla descriptions often give all the flavor the game needs, and let players tell our own stories about who fought whom, and where. Pre-manufactured storylines are nice, and spur the imagination. But I’m glad Wizards also reserves a large portion of cards for us to make our own stories.

**Flavor Four: Literature Sorbet.** For a more refined experience, switch from the *Mirage* Boomerang to the *Legends* edition, with its Shakespeare quote from *King Richard the Second.* This sort of flavor text does not exist in expert level expansions anymore.
