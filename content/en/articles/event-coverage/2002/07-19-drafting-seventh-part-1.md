Pro players traditionally avoid drafting the basic set. It is generally less challenging than working with expansions, and the format is not used in high level tournaments. Consequently, little has been written about playing *Seventh Edition* Limited.

**Magic** Online is rapidly changing that. Amateurs and pros alike are drafting *Seventh* in search of tournament staples such as Birds of Paradise or Adarkar Wastes – or even to stock up on simple necessities like Llanowar Elves and Counterspell. The format is being examined more closely, and articles about it are starting to pop up. I am happy to jump on that bandwagon.

I started drafting *Seventh Edition* a couple of months ago. As **Magic** Online beta was nearing the end, I knew I would have to draft the format soon in order to build up my online collection, and decided to learn its ins and outs before this experience would begin to cost me money. It was definitely a good idea. Although *Seventh* is a fairly simple and straightforward format, it differs from *Odyssey* and *Invasion* blocks so much, that a player in “*Odyssey* draft mode” would make a number of wrong decisions with *Seventh*. I certainly did, losing several drafts before I began to understand the specifics of the format.

Let us begin by looking at the two things that set *Seventh* apart from other formats:

### 1) The Bombs

More so than in other formats, the good rare is king. This is probably the biggest negative about this format, and the reason it is generally avoided by pros. You can out-draft and out-play your opponent, but a single Worship, Opposition, or Ensnaring Bridge will snatch victory from the jaws of defeat. Game designers realize this, but the problem cannot be helped if we, the players, want these good rares to be available to us in Constructed play. They need to be printed somewhere, and *Seventh Edition* gets to be it.

There is no ready-made strategy to get around the problem, but you can improve your odds by making sure to pick up some enchantment and artifact removal for your sideboard. Disenchant is good enough to maindeck in this format. Tranquility and Shatter are quality sideboard cards – especially Tranquility because of the Circles of Protection.

### 2) The Bears

Two-power creatures for two mana are plentiful in this format – every color has got them. There is Spineless Thug, Goblin Raider, Coral Merfolk, Knight Errant, Seeker of Skybreak and, of course, Grizzly Bears that lent their name to every 2/2 for two creature out there.

The Bears are plentiful – but they are not very good.

In every other format, fast creatures that fit your mana curve are crucial. In *Seventh* however, there are too many quality commons with toughness greater than two, which make the Bears so much weaker, it is not too uncommon to see Coral Merfolk go as 12th-15th pick. This is not to say you shouldn’t draft the bears at all – most decks will want one or two to fit their mana curve – but they are not early picks.

### The Colors

The most important thing in any draft format is to properly evaluate the cards, to make the right pick between the several cards available in your colors as often as possible. Once again, *Seventh* is fairly straightforward, but there are a few tricks and a few under- or over-valued cards you should be aware of. Let us take a look at each color’s most relevant commons.

### White

This is the trickiest color in the format, and its commons are most mis-valued. Pacifism is your best available pick – the ability to take any creature out of combat is very important – and when you face black with its Gravedigger, Raise Dead, and no enchantment removal, Pacifism becomes even better than killing their creature outright. 2/2 flyers for four mana follow. Razorfoot Griffin is very solid. Cloudchaser Eagle is generally good, but you must watch out. If your deck is a chock full of Pacifisms and Unholy Strengths (another solid card worthy of about 7th-9th pick), the Eagle may end up sitting in your hand too often.

Next best common is Angelic Page. One of the most underestimated commons in *Seventh Edition*, Page is an excellent card that helps generally puny white creatures deal with the beef available in other colors. Samite Healer is almost as good. It prevents damage even if a creature is not involved in combat, but when combat does happen the +1/+1 effect is generally better. Finally, Crossbow Infantry is the third 1/1 for 2 creature white has. Not as good as the first two, it is still perfectly playable, and will mean trouble to a number of opposing decks. Still, it just isn’t as exciting as the alternatives.

Speaking of damage prevention, Healing Salve is another under-drafted card. Although it is not as powerful as Giant Growth – the only other boon card to survive set rotations – Healing Salve is an awesome combat trick and it can counter a red direct damage spell or Corrupt. I try to have at least one in my draft deck whenever I play white.

Circles of Protection are something I do not place a very high value on. Although they can have a significant impact on the game, most capable opponents will pack sideboard cards to deal with them. Also, it is never a good idea to pick one over a card you would want to see in your main deck. COPs are very late draft picks in my book, though some players may disagree.

Bottom of the barrel (these are the cards that should never really make it into your draft deck): Eager Cadet, Honor Guard, Shield Wall, Skyshroud Falcon.

### Blue

The two blue commons you want to see the most are Prodigal Sorcerer and Vigilant Drake. Drake is the largest common flyer, and its ability to untap at will makes it a very reasonable blocker as well. Tim’s ability to ping any target for one point will take out many a bothersome creature (like all those ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![White Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif) utility creatures discussed above).

Counterspell and Wind Drake are both very solid cards, but you should not be happy with one as a first or second pick. Right below them are Unsummon and Boomerang. Bounce is extremely important in this format, especially with so many great uncommon creature enchantments (Lure, Blanchwood Armor, Serra's Embrace). These bounce spells may not have the cantrip ability of Repulse, but they are extremely important in any deck that utilizes blue as one of its colors, and should be picked reasonably highly.

Next up is Sea Monster – the largest common creature in the format it costs six mana and has a significant disability – and yet it is a very solid pick. Against decks that play islands, he is a fearsome attacker. If they are not playing blue, they are likely playing green – and then Sea Monster becomes the best wall you have in the late game, capable of stopping green’s Wurms and Treefolks. Although Sea Monster is a solid pick, think hard before you add multiples of these to your deck. You might want to watch your mana curve, and you already (hopefully) have a Vigilant Drake or two at its high end.

The next best blue creature is Giant Octopus. Nothing much to be said about it. Any “Hill Giant” (including the Hill Giant himself) is a solid creature to include in your deck. Whereas 2/2 creatures may be the common man of *Odyssey* drafts, 3/3’s dominate *Seventh* format. The only remaining creature of note is Horned Turtle. Along with its white counterpart Standing Troops, it is a cheap creature with high toughness, and is responsible partly for why weenie swarm decks do not tend to succeed in this format.

Counterspell is one of the best blue commons. There are three other common cards that counter spells, but not as effectively – Force Spike, Remove Soul, and Memory Lapse. All of these are pretty good and will often make a cut into your deck. Remove Soul is the best of the three, as this format is all about creatures. Memory Lapse and Force Spike are roughly equal. The best use for Memory Lapse would be to counter a combat trick such as Giant Growth or Healing Salve – it will ruin your opponent’s plan, and then you can work around the instant trick you already know about. But even if the spell you counter is a creature, you still traded card for card and set your opponent back a turn. Force Spike is very good early in the game, but becomes progressively worse as the game gets into its later stages, when your opponent has plenty of mana.

*Seventh* offers very few commons with built-in card economy. That makes Inspiration pretty decent – though gaining one extra card for four mana is hardly spectacular. The lack of card advantage is blue is somewhat made up for by a pair of decent deck manipulation cards – Sage Owl lets you set up the top 4 cards of your library and dig to that land you need right away, and Sleight of Hand just helps you get closer to those rare bombs you drafted. All of these are perfectly playable but not very exciting – you can use them to fill out slots 15 through 23 in your deck.

Bottom of the barrel: Flight, Merfolk of the Pearl Trident, Twiddle
