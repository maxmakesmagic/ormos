I can't put it any better than I did before:</p>&#10;                  <p>As you've noticed, three different systems have been used in the past to order mana symbols on cards. One system always put the colors in the order white-blue-black-red-green, and that ordering was used for <i>Ice Age</i>, <i>Tempest</i>, and <i>Chronicles</i>. Another goes clockwise around the 'pentagon of colors.' <i>Mirage</i> block used that ordering, and we've been following it consistently since <i>Stronghold</i>. And then there's the <i>Legends</i> system, which involved throwing mana symbols into a hat and then pulling them out at random. </p>&#10;                  <p>Our current (and final!) system for ordering mana symbols is pretty simple. If you look at the back of a <b>Magic</b> card, you'll see the pentagon of colors. Going clockwise, the colors are white, blue, black, red, green, white, blue, black . . . . To order a pair of mana symbols, find them in that list, and then put them in whichever order puts the fewest colors between them. For example, white/red has two colors in the middle (blue and black), but red/white has only one (green). That's why <a>Goblin Legionnaire</a>'s mana cost is <img alt="Red Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif"><img alt="White Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif">.</p>&#10;                  <p>Then came the <i>Apocalypse</i> 'wedge' cards. <table>&#10;  <tbody><tr>&#10;    <td>&#10;      <table>&#10;        <tbody><tr>&#10;          <td>&#10;                 <b>November 26, 2007 – <b>Magic </b> Rules Corner</b></td>&#10;          <td> </td>&#10;          <td>&#10;            <img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c7/c1/c7c1fe5cf49f58117fbd931561f1b60bd3183ad6276cf758a8fd3c7d6702fe27.jpg">&#10;          </td>&#10;        </tr>&#10;        <tr>&#10;          <td colspan="3">&#10;            <table>&#10;              <tbody><tr>&#10;                <td>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>Q: </b>Does a card that adds to the cost of another spell affect that card's actual converted mana cost? <a>Sphere of Resistance</a>, for example, adds to the cost of all spells played. Does this mean that an enchantment like <a>Veiled Sentry</a>, which relies on the converted mana cost of a spell being played, would be affected?<br> –Andreas, Hudiksvall, Sweden</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>A: </b>From the <b>Magic</b> Rules Corner:</p>&#10;                  <p>Well, Andreas, when <a>Gaddock Teeg</a> and <a>Thorn of Amethyst</a> are in play—oh, sorry, your question is about <a>Sphere of Resistance</a> and <a>Veiled Sentry</a>. But Mr. Teeg should listen up, too, because his presence in <i>Lorwyn</i> and on the tournament scene has brought questions about converted mana cost to the forefront.</p>&#10;                  <p>The quick answer to your question is no, <a>Veiled Sentry</a> sees the same value regardless of what was actually paid for the spell. With a few exceptions that we'll get to (<img alt="X Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/6b/b1/6bb1152b0a3305d0a7c2e2eb725b8ebd18620607efc62f6f38b0642b97c8501f.gif"> being the doozy), the <i>mana cost</i> of a card—whether it's a permanent in play, a spell on the stack, or a card anywhere else—is exactly what's printed in the upper right-hand corner of the card (or the upper left-hand corner of the art, in the case of <i>Future Sight </i>&quot;timeshifted&quot; cards). This is true regardless of what you actually paid for the spell (again, with a few exceptions we'll cover shortly)—cost reducers, cost increasers, additional costs, alternative costs, whatever. Mana cost is mana cost is mana cost.</p>&#10;                  <p>Mana cost isn't the same thing as <i>converted mana cost</i>, but they're closely related. An object's mana cost includes color, so the mana cost of (say) <a>Wrath of God</a> is <img alt="2 Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif"><img alt="White Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif"><img alt="White Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif">. An object's converted mana cost is a number equal to the total amount of mana in its mana cost, regardless of color. So <a>Wrath of God</a>‘s converted mana cost is 4—and that's true no matter where it is and no matter how much was actually paid for it. How much you pay for a spell when playing it is its <i>total cost</i>, but that's a term you won't see used outside the Comprehensive Rules.</p>&#10;                  <p>Confusing the issue somewhat is the fact that older cards such as <a>Veiled Sentry</a> use some words we don't use anymore. C<i>asting cost</i> is an obsolete term for mana cost, and <i>total casting cost</i> is an obsolete term for converted mana cost, which causes some confusion with &quot;total cost.&quot;</p>&#10;                  <p>Okay, on to the exceptions. The converted mana cost of a spell with <img alt="X Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/6b/b1/6bb1152b0a3305d0a7c2e2eb725b8ebd18620607efc62f6f38b0642b97c8501f.gif"> in its mana cost is calculated treating X as zero anywhere but on the stack, and treating X as the value was actually paid for it when the spell is on the stack. So a <a>Blaze</a> on the stack with X=3 has a converted mana cost of 4, but the same <a>Blaze</a> in your graveyard has a converted mana cost of 1. A <a>Blaze</a> with X=3 with a <a>Thorn of Amethyst</a> in play has a total cost of <img alt="4 Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/36/29/3629288966daa042decf01d3752789ab6676fddd67c3679fa580cd3baecae158.gif"><img alt="Red Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif">, but its converted mana cost is still 4. A <a>Fireball</a> with X=3 that's dividing X between three targets has a total cost of <img alt="6 Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/2d/12/2d12c8318f11a9188ce9fdd831b87ed97536df32e65841004ce583d399c4b454.gif"><img alt="Red Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif">, but its converted mana cost is also 4, because the extra mana paid for multiple targets is an additional cost. So your <a>Veiled Sentry</a> would end up as a 4/4 in all of these cases.</p>&#10;                  <p>(Incidentally, this is why <a>Gaddock Teeg</a> prevents noncreature spells wth <img alt="X Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/6b/b1/6bb1152b0a3305d0a7c2e2eb725b8ebd18620607efc62f6f38b0642b97c8501f.gif"> in their mana cost from being played entirely—to forgo the rules oddities that would arise if he was trying to keep you from playing a spell whose converted mana cost was <i>going to be</i> 4 or greater.)</p>&#10;                  <table>&#10;                    <tbody><tr>&#10;                      <td>&#10;                        <div>&#10;                          <div>&#10;                            <a>&#10;                              <img alt="Fireball" src="https://i.ormos.uk/ed/a7/eda7b4fad9db2e6bf986cfc4d14ebc59526f65a877d8ffb3070d5dde2a8311f5.jpg">&#10;                            </a>&#10;                          </div>&#10;                        </div>&#10;                      </td>&#10;                      <td>&#10;                        <div>&#10;                          <div>&#10;                            <a>&#10;                              <img alt="Boom" src="https://i.ormos.uk/8c/1c/8c1c655b4b51fe1c4fc26d8adb01430d7414c3a5bc74d7c573dc0921c814750a.jpg">&#10;                            </a>&#10;                          </div>&#10;                        </div>&#10;                      </td>&#10;                    </tr>&#10;                  </tbody></table>&#10;                  <p>The second exception is split cards. A split card on the stack has the mana cost (and name, color(s), etc.) of only whichever side you played—so <a>Boom</a> would cause <a>Veiled Sentry</a> to become a 2/2, while <a>Bust</a> would make it a 6/6. However, and this is where things get weird, a split card anywhere else has the mana cost of both sides. That doesn't mean they're added together—it means that any time you look at a split card's mana cost or converted mana cost, you get both answers. (The same is true for all other qualities of a split card that isn't on the stack.) <a>Rough // Tumble</a>‘s converted mana cost is 2 <i>and</i> 6. That means that if you (for instance) mill it away with <a>Lammastide Weave</a>, you'll gain 2 and 6 life, for a total of 8. But if a player plays <a>Void</a> and chooses 2, a <a>Rough // Tumble</a> in your hand will be discarded, because its converted mana cost is 2.</p>&#10;                  <p>This is especially bizarre when clashing. If you play <a>Oaken Brawler</a> and reveal <a>Kithkin Greatheart</a> (converted mana cost 2) while I reveal <a>Dead // Gone</a> (converted mana cost 1 and 3), we both win the clash—we each revealed a card that had a higher converted mana cost. That means that your <a>Oaken Brawler</a> does get the +1/+1 counter, but I also draw a card off of <a>Sylvan Echoes</a>. Everyone's a winner!</p>&#10;                  <p>And that, hopefully, is more about converted mana cost than you'll ever need to know.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <i>The <b>Magic</b> Rules Corner is a weekly feature dedicated to answering your rules questions. In fact, after you announce that you're playing a spell, <b>choosing modes is almost always the first thing you do</b> (along with choosing the value of X and announcing your intention to pay any additional or alternative costs). When you play <a>Profane Command</a>, you must announce which modes you are choosing (that's rule 409.1b in the Comprehensive Rules), then announce which target(s) you are choosing for each mode (that's rule 409.1c). You can't choose modes for which there are no legal targets.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>Changing targets:</b> When you change a spell's targets, <b>you don't get to choose different modes</b>. It has the same modes and targets as the original—although in this case it copies the new targets you just chose. Your opponent loses X life, then the other mode does whatever it says to do (and remember, you control the copy, which matters if the &quot;return target creature card&quot; mode was chosen).</li>&#10;                      <li>Assuming nobody has any responses, the original <a>Profane Command</a> resolves. Your opponent loses <i>another</i> X life, then the other mode does whatever it says to do (but your opponent controls it this time).</li>&#10;                    </ol>&#10;                  <p></p>&#10;                  <p>At this point, your opponent has lost X life twice, and you've gotten to perform the other chosen mode twice (giving two creatures -X/-X, giving your own creatures fear on his turn—not too helpful, but it could keep his creatures from swinging in to kill you—or returning the best creature card in your graveyard and the worst one in your opponent's graveyard to play). This is a replacement effect and follows the usual rules for those (we'll cover what that means in a moment). Once again, that's in sharp contrast to the planeswalker combat rules.</p>&#10;                  <p>The other strange nuance here has to do with damage prevention effects. Damage prevention effects are also replacement effects. When multiple replacement effects try to apply to the same event, the affected player or the controller of the affected object gets to choose what order to apply them in. This means that if one effect (say, the damage prevention shield from <a>Bandage</a>) is attempting to prevent the damage dealt to a player by a spell or ability and another effect (say, the planeswalker rules) is attempting to redirect that damage, then the player who would be damaged gets to decide whether to prevent the damage or send it along.</p>&#10;                  <p>You might wonder why the rules work so differently for combat and noncombat damage. You'd have difficulty finding a more Timmy experience than dropping a memorable 20/20 <a>Kilnmouth Dragon</a>. My Dragon deck survives today, though I've cut the overpowered ones like <a>Covetous Dragon</a> because they simply made the deck less fun. Elves could splash multiple colors off of <a>Birchlore Rangers</a>, outlast by drawing lots more Elves (<a>Bloodline Shaman</a>), beatdown with massive Elves (<a>Elvish Soultiller</a>, <a>Elvish Aberration</a>, <a>Timberwatch Elf</a>, <a>Gempalm Strider</a>), or go combo with the greenest card ever printed (<a>Biorhythm</a>), and all of it fun. Beasts gain points for being a tribe of large creatures, which are always fun, but lose points for being a creature type that ended up on so many random cards in the past, but gain those points back again for random awesome combos like <a>Contested Cliffs</a> + <a>Spiritmonger</a>.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>Zombies</b>&#10;                  </p>&#10;                  <p>Zombies hold the distinction of being the most flavorful tribe in <i>Onslaught</i>. They emerge from the graveyard, living things fear them, they can walk across swamps, and they want to eat your brains. All that flavorful stuff translates well into <b>Magic</b> cards.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>Soldiers</b>&#10;                  </p>&#10;                  <p>Soldiers have the whole buddy system going on, and there's plenty of them, but Soldiers always felt like they were never the best at anything (what we in R&amp;D call a &quot;strong mechanical identity&quot;). They weren't as good at attacking as Beasts, they weren't as good at blocking as Walls, and they weren't as good at what I call &quot;doing lots of stuff for no good reason&quot; as Elves.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>Clerics</b>&#10;                  </p>&#10;                  <p>Be warned, we've passed into &quot;unfun&quot; territory. That's nice. Moving on.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>Wizards</b>&#10;                  </p>&#10;                  <p>Wizards is a blue tribe that draws cards (<a>Riptide Director</a>) and counters spells (<a>Voidmage Prodigy</a>). Thanks to cards like <a>Goblin Warchief</a>, <a>Skirk Prospector</a>, <a>Goblin Piledriver</a>, and <a>Gempalm Incinerator</a>, Goblins are by far the most powerful tribe ever, and the despairing truth is they always will be. The only saving grace of the Goblin tribe is its testament that power corrupts...and fun in <b>Magic</b> is paramount.</p>&#10;                </td>&#10;              </tr>&#10;            </tbody></table>&#10;          </td>&#10;        </tr>&#10;      </tbody></table>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Every <b>Magic</b> player should learn what these keywords do. In general, though, you won't see a singleton card with a keyword from another block. Does this mean that if you have a planeswalker your opponent can attack it nevertheless?<br> –Davide, Grenoble, France </p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>A: </b>From the <b>Magic</b> Rules Corner:</p>&#10;                  <p>That's exactly what it means. When your opponent declares attackers, he or she chooses what each creature is attacking: you, or a planeswalker you control (and if you have more than one, which one). These rules are similar to the &quot;Attack Multiple Players&quot; optional multiplayer rules. (They also work with those rules—in a multiplayer game using those rules, the controller of an attacking creature chooses which player or planeswalker each creature will attack.)</p>&#10;                  <p>If a creature is attacking a planeswalker, it isn't attacking a player—it's attacking a planeswalker instead. So it can attack a planeswalker even if it couldn't attack that planeswalker's controller. Similarly, if you control a Ghostly Prison or Propaganda, the controller of a creature doesn't have to pay <img alt="2 Mana" src="https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif"> in order for that creature to attack one of your planeswalkers.</p>&#10;                  <p>As the <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20090601210610/http://www.wizards.com/Magic/TCG/Article.aspx?x=magic/rules/planeswalkers">Planeswalker Rules Page</a> puts it:</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    </p><blockquote>As the declare attackers step begins, if the defending player controls a planeswalker, the active player declares who or what each attacking creature is attacking: the defending player or one of that player's planeswalkers.</blockquote>&#10;                  <p></p>&#10;                  <p>You may notice that the planeswalker rules still refer to the controller of a planeswalker being attacked as the &quot;defending player.&quot; The reason for this is simple: There has to be a defending player. There are just too many effects in <b>Magic</b> that refer to the &quot;defending player&quot; to let that be a null set. This is why, for instance, landwalk abilities still work when a creature attacks a planeswalker.</p>&#10;                  <p>So when a creature attacks your planeswalker, you aren't being attacked, but you're still the defending player. Neat, huh?</p>&#10;                  <p>Note that all of this applies only to creatures attacking planeswalkers—the rules for planeswalkers being damaged outside of combat work quite differently. <table>&#10;  <tbody><tr>&#10;    <td>&#10;      <table>&#10;        <tbody><tr>&#10;          <td>&#10;                 <b>November 2, 2007</b></td>&#10;          <td> </td>&#10;          <td>&#10;            <img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/f0/9f/f09f4d2a172231ed02bcfadb60e0264389ebd0a5d2bbaaf40ad6a927154a8f0e.jpg">&#10;          </td>&#10;        </tr>&#10;        <tr>&#10;          <td colspan="3">&#10;            <table>&#10;              <tbody><tr>&#10;                <td>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>Q: </b>Is it legal to alter cards for tournaments? I'm talking about updating old cards that got e.g. creature type or wording update in Oracle with their new types / wordings. Technically I don't see what's the harm of that, but as far as I understand, a card is illegal if a rules-impacting part of it is somehow covered. So, how's it with cards that have obsolete information written on them?<br> –Jarno, Finland </p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>A: </b>From John Carter, DCI-Certified Level 4 Judge:</p>&#10;                  <p>Technically, this isn't legal, but check with your head judge.</p>&#10;                  <p>Alteration of cards is covered by Universal Tournament Rule #28:</p>&#10;                  <blockquote>Cards used in a tournament may not have writing on their faces other than signatures or artistic modifications. Modifications may not obscure the artwork so as to make the card unrecognizable. If modifications to a card are deemed by the Head Judge to constitute outside notes or unsporting conduct, the player using such cards will be subject to the appropriate provisions of the DCI Penalty Guidelines.</blockquote>&#10;                  <p>The head judge of an event has final authority to determine the legality of modifications. The lower the rule enforcement level (level of competition), the more discretion a judge will generally allow. Noting errata may seem innocent and helpful to both players, but it can still be disallowed. One concern is that the notation may look like outside notes. Another is that by altering the cards you are representing what looks like all current errata. If a player makes a mistake or misses a detail, this looks bad for the player.</p>&#10;                  <p>In all cases, I encourage you to ask the head judge before the start of a tournament—receiving outside assistance, even with the best intentions, can cause removal from the tournament. One fork (we'll call it &quot;left&quot;) is to embrace the fact that a changeling is all creatures, all at once, all the time, so every changeling card should show a creature that's a crazy conglomeration of different creatures, the more the better, like a turbo-hallucinogenic chimera! The other fork (&quot;right&quot;) is to decide that although changelings are mechanically all creature types at once, in the conceptualization of a fantasy world they are only one thing at a time.</p>&#10;                  <p>We took the right fork for some good reasons. I'll list five, because even though Rosewater says things work in threes, Magic works in fives.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    </p><ol>&#10;                      <li>If we had taken the left fork, all changeling cards would look more or less identical, because all of them have all the same creature types. We like it when cards are distinct from each other. (For example, I thought <a>Ramosian Sergeant</a> / <a>Lieutenant</a> / <a>Captain</a> / <a>Commander</a> were really pushing it. Who can remember which is which?) </li>&#10;                      <li>&#10;                        <i>Lorwyn</i>'s elementals are already weird conglomerations, most of which contain animal elements. We didn't want changelings and elementals stepping on each other's toes. </li>&#10;                      <li>Artists get pretty annoyed when you ask them to paint a creature comprised of a hundred separate things, let alone a hundred different things. <a>Pain</a>ting a simple thing really well is hard enough. </li>&#10;                      <li>Crazy, chaotic turboglomerations of many different creatures—things that make the elementals look like cute little plush toys—wouldn't be at home in the idyllic, storybook-inspired world of <i>Lorwyn</i>. </li>&#10;                      <li>The fact that the changelings' mechanics do not reflect every creature type presents a pretty big problem. For example, any given changeling is a Bird, a Bat, a Dragon, and an Angel, but only two of the nineteen changeling cards should be shown flying: <a>Avian Changeling</a> and <a>Wings of Velis Vel</a>. </li>&#10;                    </ol>&#10;                  <p></p>&#10;                  <p>So by choosing the right fork, we had committed to the idea that the changelings were one thing at a time—that they changed shape. From there, it just seemed silly not to call them shapeshifters, since shifting shape is how the changeling ability was concepted. Even though it isn't game-functional, having the word &quot;Shapeshifter&quot; on the type line gives an important cue to how the flavor of the changeling ability works.</p>&#10;                </td>&#10;              </tr>&#10;            </tbody></table>&#10;          </td>&#10;        </tr>&#10;      </tbody></table>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;</tbody></table>
