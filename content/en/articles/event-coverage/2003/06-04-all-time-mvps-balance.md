Of the three choices in last week’s poll, Balance won by a resounding majority. I expected it to. After watching the *Eighth Edition* polls on MagicTheGathering.com, I was curious to see if the way people voted there would translate here, especially when given choices of different power levels. In the MagicTheGathering.com polls, the card with the higher power/secondary market value won the majority of the polls, with the notable exception of Rewind beating Dismiss. I wanted to see if this would happen in All-Time MVPs, especially with *Sideboard’s* more tournament-focused readership. It did.

For the first poll, I took three cards with different levels of power and reputation. First, there was Balance, a clear-cut winner in power and longevity, still played in many Type I decks. Second Erhnam Djinn, a creature that was potent and feared in a single metagame, but has been ineffectual in others. Last, Icy Manipulator, a card that has been the lynch pin in several decks, but a team player more than an individual standout. All three of these cards are All-Time MVP-caliber cards that have served different functions over the years.

This week’s poll focuses on three red cards that have figured prominently into Sligh decks. Be sure to vote for the one you want to be the next All-Time MVP.

Balance, on the surface, is the ultimate equalizer. That’s what the card says, anyway. Equalize both players’ cards in hand, creatures in play and lands. It seems fair enough. Right? ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![White Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif): put everyone on equal footing. It restarts the game in both card advantage and board position. If that was all the card did, it certainly wouldn’t be an All-Time MVP.

Like many of **Magic’s** cards, Balance has a symmetrical effect – it hits you as much as your opponent. Because of this, Balance was not immediately recognized to be a power card. It isn’t until you discover how to break the symmetry of a card like Balance that it becomes potent. The split second that someone figured it out, Balance became one of the most powerful cards ever created, going from a cheap, ineffective way to even out card advantage to a brutal swing effect. Christopher Carbone sent in this explanation: 

*“On the surface, it is a “play to not lose” card - i.e. if you are creature poor, mana screwed, or at card disadvantage, you get this card as a savior. When seen like that, the card went largely unnoticed. However, if you view the card as it SHOULD be seen - as an offensive opportunity - then the card is phenomenal. When finally noticed by players, the card was soon restricted (just like Recall and Lotus). The card makes overextended players suffer and rewards smart asymmetrical play.”*

Once players figured out how to use Balance, this “equalizer” turned into a combination Armageddon, Wrath of God and Mind Twist. Taken separately, those three cards are amazingly powerful, future MVPs in their own rights. Put together in one card, it is ridiculous. ### When It Was Legal…

The trick of breaking Balance’s symmetry was first revealed to the internet Magic community by Adam Maysonet, who designed the deck in 1994. Though his name has fallen through the cracks, Adam was a well-known player in those days and his deck was received well. He showed us his deck, Rack-Balance, and gave a brief breakdown. You can read his article at an archived version of the Dojo, [here](https://web.archive.org/web/20030604000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.classicdojo.org/history/mrb.html&origin=sideboard_feature_20030604a).

Looking at this design, it’s obvious how powerful Balance is. The entire deck is based around it. There is minimal removal, a minimum of pure threat cards – all this deck wants to do is find Balance. Like many older decks, in retrospect, there are cards that wouldn’t make the cut – or redesigns made obvious by years of **Magic** theory. But to criticize this deck for a lack of technology is like criticizing NASA for not using a space shuttle for the moon landing in 1969. It was groundbreaking, one way or the other.

The strategy of Rack-Balance is simple: find Balance, wreck your opponent and let them die on The Rack. The entire deck is set up as cheap search cards, removal spells and utility cards. Balance doesn’t affect artifacts or enchantments, so they are a great way to keep your cards around after a Balance. All the cards in this deck are dirt cheap, so you’ll burn through your hand casting artifacts and enchantments, while discarding extra lands searching with the Bazaar of Baghdad, leaving you in a great situation to Balance. This can happen as early as turn 1 or 2. When you Balance in this deck, you’re going to gain massive card advantage. 

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>&quot;It almost always causes a game swing that is nearly impossible to recover from.&quot; - Enrico Torazza</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Balance was restricted shortly after this deck gained prominence, but that scarcely stopped it from being a force on the tournament scene. Even restricted, it appeared in many decks as a reset button, especially combined with Zuran Orb, which allowed a player to sacrifice all of his or her lands to Armageddon. Type I decks had several methods to search for Balance and Standard decks received Mystical Tutor in *Mirage*.

Consider This Scenario

David Chapman sent in this play as a testament of Balance’s stopping power:

*“I remember a game I played in 1995 where I led with turn 1 forest, Tinder Wall, double Orcish Lumberjack, turn 2 Shivan Dragon and Whirling Dervish while holding a forest. Setting a two-turn clock on turn 2 would normally be enough, but my opponent just dropped a second Plains, Balanced us down to zero land, zero creatures and one card, and went on to win the game.”*

### Why Was It Great?

“In a bad situation, Balance immediately provides one of the largest swings of any spell in the entire game,” says Philip Stanton. This largely sums up what makes Balance an All-Time MVP. Balance is magnificently broken. Unlike Ancestral Recall, which is just plain broken, Balance has a direct impact on the game. As such, it requires a little setting up but, unlike the vanilla +2 card advantage of Recall, Balance takes a larger amount of card advantage from your opponent. It also causes massive swings in board position. A player can be sitting comfortably with four lands in play, three good creatures and three cards in hand. Balance makes them all go away.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/d3/5d/d35d7fbea0fde56ca620b79ff6ca47fef6914a7a4f3c1ffaf84233b36b14e2d3.jpg) As anyone who has ever been hit with a Mind Twist for five on turn 1 can tell you, it’s a lot worse to lose your own cards early in the game than for your opponent to get a couple extra. You can compare the playability of Mind Twist to Braingeyser on this one. The discard aspect of Balance is probably the most brutal. Players are generally prepared for effects that clear the board of creatures and spells that destroy all lands. The way people plan for these sweeping effects is to play conservatively and not overcommit to the board. When Wrath of God gets played, creatures in hand are safe. Not so with Balance. 

Ideally, the Balance player will set up a situation where both players end up with no creatures, no lands and no cards in hand – with some artifacts or enchantments in play to pull out of the situation faster. If you’ve ever been on the wrong end of Obliterate right after emptying your hand, you know how difficult it is to start the game over with no cards in hand, especially if you’re opponent has cards to rebuild with.

Balance is such a powerful effect that there have been few attempts to fix it. White generally does the creature kill and land kill things, but discard is more of a black effect. While there have been many Wrath of God variants (about one per block) and a couple Armageddon-type spells, Balance hasn’t been emulated too often. Equipoise, printed way back in *Visions*, was a strange Balance-like enchantment. It did find its way into a reasonable deck when coupled with Sands of Time, to make the phasing permanent. Balancing Act, appearing in *Odyssey*, looked closer to Balance, appearing at a time where it was more difficult to play a Balance-style strategy. Even still, people found a way to make it work with Invasion’s sacrifice lands and Terravore. It was a rogue deck at best, but if you’d ever been on the wrong end of it, it certainly had shades of 1994.

If nothing else, Balance showed us that putting three great abilities on a single card that is cheaper than any one of the abilities was a bad idea. This seems painfully obvious now, but remember that Balance was largely ignored for over a year. Balance taught us that symmetry could be broken to great effect. This knowledge has players going straight after cards that wouldn’t have been touched in ’94. It also forces R&D to consider symmetrical effects very, very carefully, as in the early days, they undercosted spells that have a drawback to their caster, like Balance, Winter Orb and Juzam Djinn, thinking the drawback itself would be enough. 

A few months back, MagicTheGathering.com’s Ben Bleiweiss did a story on the top 50 white cards. You can read it [here](/en/articles/uncommonknowledge/2003/02-07-the-top-50-white-cards). Guess where Balance ranked? That’s right, #1.
