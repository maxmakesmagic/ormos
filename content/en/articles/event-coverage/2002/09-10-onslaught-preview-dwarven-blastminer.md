Nonbasic lands have been a huge part of **Magic**, both in tournaments and in casual play, since the release of Alpha. The ancestors of today's painlands, filter lands, and comes-into-play-tapped lands alike, the original dual lands were simply superior to their basic counterparts. Their "drawback" - that they counted as land of both types and were thus vulnerable to both Karma and Tsunami, or mountainwalk and forestwalk - hardly balanced out the enormous boost in mana consistency they provided. It wasn't long before dual lands made up the entire mana base of most competitive decks, providing access to all five colors of mana at no cost beyond a hefty investment in booster packs.

Rather than simply allow the tournament scene to degenerate into a field of dual lands as far as the eye could see - or turn into a battle of the haves versus the have nots - Wizards of the Coast introduced cards to punish strategies that relied too heavily on them. Thus, nonbasic land hosers were born. The first of these was Blood Moon. Introduced in *The Dark*, Blood Moon was very powerful against decks with all nonbasics, but not particularly devastating for decks that could still produce non-red mana with basic lands or alternative mana sources. Additionally, Blood Moon did nothing to cripple an opponent permanently, since its effect immediately ended once it left play. Often players could simply Disenchant a Blood Moon off of a basic plains or a Mox Pearl, or even operate effectively with only red mana by employing artifacts and red spells.

The next nonbasic land hoser to come along was Primal Order in *Homelands*. While initially met with a warm reception, this enchantment - a ![2 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif) Karma for nonbasic lands - quickly fell out of favor with tournament players when they realized its effect was simply too slow and too weak to be effective. The fact that an enchantment that permanently dealt damage to users of nonbasic lands for each they had in play wasn't enough to discourage players from using them - simply because the advantage gained by duals was too great - must have struck a chord at Wizards of the Coast, because the next few nonbasic land hosers took things up a notch.

The most impressive of these were Dwarven Miner from *Mirage* and Back to Basics from *Urza's Saga*. Both saw a great deal of play in tournament caliber decks in a variety of formats as long as they were legal, with both remaining absolute powerhouses in the Vintage format as well as serious threats in Extended, at least prior to the rotation of dual lands. Even the Standard format has been so permeated with nonbasic lands that these cards have historically been quite powerful, with Ben Rubin's Dwarven Miners in his Sligh deck proving decisive in his matches against Survival-Recur decks when he placed second at the 1998 World Championships.

Just a brief glimpse at the popular Standard decks at the recent World Championships can give a taste of how powerful these cards might be today. Even the two-color Psychatog decks sport upwards of ten nonbasic lands, with the more eccentric three and even four color decks use well over a dozen. Painlands, tap lands, and filter lands alike - and even the original painland in the form of City of Brass - all see play in popular decks, just asking for Dwarven Miner to return from retirement and take things - at the risk of a miserable pun - back to basics.

Ask, it seems, and ye shall receive. Meet Dwarven Blastminer, *Onslaught's* addition to the long line of nonbasic land hosers and clearly a direct descendent of the angry little Dwarf in the rutabagas. For the same mana cost as his granddaddy, Dwarven Blastminer is a 1/1 creature with an identical ability to that of the Miner of old. Dwarves these days have apparently taken to the use of explosives, however, thus necessitating the change in name, and in doing so seem to have gotten a bit weaker from not carrying around pickaxes all day, with their toughness dropping from two to one. These are the prices one must pay for such technology.

Technological advancements in the world of **Magic**, however, aren't all about Dwarves getting a bit softer around the belly because they can just blow things up rather than hack them apart. By now everyone knows about the new morph mechanic, and this new twist helps spice up this old favorite. Apparently along with explosives, the new Dwarven mining companies are equipped with a full assortment of Illusionary Masks, because they can go undercover and get their beat on as 2/2s rather than blowing up nonbasic lands if the need arises. One of the unfortunate drawbacks of the original Miner was the fact that he was extremely weak against decks without a plethora of nonbasic lands. A one power, two casting cost creature is hardly the picture of efficiency. Of course, Gray Ogres aren't the cream of the beatdown crop either, but they do offer a significant step up the ladder from the mighty Squire.

Against decks with lots of nonbasics, then, Dwarven Blastminer is an absolute wrecking ball, blowing the opponent's entire mana base into little bite size pieces while your other creatures mop up the mess. Against other decks, he's a Grey Ogre - nothing flashy, but certainly better than he'd be without the morph ability. If your opponent starts out with basic lands and you play him as a 2/2 and then they get tricky by dropping things like Nantuko Monastery or Cabal Coffers, Dwarven Blastminer can throw off his mask and start in on the real dirty work by just paying his morph cost - he won't even have summoning sickness if he's already been in play, and he can start tossing explosives around at your opponent's land that very same turn.

While *Invasion* block may be rotating out of Standard, and along with it many popular nonbasic lands, Dwarven Blastminer will still have a big role to play in the new environment. *Seventh Edition* painlands, *Odyssey* Block filter lands, and perhaps even a number of the new *Onslaught* lands will all see play, and there are few better ways to beat a multicolor deck than to deprive it of its mana base. This new incarnation of Dwarven Miner may not be as devastating as its ancestor was in formats of old - or as fond of rutabagas - but the Blastminer will be sure to make his granddaddy proud.
