*Coldsnap* will hit the Constructed universe later this month, a changed projected by many to transform our metagame of Guild-stamped Karoos and Kamigawa Block Dragons into a snowstorm of Scrying Sheets and a tangle Ohran Vipers for the remaining National Championships competitors... but not yet. You might expect the format, which has been essentially unchanged since the Regional Championships back in May, to remain static because of the incoming and irresistible format alteration, but the fluctuations between different National Championship Top 8s from week to week indicate that this is anything but true.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img alt="Ohran_Viper" src="https://i.ormos.uk/15/80/1580f2fd0ef5e0b825f11b3a3c44ed48c032422c06fbfc31fe00a61ddf7f4c39.jpg"><br><i>The shape of things to come?</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Case in point, last week at the U.S. National Championships, riding its domination of Australian Nationals and popularity online, we saw Solar Flare or "Japanimator" rise to a level of popularity untouched even by White Weenie at last year's tournament, eventually taking two of the three available National Team spots. This week... not so much. Is/was Solar Flare a flash in the pan/deck of the week in real-life **Magic**? I don't think the deck's career is over, necessarily, but for certain its popularity has fallen through the floor, with many smart players opting for the so-called [Wisdom of Youth](/en/articles/theweekthatwas/2006/08-04-the-wisdom-of-youth). From purely anecdocal experience, I competed in a North American Challenge Qualifier \[Standard tournament\] at Neutral Ground last week, and if there was a Solar Flare player in the room, I didn't see him. I did, however, fight Vore or Heartbeat in every Swiss round but one, and Heartbeat again in both the semifinals and the finals. Just as Solar Flare rocked the U.S., so it seems today - or at least last weekend - is / was the age of the anti-Flare.

*How Does It Work?*  
Heartbeat of Spring initially uses Sakura-Tribe Elder and Kodama's Reach to establish a strong mana base. Via its many Transmute cards - Drift of Phantasms and Muddle the Mixture - Heartbeat assembles an essentially inexorable combination kill. Drift of Phantasms can search up either combination element (Early Harvest or Heartbeat of Spring), or either kill card (Maga, Traitor to Mortals or Invoke the Firemind). Muddle the Mixture's primary role, besides permission resistance, is to nab Weird Harvest. Weird Harvest's job is to get all the Drifts, possibly some Tribe Elders, and usually Maga; for their part, the Drifts will profitably exchange for Early Harvests once Heartbeat of Spring is already in play. Given all the relative material, the deck will generate a massive amount of mana with Early Harvest and Heartbeat of Spring, then unload with Maga or Invoke for 20+ life loss or damage. A key incentive to Heartbeat of Spring is that unlike many historical combination decks, due to the Weird Harvest engine, given sufficient mana, it never, ever, fizzles.

Guillaume's version plays a single sideboarded Research // Development. This card can play alternate kill condition, but is really there to recover combination pieces should he be struck by a Cranial Extraction. Research // Development is a prime Muddle the Mixture Transmute candidate.

*Position:*  
 During the Charleston PTQ season, we continually argued that Heartbeat of Spring was the best deck in the format. By the end of the season, 100% of qualifying teams were fronting Heartbeat decks. The archetype plays many of the best cards in the format, including Sakura-Tribe Elder and Sensei's Divining Top, and can consistently outmaneuver "real" permission decks with its light eight-pack suite.

... And then it largely fell off the radar.

The rise in popularity of Solar Flare, a deck long on short-term power but little consistent resistance to a combination deck with Sensei's Divining Top (due in large part to a relatively slow clock and tons of "dead weight" creature removal spells) has made Heartbeat a contender once again.

Subtly, this dirty combo deck has two nice edges on the format. The more important one is that it is a solid performer against Solar Flare; the almost-as-important one is that it is a solid performer against co-Solar Flare hater, Vore. Barring the dreaded Eye of Nowhere draw on the play, Heartbeat has the advantage over time because, well, Kodama's Reach versus Demolish in a fight... Pick one.

Guillaume ran the Canada meat grinder, then went 5-1 in Standard, then ran the Top 8. Doing the math, we can see his Standard portion as roughly (and I am using broad numbers here), 1,000,000,000,000-1. 

Heartbeat of Spring, you get this week's Swimming With Sharks Two Thumbs Up.

*How Does It Work?*  
 Snakes is a straightforward offensive deck... that has more tricks than the cereal aisle at your local grocery store. *How Does It Work?*  
 This is an offensive deck. It starts on Hound of Konda first turn, and plays highly efficient drops at every mana point up to Ghost Council of Orzhova. Rather than relying on gimmicks like Arcane + Tallowisp or combination kills like Nantuko Husk + Promise of Bunrei, Nathan's version has incremental card advantage via Phyrexian Arena and simply makes efficient plays, maximizing high incentive cards like Umezawa's Jitte and Mortify. While it doesn't do anything fancy, Nathan's deck has four copies of Paladin en-Vec and four copies of Umezawa's Jitte; together the two have been called "the best combination in Standard" by none other than design god Tsuyoshi Fujita. He borrows Persecute, a key element of the previously ascendant Solar Flare deck in many matchups, and can transform into a beatdown-hating board dominator via Descendant of Kiyomaro, Manriki-Gusari, and Last Gasp.

*Position:*

Because of their workmanlike rather than exotic or show-stopping methodology, Hand variants get less press than Ghost Dad or Ghost Husk, but it is telling that not only did Nathan make his National Team, but that his 4-2 Standard record included two concessions, including one in the final round where he was already a game up.

Who knows how well an efficient, reasonably fast, and disruptive, deck like this one might be in the erratic Wild West shootout that is pre-*Coldsnap* Standard?

*Deck*: Zoo

*How Does It Work?*  
 Zoo is basically the simplest deck in the format. *Position:*

*How Does It Work?*  
 Solar Flare is probably properly called a hybrid deck. It plays multiple angles, but does not specialize in any one. *Deck*: Boros

*How Does It Work?*  
 Unlike the Lantern Kami / Suntail Hawk decks that were so popular almost a year ago at Champs, the modern Boros deck is built - albeit a bit loosely - on a Fujita 20/20/20 model: 20 land, 20 2/2s (yeah, yeah, Savannah Lions is 2/1 I know), and 20 "stuff." Jason has mixed it up a bit, adding a 21st land (Shinka) and a 21st 2/2, cutting a burn spell and a Jitte to do so. Because the deck has "all 2/2s," running Isamaru on one, Hand of Honor on two, and Paladin en-Vec on three, it has highly redundant draws. The 2/2s match up very well with other creature decks, with Hand of Honor dominating B/W and trading nicely with Heezy and Zoo, while Paladin en-Vec dominates any and all matchups via equipment and can hold his own even against a 20/20 Magnivore. The genius of the deck, though, is its mana base.

Originally derided as "budget Boros," the inclusion of Boros Garrison is what makes viable this style of beatdown deck at all, essentially justifying the Fujita 20/20/20 breakdown. Boros Garrison is what lets the deck shave between two and four lands, allowing the deck to play more 2/2s, more burn spells, more "stuff" in general, acting as a subtle card advantage, and moving equipment for strategic one-man attacks during the middle turns.

*Position:*  
 Teddy Card Game, editor of this site, spent the weekend trying to get Canadians to play this deck. "No one" listened, but Patterson scored a perfect 6-0 in the Swiss. Boros may in fact be the ideal deck for the present metagame. Jason said that he played it due to great matchups with Solar Flare, B/W, and Vore, but this style of Boros also has an excellent Heartbeat matchup! Unlike U.S. Nationals, where many American Pros, viz. Osyp Lebedowicz and Antonino De Rosa, went with G/W, the Canadian Top 8 has not one Loxodon Hierarch, not even in Noworaj's sideboard! 

Despite an unpredictable loss to McGregor's Husk in five, it looks like clear sailing for Boros... unless Three Stupid Elephants (tm) decide to reappear in the near future. With Solar Flare and now Heartbeat at the top of high profile National Championships, the reappearance of G/W decks does not seem likely in the short term. The Boros Legion's other enemies - straight U/W Control, Firemane Control, Ghost Dad with Descendent of Kiyomaro, and so on - are simply not popular.

*How Does It Work?*  
 The draw that makes the girls cry, sends our boys to war, and ultimately lights the fire of inspiration beneath the bottoms of potential Vore players is Eye of Nowhere on turn 2 (forcing the discard), followed by another Eye or Stone Rain, followed by Demolish, followed by a re-load. Somewhere before the end, the Vore player will deploy an enormous Magnivore - fueled by innumerable sorceries (note the use of Eye of Nowhere over Boomerang and so on) - which will kill in as few as one trip across the Red Zone. Less dramatic, if more consistent, methodology is to manage the opponent to about four or five lands and then play Wildfire, clearing everything, again planning to win with an efficient Magnivore.

On the play, Vore may be the most imposing deck in all of Standard, and can certainly blow out any and all opponents. The real beauty, though, is that Vore never, ever, runs out of gas. It has card drawing on turns one, three, and five. It can flip a Compulsive Research with Goblin Flectomancer, or murder a Simic Sky Swallower with Wildfire and Pyroclasm in concert. 

*Position:*  
 From purely an anti-Solar Flare position, Vore is either the best or second best Level One deck. It is a long game dog against Heartbeat, but will often blow the dirty combo player out if it wins the flip, by going first in two games. The real disincentive to Vore - and its nagging criticism for some months - is a soft matchup with beatdown. Surely Vore can beat any kind of deck, and Pyroclasm and Wildfire can sweep small creatures, but Vore has consistently had problems performing against creatures, especially in Game One. I would still give it a gold star and happily recommend that a friend play it in the present metagame, just so long as he understands the importance of going first, protecting margins, the possibility of playing against Level Two metagame decks like Boros, and - most importantly - showing up with a strong sideboard. Check out Nick's... He has almost a full transform for creatures, with Spell Snare for Bears, Volcanic Hammer for Wolves, and Threads of Disloyalty for... um... other Bears and Wolves.

It will be interesting to see if the adversarial tension between Solar Flare and anti-Solar Flare camps continues this week, and who the big winner will be, if either.
