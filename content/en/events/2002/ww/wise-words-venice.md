Travelling off-continent with Neil Reeves makes for an interesting contrast. Me, I love to see the differences the rest of the world offers. Buildings older than Canada, those little cultural differences that make those worlds so different from my own. Traveling is a chance to see something different and while I don’t love sightseeing, give me a massive 500-1000 year old building to sit down and stare at and I’m taken care of for hours.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>The biggest smiles of the event were being worn by one man: Tomi Walamies.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Neil, on the other hand, thinks that’s cool and all, but can’t figure out for the life of him why anyone would want to live anywhere else in the world other than the good ol’ USA. America’s the biggest. America’s the best. How could the choice to live anywhere else be anything but just plain dumb? The result of these divergent attitudes was obvious this weekend, as we boated from the airport, past the beautiful buildings, a wild sunset in the background. The players of the Pro Tour could only agree on one thing: we all live to game.

For me, the city was awesome, while the event proved equal. A disappointing Masters dampened the weekend a bit, but the newshound in me was pleased at how easy it was to sniff out the stories for this column. The happenings erupting from Venice were obvious and that makes my task this week an easy one: here’s a look at the weekend’s more colourful moments:

### Osyp

“The first time I played Osyp Lebidowitz”, Dirk Baberowski reminded me Sunday night amid a frozen feast of lasagne and spaghetti “I said he would win a Pro Tour some day”. Baberowski is a confident man and doesn’t make comments like these with reckless abandon. That quote means he saw something special in the man they call Joe Black.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptven03/763.jpg)

Osyp burst onto the scene a year and a half ago at Pro Tour-San Diego, where a Top 16 finish and Top 8 near miss put the world on notice before he took it a step further at Osaka where he made his first Top 8. Since then, the African-American-looking-guy-who-isn’t-an-African-American (he’s Ukranian) had quieted down some, even inspiring his team mates to believe that he might be quitting the game any day leading up to Venice.

Osyp looked like a warrior in the Top 8, especially in his bad deck-on-deck Semifinal match up against William “Baby Huey” Jensen, with a spectacular fifth game proving the decider before Lebedowitz advanced to the final and eventually the championship. Some players just have the look of a winner and Osyp, at times so funny and outgoing (as evidenced in his tournament reports over on www.togit.com. This one should be a doozy), bore down with as focused a glare and desire as I’ve seen on a Top 8 face in a long time.

Osyp’s win is especially remarkable in light of the internal problems Team TOGIT experienced throughout the preparation period of the tournament, problems that also involved CMU and which Joe Crosby will be delving into on this site in the near future. The weekend saw the light at the end of that particular tunnel Friday night, when the two agreed to amalgamate Friday night, forming a massive eighteen person PT mailing list with a casual enough attitude that newly qualified friends may find a place on it as well. I’m moving to Pittsburgh in a week or so. I’ll be on that list.

### Fuller Disqualified

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptven03/974.jpg)

Between round one and round two, the news swept through the room like wildfire: Ryan Fuller had been disqualified. I almost always pump the fist when the judges catch someone on the cheat, especially when it’s someone like Ryan with whom I have a more than slightly checkered past, but the jury’s still out on whether the intent to cheat was there. Fuller purchased 100 sleeves of Japanese design that came in packs of fifty before the beginning of the tournament and sleeved his deck prior to the first match. Round one saw him get a random deck check, to which PT scorekeeper Gord Culp said “Fuller’s never going to believe this was random”, and the judges found that Ryan had fifty spells in sleeves marked one way (including sideboard), one spell and twenty four lands in sleeves marked differently. At the end of the weekend, the jury was still out on whether there was intent here.

Coming in the wake of the Trey Van Cleave (who made an appearance in Venice where he promptly tried to break WotC event rules by selling cards without purchasing a card table) suspension announcement, I think it’s important to wait for due process on this one before jumping to “guilty” or “error prone” judgment without DCI ruling. Yes, Fuller’s past is spotty at best, but he’s just done his time, had told friends he was “going straight” and if that’s the case, it would be unfortunate to see his opportunity for redemption ended by player paranoia. The simple fact is, love him or hate him, the man can play; it would be a shame for the Pro Tour to lose one of its more colorful characters due to conjecture and misunderstanding.

### Return of the Finn

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptven03/811.jpg)

The biggest smiles of the event were being worn by one man: Tomi Walamies. A finalist at this year’s Invitational, “Jelly” (pronounced yee-leh) hadn’t shown up on PT since. Tomi, normally stoic and somewhat expressionless, knew from the eleventh round on that he was Top 8 bound and the man enjoyed it as much as anyone could. His mother was even in attendance to watch her son succeed, a la Mrs. Kibler (mom, you’re going to Yokohama!) and she got to see him survive a mirror match with team mate Mattias Jorstedt (who after such a long wait now has two consecutive Constructed Top 8s. How good is Punisher?) and administer a fast and furious beating to Jordan Berkowitz (who has wanted to see his name in this column for some time now. With an 8-0 start, how can I say no?), before finally losing to Osyp in the finals. Walamies joins Mark Justice, David Mills and Kamiel Cornelissen as the only players with two losses and no wins in PT finals. The heart and soul of Punisher is back and he even told me he should be around for the foreseeable future.

### The New Kai?

I’ve been saying for a long time that Bill Jensen has as much talent as just about anyone in the game. For years, he’s been playing a fast and clean game reminiscent of Kai Budde’s, but that had always been tempered by a set of priorities that left Pro Tour success a little too far down the list to be easily grabbed.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptven03/803.jpg)

Now, a little older and a little wiser than he was in his first PT Top 8 in London ’99, Jensen has gone from an occasional player to a ***Magic** Online* fanatic, doing three-on-three booster drafts a good ten times a week, thanks to the new Limited Games area in Casual Play and that has led to his game reaching a sharpness it’s never seen before. Coming off a Top 8 finish in Chicago, where he lost to the German Juggernaut, Jensen made it back-to-back Sundays with his Semifinals appearance in Venice, making him the first player since Jens Thoren to post consecutive Top 8s in Limited and Constructed.

What’s most noteworthy about Jensen’s revival though is the desire with which he’s playing. At almost the same moment as his 8th place finish in the Swiss was being announced, he was asking if Scott Johns was still the only player with three consecutive Top 8 finishes on the Tour (Johns did it way back when in the Pro Tour’s inaugural season), showing the will and want to become more than just a flash in the pan. Jensen has an appreciation for the history of the game and he now clearly wants to become part of it. Aside from himself, I can’t for the life of me figure out who’s going to stop him.

Osyp stands at around 6’4” and Jensen is a couple inches taller, but the men (and women) on the table were far larger than either this weekend. With Slide proving an issue for every quick creature-based deck, even red decks were forced to resort to the use of larger-than-usual creatures like Avarax, Mencing Ogre and Rorix Bladewing with the hope being that the occasional hit would be a hard one.

With Slice and Dice being uncounterable in an environment that would otherwise have seen elves and wizards seem viable and that it was partnered with Starstorm as a nearly impenetrable combination of effective creature control, the best solution was to run creatures that couldn’t be controlled that way and that meant protection from red (Akroma), regeneration (Silvos) or a mana: toughness ratio the mass kill spells couldn’t race (Silklash Spider, Canopy Crawler, Clickslither). Legends of all varieties saw play, with all five pit fighters and even Kamahl, Fist of Krosa made Day 2. I have to say that made for some really cool gaming moments that the most casual of gamers could appreciate. I’m guessing this format, which is far more diverse than predictions suggested, will make for a great PTQ season.

### Japanese Champions

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptven03/349.jpg)

Japan has long been **Magic’s** second largest market after the United States, but until this weekend, no Japanese team or individual had managed to capture a Premiere Event title. PS2, with last year’s Rookie of the Year Katsuhiro Mori, Masahiko Morita and Masahiro Kuroda finally put an end to that in Venice with their win in a Masters final with a pair of surprise competitors.

Defeating PT-Boston finalists 20/20 in the finals, PS2 avoided a run in with the “greatest team ever”, Phoenix Foundation, as the German squad was defeated by two-time Masters finalists Panzer Hunters, who became the first team ever to defeat Budde, Blume and Baberowski in a sanctioned Team Rochester. 20/20 meanwhile beat Courtney’s Boys (Bob Maher, Neil Reeves and you-know-who) for the third time in two events, after the Boys had taken out Gateway winners Illuminati (Justin Gary, Zvi Mowshowitz and Alex Shvartsman) in the Quarterfinals.

The Japanese have shown signs of integration of late, joining the ranks of Sunday drafters and chatting, albeit in constantly broken English, with the Pro Tour’s Western stars. Perhaps now, when asked to name five Japanese pros, their American counterparts will be able to do so.

### Teams

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptven03/877.jpg"><br><i>Looking for a third.</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

A few Boston teams solidified their standing this weekend, leaving relatively few free agents among gravy train types. Ken Ho finally confirmed he’d be teaming with Brian Kibler and Ben Rubin, leaving Jeff Cunningham and Brett Shears looking for a third. Potentially, it could be highly sought-after Ben Stark, who is also considering an offer from Brian Hegstad and Peter Szigeti. A couple of weeks ago, Jon Finkel confirmed he’d be teaming with Eric Froehlich and Kamiel Cornelissen, the unlikely tandem that teamed with Noah Boeken last year and Kyle Rose the year before that. Boeken, who’s been considering retirement for a time, is still teamless, as are is Gab Tsang and Chris Benafel, who’d been rumoured to be searching for a third for he and Fuller, who’s status is now up in the air. I’ll take a closer look at the team situation as it’s shaping up in the next few weeks.

That’s about it for now. As mentioned, I’m moving to Pittsburgh this week, likely on the 1st of April in order to start playing live again. As much as I enjoy ***Magic** Online*, it’s made my game lazy and it’s been showing in my play of late, so the job calls for a change of locales. Hopefully the move goes smoothly and proves beneficial, but regardless of the results in the standings, I know I’m looking at good times in the months to come. If the move goes according to plan, there’ll be another installment next week, but this is a precautionary measure warning of the possibility there won’t be in case the U.S. government stalls my entry. Better to err on the side of caution.

Have a good week.
