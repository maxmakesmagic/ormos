A few weeks ago now, I ventured here to England to renew myself, both as gamer and person. Gone was the fast food, might makes right, capitalist North America that I'd been accustomed to, as I looked forward to the quiet, quaint world that lay across the ocean in England. I finally made my journey on the twentieth of September, vowing not to look back, at least for a little while.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>As I started playing on a daily basis, I started to rediscover what had drawn me to the game</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

When I arrived, it was to a different England than the one I expected, the one we see in the movies where everyone is charming and polite, drinking their tea as they form their small niches in the lives of assorted Hugh Grant-portrayed characters. The one I found was instead a little grimy and a lot more fun, with bars and pubs the coffee houses of the isle, where beer is as abundant as air (and is inhaled as such) and the beef isn't as bad as we'd all like to think.

The renewal of Gary Wise, the gamer, was to see me refocus myself. For the last couple of years, I just haven't been playing **Magic** as much as a self-proclaimed professional should, and it was showing in my results, with not just my numbers, but also my play really suffering for it. I was making stupid mistakes that I shouldn't have been with alarming frequency, and it wasn't for lack of an understanding of the game's strategies, it was just sloppiness, though my tactical game was slipping a little also. In short, it was time to get in gear or quit. I chose the former.

An interesting thing happened when I arrived. As I started playing on a daily basis (I've played every day I've been here), I started to rediscover what had drawn me to the game: I was having a good time, both through the search for the answers to the questions *Odyssey* Limited and Extended offered and the act of playing itself. I get my serious competitive fix by playing against my teammate Ben Ronaldson, then play for fun against his brothers Ivan and Luke. I can't get enough right now, and I couldn't be happier about it. **Magic** really is fun again.

With this renewed, broader attitude, I thought this week we might take a look as some of **Magic's** fun formats and how they might help you in the quest to improve your game. The Invitational offered a look at a number of them as it's looked at others in the past, and there are technical merits to be found in their practice. Furthermore, trying these formats out seems like a good excuse to get back to the grass roots, so I recommend giving each one a shot.

### Rotisserie Draft

![](https://i.ormos.uk/32/0a/320aec9383da4a6cd9998e0891532395357798cf8b3ebd5f43a0eed74023a752.jpg)The real inspiration for this week's column, this format, used at the Invitational this month, was originally used at last year's Texas Invitational, a tournament that sees that state's finest compete against one another in a number of fun formats. The concept of Rotisserie draft is simple: you take an entire standalone set like *Odyssey*, lay it out on a table and draft it with eight people in the Rochester style, going from seat one through eight, then eight through one and keep repeating the process. 

What I really like about Rotisserie draft is the fact it allows you to play with obscure rares early in a format that you would otherwise not get many chances to draft. With fully one third of the cards being rare, everyone is bound to have a number in their decks, with each player having an equal opportunity to have power cards. It seems to be an especially valid exercise with *Odyssey*, where the top eight or nine cards are very close to one another in strength.

In addition to teaching you about how rares interact with one another, Rotisserie draft will also help you learn about Rochester draft and manuvering yourself in such a way that you have an advantageous seat in a basic way. While in Rochester you have to concern yourself with the colors those drafters around you are drafting, in Rotisserie, the only real issue is noticing how heavily each color is being drafted and taking advantage in kind. If you can get a set together, find yourself seven friends to try a Rotisserie draft with. You'll have a lot of fun with the power cards and learn a lot more than you expect.

### Five Color

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/MI01/947.jpg)How many unsanctioned formats have their own web pages? [www.5-color.com](https://web.archive.org/web/20011017000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.5-color.com&origin=sideboard_ww_20011017), run by the format's creator Kurtis Hahn goes in depth when looking at deck lists and strategies for the format, but basically, this is a Constructed format where you must use at least 18 cards from each of the five colors, in a deck numbering at least 250 cards in all. 

This format really takes you back to the game's early days, with ante cards allowed along with the Type 1 power cards. You don't need to spend thousands of dollars on your deck though, as you play for ante, meaning that using valuable cards risks your losing valuable cards, so the all-common creature deck is not an unreal strategy to make your own.

Five does offer itself as a tutorial in the art of deck building, the specialty it allows you to study being the art of fine-tuning your deck. Think about it, if you can get your 250-card deck to be just right, imagine how easy it should be to get your 60-card selections down pat. Some of the world's best deck builders, including Brian Kibler, Brian Kowal and Adrian Sullivan have been known to tinker with the format, and if they're learning from it, so can the rest of us.

### Solomon Draft

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/38/1b/381b73ad207922372b69ba27a61ea286a3934cd8d159e20c014aec026805566c.jpg"><br><i>Fact or Fiction meets drafting.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

I've heard more than one player refer to Solomon Draft as the most skill intensive format in **Magic**, and they may not be too far off. A two player draft format, in Solomon, the two players open three boosters a piece and then shuffle all ninety cards together, making ten packs of nine cards each. Player A takes the first pack and divides it into two piles, balancing them as well as possible, with player B then selecting one of the piles. Then, player B does the dividing and player A makes the choice. When all the packs are drafted, you make a deck out of the cards you've got.

The real value of Solomon draft can be found in the way it forces you to precisely evaluate card quality, trying to find a balance in the two piles. If you don't know the card's strengths, you're going to get slaughtered, and you have to reevaluate the cards with each new pack you see. When the drafting is done, you know every card your opponent has drafted and have to be able to account for them in the course of a game.

The one other skill the format will really enforce is the balancing of your colors. With such a small card pool and both players fighting for quality decks, you'll often find yourself having to play up to four colors. The mana balance in Solomon decks tends to be very delicate as a result, so you'll really be forced to learn the math behind land quantities in Limited decks.

### Duplicate Sealed

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>The idea here is that the player who builds their deck best will have the inside track.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

The staple format of the Invitational, this is a favorite of mine as it really rewards strong deck building. As the name suggests, this format is one where each player receives a sealed deck with the exact same pool of cards as everyone else, with the five colors as balanced as is possible. The idea here is that the player who builds their deck best will have the inside track, but unlike regular sealed deck, one player receiving a superior card pool to those of their opponents doesn't tip the balance of the format.

Duplicate Sealed is going to teach you the ins and outs of sealed deck construction to a fault, with the slightest mistake echoed in the construction of the decks played by your opponents. The one real difficulty is the accumulation of the cards needed for the card pool in the first place, but you can find some suitable lists in the Sideboard's coverage of past Invitationals.

After you finish the building and playing of your deck, don't be afraid to take a look at the differences between your deck and those played by others and discuss your choices. I know that I've made mistakes in each of the Duplicate sealed events I've participated in and listening to others has helped teach me the error of my ways. Such discussion is always helpful if you're open-minded enough to take advice from others.

### Multiplayer

I know Alongi has been waiting for this one. More casual than the other formats listed here, multiplayer is, as suggested, a wide-open format where more than two players try to fend off all comers in the hopes of being the last man standing when all is said and done. The most popular ways to work it are either a free-for all melee or an attack to the left dictum, and I prefer the former thanks to one particular skill it really forces you to use to your advantage: Politicking.

In Rochester Draft, politicking is an important skill that will allow you to befriend drafters and know when its time to betray that friendship in a way that is most advantageous. Multiplayer exploits and develops this skill in a much more pronounced way, with player A and player B teaming up to ruin player C until player B sees an opportunity to wreck player A.

Obviously, the politics of Rochester draft are much more subtle, especially with communication outside of card selections not being allowed, but I think you'll find that as you master the politics of Multiplayer, you'll start to understand Rochester a little better. As a bonus, you'll probably have a lot more fun than you would in a stoic, one on one match. 

### Mental Magic

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/1f/b0/1fb00bad990ac7a9d26a17759eab88251f1d18b27cc86bae5eb18b7977bc6e42.jpg"><br><i>Nobody ever expects this cantrip Force Spike.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

As I just suck at this format, I can really appreciate just how hard it is. Mental **Magic** sees players use a pile of random, non-land cards, with each card offering the option of being played as a painless City of Brass or its use as a spell. One twist is that you may not use the card as it actually is, but instead can use it as any other spell of the same casting cost. For example, Counterspell can be used as Boomerang, and vice versa, but neither card can be used as itself. A second twist is that the players may only name each spell (not each card) once, its use eliminating it from future use.

Mental **Magic** as a teaching tool enforces three important strategic ideals. The first is environmental knowledge, which is extraordinarily important here, with each card providing dozens of possibilities and forcing you to account for all of them. The second is play selection, with those dozens of card options each providing dozens of utilization possibilities. The third is card advantage, with the winner more often than not utilizing each card many times, casting spells with buyback, cantrips and card drawers.

A very difficult format to be very good at, Mental **Magic** will force you to really know the ins and outs of the game, causing you to learn all the cards, not just the most recent ones. You'll marvel at the uses you'll find for assorted cards as you realize how strong they can be in certain situations.

### Mini Master

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/odyssey_booster.jpg)Just an afterthought here, this is an easy format to play: take a fifteen-card pack, shuffle in two of each basic land type and play without looking at the cards. Mini Master will force you to utilize even the worst cards in trying to find a way to win, as well as teach you the importance of deck counts as decking becomes a very important factor. This is a great way to open a pack, actually learning a little something while allowing the tension of not knowing what rare you've opened mounts.

Give these formats a try and remember to have fun with them as you develop some of the game's more important skills. That's all for this week. Have a good one.
