I’ve wanted to write this article for a while. “But Gary” you ask, “isn’t Wise Words your column? Don’t you get to choose the topic matter? Don’t you have final say?” Yes, gentle reader; all of these things are true. It IS my column, I DO choose the topics and I COULD arguably stake claim to final say (arguably) within this hallowed hunting ground, but this is a Wise Words I was ill-prepared to complete until a long discussion with Jin Okamoto\* and Japanese judge Yoshiya Shindo\*\* provided me the information I needed to do so.

Japan and its people are a mystery to those of us not living on the isle of the rising sun, and I’m not just talking Magic-wise. The honor code, caste systems, martial arts, sumo, geishas, kimonos and Zabuton combine to create a society that seems very alien to the rest of us, and it is the natural instinct of the human animal to fear the unknown. Maybe, that’s why the rest of us have been so ignorant about the players of Japan for so long.

It never fails. Ask any North American pro to name five of their Japanese counterparts and you’ll get a rambling diatribe like this:

> *“Um… I dunno… there’s that guy with the funny hats… Satoshi! Yeah, Satoshi… and there’s Fujita, that’s two… Fujita’s first name? Rakishi or something… anyways, there’s the fast guy, Katsuhimoito Maki and um… I dunno, shut up.”*

…at which point they start running together random syllables in an attempt to create Japanese names. It’s a clash of cultures, and of ideology that makes us so different, and those dissimilarities may be responsible for our love affair with Japanese culture and our tendency to put down the unfamiliar. Of course, when it comes to the Japanese pros who spend their time and money to make their way to the Pro Tour, it’s the similarities which have, at least of late, started to bridge the gap. In the end, Japanese, European, American, Canadian… we’re all gamers.

The most interesting thing I found out while talking to Jin was that like North America, Japan is fragmented. There’s no American team; there’s YMG, CMU-TOGIT and Supernova, but despite this, when we look at Japan, the question asked is inevitably “what are the Japanese playing?” as if the entire 120 million person population got together and agreed upon one deck, sending it through a couple of levels of government in order to gain national approval on their card selections. Shocking that they divide and fragment in order to top one another just like… well… everyone else. I guess this fact wasn’t as interesting as was my failure to recognize the simplicity of the truth before it was explained to me. To sum up, I’m as dumb as everyone else. Maybe more so, after all, I actually know these guys.

So, now that we’ve figured out that the Japanese are actually human, going through the pointless rituals of eating and breathing like the rest of us do, my hope with this week’s Wise Words is to educate both you and I as to who’s who and who’s with who in the higher levels of the competitive Japanese community. With PS2’s win at the Venice Masters and the Top 4 finishes enjoyed by Masashi Ooiso and Tsuyoshi Ikeda in Yokohama, Japanese players seem to have finally joined the fray, where in the past they were mere observers. I think that means its time to look at these guys with the respect due anyone that’s risen to the level they have.

**Akira Asahara** –A Tokyo native, Asihara may not be too well known outside of his homeland, but there he’s treated like the god of deck building. Finishing 12th at PT-Houston this year with an Aluren deck of his own design, Akira did that performance one better with his win at the Limited GP-Kyoto this year. A member of Team Hachiogi, Asihara often trades Constructed information with Jin Okamoto.

**Kenichi Fujita** – Known throughout Japan as “the bad guy”, Fujita inherited his nickname due to his constantly appearing to be in a bad mood. Japan’s first Limited specialist, Fujita has been on Pro Tour for half a decade now. A long time teammate of Itaru Ishida and Koichiro Maki, Kenichi’s strongest PT finish came at 2000 PT-LA, where a final round loss to Satoshi Nakamura cost him his, and Japan’s, first Top 8 finish. 

**Osamu Fujita** – Another long time PT denizen, Osamu is known throughout Japan for his insatiable Magic appetite. The man cannot get enough. It doesn’t matter who you are or what you’re playing for, if your team needs a third or your draft an eighth, this Kyoto native is the man to call, because it seems like regardless of location, this Fujita will make the trip.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTTOK01/654.jpg)

**Tsuyoshi Fujita** – Probably Japan’s most famous Magic player now, it was Tsuyoshi who became the first Japanese player to reach a Pro Tour Top 8, making it all the way to the finals at 2001 PT-Tokyo. As important as that footnote in history is, Tsuyoshi’s more important contribution may be his efforts in the social arena, where his demeanor and approachability at the 2001 Invitational allowed a number of top pros to get to know a Japanese player for the first time. Primarily a Constructed force, Tsuyoshi is referred to by Okamoto as Japan’s best tournament player, and his numbers hardly disagree. Hailing from Kyoto, Tsuyoshi does a large portion of his extensive playtesting with Masahiko Morita and the other members of PS2.

**Takao Higaki** – If the Hiroshima players have a leader, it’s this man, the Japanese king of the near miss. Twice, Higaki has clawed his way to finals in Japan’s exclusive end of year tournament only finish on the wrong end of that final match up. 

**Tsuyoshi Ikeda** – Owner of a PT Semifinal appearance courtesy of Yokohama, Ikeda’s been on tour for over four years now. Before that tournament, Ikeda’s best finish was sixteenth at PT-Osaka, suggesting this is a player hindered more by jetlag than inadequacies in his game. A card shop owner, like many of his ilk, Ikeda plays everything from Magic to tic tac toe, which he may well be doing soon with his newborn child.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/mi02/950.jpg)

**Itaru Ishida** – Tokyo’s silent assassin, Ishida has been on the Pro Tour almost as long as there’s been one, and shows no signs of slowing down. Becoming famous for his successes early on - while still a teenager - throughout the Japanese game community, Ishida manages to crack PT Top 16s, but never the Top 8 barrier. Winner of the 2001 end of year tournament, while his skills as an individual are obviously impressive, it’s in the team arena that he does his best work, doing the brunt of the drafting for the Panzer Hunters, who have made two straight Masters finals and ended Phoenix Foundation’s team Rochester streak in Venice. One could definitely argue that this Tokyo native is Japan’s finest player, with most counter arguments coming from the Okamoto and Tsuyoshi Fujita camps.

**Akahiro Kashima** – When Kashima made Top 8 at PT-Venice this year, the Japanese players as a whole were more stunned than anything else because frankly, they had no idea who this guy was! Working by himself and bringing a deck of his own design to his first Pro Tour, Kashima managed to make it to the Quarterfinals before losing to eventual winner Osyp Lebidowicz. An inhabitant of Satoshi Nakamura’s offices, Kashima is slowly becoming better known to his countrymen, but whether he can duplicate his Venice success is as big a question to them as to the rest of the world. 

**Masahiro Kuroda** – Along with PS2 teammates Masahiko Morita and Katsuhiro Mori, Kuroda hold the distinction of being the first Japanese player to win a truly international Premiere-level event. A Constructed specialist, Kuroda’s Magic time is split between the table and the computer terminal, where he does a fair amount of work for Sideboard Japan. A former national team member, Kuroda hails from Tokyo.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gputs02/970.jpg)

**Koichiro Maki** – If you want proof that not all Japanese players fit the same mold, here it is. Maki, a one time Invitational invitee, may be as well known for the skull rings he wears around his fingers as for his play, but as a six year veteran of the Pro Tour wars, both probably deserve equal recognition. More of a Limited player than Constructed, Maki was once a member of team Skull, but now affiliates himself with Kenichi Fujita and Itaru Ishida in the comfort of the Tokyo games center. Oh yeah, and I once cast Equilibrium against him at a PT when he had Rathi Dragon.

**Katsuhiro Mori** – Mention this name and a quiet awe surrounds the Japanese crowd, which one can understand once they’ve seen him play. Frenetic, a blur, blinding… there are a thousand ways to describe Mori, but the most suitable is likely *fast*. Turn after turn, this guy draws, plays a land, casts a spell or two, attacks and passes priority in the space of a few seconds, almost always making the right play in the process, and while he may not be the most popular player in Japan (due to the attitude that must inevitably come with talent), ask his opponents and they’ll tell you he’s a prodigy, and that no player in the world save Kai Budde should be as feared. Rookie of the Year in 2001, Mori’s payout of course came in Venice.

**Masahiko Morita** – Despite Mori’s obvious strength, it’s Morita who many refer to as PS2’s strongest player. Called “the pure player” by many in Japan, Morita is as identifiable for his crazy hairdos as for his strong play, which has resulted in his making a number of GP finals. After PS2 won the Masters, Morita took the money and ran all of about three feet before he started throwing it around, to the point where by the time Yokohama had come around, his $20,000 share was spent with little to show for it other than good times. One might think his plan involved eventually getting more.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTTOK01/907.jpg)

**Satoshi Nakamura** – The living legend of Japanese Magic, Satoshi is the bridge between the old and new guards. A fantasy writer known for his crazy hats, “fu man chu” facial hair and colorful ice cream erasers, Satoshi’s popularity in Japan became so pronounced that his likeness was used to represent the Duel Master in a Japanese Magic comic. With multiple Top 16s and masters to his credit, Satoshi’s career is undoubtedly the most complete of any Japanese player to date. 

**Shuhei Nakamura** – “Shimi Shu”, the winner of the 2002 end of year tournament, is best known in Japan for his tendency to bring aspects to the game that may seems alien to his Japanese counterparts. This Japanese funny man bluffs and jokes his way through tournament fields that usually ignore such aspects of tournament Magic. 

**Jun Nobushita** – No player received higher praise from Okamoto than Nobushita, the man Jin beat to win the last APAC Championships. Each community has a few players that provide the foundation and Nobushita is that to Japan, a very giving player who helps his teammates in any way possible. Okamoto credits Nobushita with much of his success, and the two are now teammates as a result of their symbiotic relationship.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/pthou02/967.jpg)

**Jin Okamoto** – Ask the players of Japan which one player is the best in the country, and many will name this man, the eerily silent former rice house waiter turned Magic pro, who can’t win at home. Time and again, Jin has made his way to Japanese tournaments and failed to succeed in them, but put him on a plane and he turns to gold, earning the victory at the 2001 APAC Championships and a 9th place finish at 2001 PT-San Diego. Jin’s silence is explained by friends who suggest he’d rather let the cards do the talking. A regular Sideboard Japan contributor, Jin is now playing full-time.

**Masashi Ooiso** – The new kid on the block, Ooiso have having a very solid year, leading the Rookie of the Year race by five points, before exploding for a 2nd place finish at Yokohama, thus turning his into one of the finest rookie seasons of all time. Ooiso is a Limited specialist who has been playing with Takeo Higaki in Hiroshima over the last couple of years, and it’s now paying dividends: in addition to his Yokohama finish, he came 59th at Venice 3rd at GP Kyoto, 4th at GP Utsunomiya and 26th at PT-Boston this year. 

…and that’s about it for this week. Before I go, a quick apology over the lack of last week’s column, but cabin fever got the best of me. After a week of being locked in, I just had to get out. Hope all is forgiven. Have a good week.

\* - Who speaks absolutely no English mind you. The conversation was a little exasperating for both of us as my Japanese is “ru-sty”, a Japanese word meaning non-existent.

\*\* - Who thankfully speaks English.
