![](http://wizards.com/regionals/images/regionals.gif)Last week, in [Win One for Texas!](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/04-11-win-one-for-texas), we presented a challenge. In a fairly open field with the largely untested cards from *Torment* (untested in Standard, that is), what is the best way to attack the metagame using the AustiKnight principles set forth by Macey and Ho?

*What are the best new cards and mechanics to play from Torment?*

*How will we prepare for parallel innovations from other players who know the same rules that we do?*

For inspiration, we looked at one of the best ever Texas-rules decks, Brian Kibler's The Red Zone. It should be no surprise, therefore, that the "correct" answer to our puzzle in the light of U.S. Regionals 2002 should also be Kibler-inspired:

![](https://i.ormos.uk/fc/d3/fcd303e216c832a96e5657bf356203e657af86193465daa8f87aac71517049b4.jpg)The New York contingent of our playtest group considered only two decks for U.S. Regionals 2002. Of that New York portion (myself, Paul Jordan, Don Lim, and Josh Ravitz), Don continued his love affair with the excellent ZevAtog, while the rest of us played Kibler's Rug. Those of us who did not play ZevAtog played Rug because it was literally our only deck in testing that did not lose to ZevAtog in game one (Rug had a very slim game one advantage); Don and Josh qualified for Nationals by finishing in the Top 8, Paul finished a strong (but not strong enough) 5-2, and I, out of a possible 354 pairings, met Josh in a 75-card mirror match in round 1, and could not win out the next 8 rounds (or even the next one round)... and instead studied at the foot of Mowshowitz while my list-mates tore up the Swiss.

The main deck was played to a card as it was given to us by Dragonmaster Kibler, but the sideboard was designed by John Shuler and myself. I will get to the sideboard after I explain just why the Rug is such a devastatingly powerful deck in the main, as well as why it answers the AustiKnight questions for new formats.

The answer to our first question (What are the best new cards and mechanics to play from *Torment*?) can only be to break black or to break madness. The problem with trying to break black is that the best *Torment* black cards are at odds with the prime rules of Regionals. 

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>At Regionals, you should always play a proactive deck.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Even if you decide to play control at Regionals (for example you have Napster or Replenish at hand in the Spring of 2000), you should still play a deck capable of overwhelming tempo and/or high damage potential (like Napster's turn-1 Phyrexian Negator or Replenish's instant team of 3/3 and 4/4 monsters). At Regionals, you should always play a proactive deck. You have no idea what specific decks you will play against every round, so it is next to impossible to figure out all the possible holes to plug in a control deck's strategy. The best way to plug a hole overall is to just win the game, which increases the incentive to tempo and proactive threats at this kind of tournament. This is why, though they are both "control" decks playing one non-blue color, ZevAtog is a much sounder choice for Regionals than u-w Millstone.

*Torment's* black cards, while powerful, only fit into either a Braids deck or a dedicated board control deck. We dismissed Braids because of its unpredictability in the mirror match, and more importantly, because as we got better with the other decks, Braids won fewer and fewer test games. For black, that meant analyzing black control.

If the opponent is playing Domain, playing an end-of-turn Prophetic Bolt deck, or even just playing Millstone, the main deck of a black control design gets less and less attractive. If it is a local tournament and you can script what everyone will play by observing what they do play every week - and what you script is g-r fatty deck after g-r fatty deck - black control obviously becomes very good... but if someone decides to play Braids (and Braids dominated the Grudge Match just weeks before the Northeast Regionals), black control becomes a non-option. Consider again and again for decks based on enchantments, decks with a non-PsychatogUpheaval plan, or decks with something as simple as Circle of Protection: Black after sideboarding, and then cross that with nine rounds, and you will see why we dismissed this deck as well.

So we were left with breaking madness. Luckily it was already broken.

Madness is a four-pronged mechanic. The most obvious way to exploit madness is card advantage (Merfolk Looter and Fiery Temper, or Compulsion and Obsessive Search, for instance); the next most obvious is its simple undercosted nature (Arrogant Wurm or Basking Rootwalla with Wild Mongrel playing mana accelerator). Most madness decks only make explicit use of two or three of the mechanic's advantages; Kibler's Rug makes use of all four. Additionally, they are:

### Redundant Tempo

![](https://i.ormos.uk/5c/90/5c90d93173a2203519d1e580204bee7e1f35c36846100b6947f08f302b2004eb.jpg)The Kibler deck controls tempo better than any deck in the format other than ZevAtog. While ZevAtog prevents the opponent from generating tempo with Standstill and reverses tempo on the ground with Psychatog and its 8 bounce spells, the Kibler deck apes a standard g-r deck with the potential for broken openings. Like any random g-r deck, it can play a Birds of Paradise into Call of the Herd on turn 2. Unlike all but Frog in a Blender, Rug can also play for an extremely fast kill (as fast as turn 4), by just drawing a Wild Mongrel and the burn spells.

If this were all that the Rug could do, it would in fact be a weaker tempo deck than a standard g-r design because it has fewer turn-1 mana accelerators than Fat Crank for Call acceleration, and fewer Fiery Tempers than Frog in a Blender for the turn 4 "dump my hand" kill.

Like Fat Crank, the Rug also plays four copies of the single best tempo card in the format: Flametongue Kavu. Because of its Fact or Fictions and Merfolk Looters, it plays like it has many more Flametongue Kavus than Fat Crank. This allows Rug in the midgame to play its most powerful proactive tempo cards with greater regularity than the typical g-r decks.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/e6/63/e6632160b19524e012f85bdedfa2e88a5cdaf93852c1aec52d72bcf04b4210b2.jpg"><br><i>Flametongue Kavus 5-8</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Interestingly, Rug can also play the defensive tempo game. No beatdown player should ever run his army into a Merfolk Looter or Wild Mongrel if the Rug player has 3 mana open... unless his specific plan is to lose his best creature. Much like the bounce suite of ZevAtog, Rug steals an opposing deck's tempo on the ground with just the threat of Arrogant Wurm.

And yet players choose to swing into Arrogant Wurm.

If we allot for this kind of strategy (and trust me, it happens over and over), Rug plays with 8 4-power Nekrataals... Arrogant Wurm joins Flametongue Kavu as an excellent tempo card.

### The Counter-Rebel Plan

![](https://i.ormos.uk/38/1b/381b73ad207922372b69ba27a61ea286a3934cd8d159e20c014aec026805566c.jpg)Just as Rug can take control of the board like a g-r deck at times, it can also play Counter-Rebels against a control deck. Rug can play straight aggro-control by getting down Wild Mongrel or Call of the Herd and then protecting it with one of the 4 copies of Circular Logic (or, as Scott McCord says, "Dismiss"), or it can play Wild Mongrel or Merfolk Looter and run test spells against a control deck's sanction at the end of the opponent's turn. Because Rug has a natural advantage on the board against most control decks because of its low curve, it can often force the opposition into action with end step Fact or Fiction and Arrogant Wurm, or just hassle at the life total with Fiery Temper and Violent Eruption. 

Should Rug have any kind of clock in play, the Counter-Rebel plan becomes extremely annoying extremely quickly. 

If you assume that ZevAtog's long game plan every single duel against a creature deck is to go for the Wrath-Geddon-Hatred kill, then a fight involving a 1-mana Circular Logic against a 6-mana Upheaval on the opponent's main phase begins to look extremely attractive for Rug. It may not do it quite all the time, but unlike most aggressive decks, Rug at least has a plan for maintaining its tempo on the board against the single best mainstream deck in Standard.

We ended up playing the Rug for three reasons. The first was that it made excellent use of madness. In fact, in answer to the Ho question of how we would deal with the fact that other people would be harnessing madness, our response is that the Rug harnesses it more efficiently. While Basking Rootwalla is one of the few ways to beat ZevAtog, it is a non-card against the Rug... card advantage sometimes, but little better than Obsessive Search unless Rug is already under significant pressure. Kibler chose only the most powerful madness spells to play, and we supplemented the sideboard with cards that exploit the vulnerabilities (Roar of the Wurm and Upheaval specifically) of other decks with the Rug's philosophy. While Rug plays only three Fact or Fiction, that is still two more than most decks of this style; our Flametongues and burn spells would wreak havoc on straight u-g decks while our Merfolk Looters gave us twice as many ways to pitch Violent Eruption than Rug's pure g-r brethren.

The second reason was that Rug played both of the best cards in the format: Flametongue Kavu (both the best tempo card and arguably the best creature) and Fact or Fiction (probably the best spell overall). Why not play the best cards?

![](https://i.ormos.uk/21/f1/21f13577e08dfabe36fd570de5c856a0b3774e0041f5772454e5377bcc26861c.jpg)The third reason is that the deck had good matchups across the environment.

I mentioned before that Rug was the only deck we considered that had a positive win percentage against ZevAtog (and Don chose to go with ZevAtog himself, with great success). Our other aggressive decks would get 18 points in and then find themselves with a hand full of expensive spells, staring down Psychatog with no permanents in play. Our decks that could beat ZevAtog could not beat anything else. Rug, on the other hand, was brilliant against Braids with its Violent Eruptions and diverse paths to tempo and extra cards. We found over and over that any opponent who did not destroy Merfolk Looter immediately would just lose.

The sideboard was particularly good.

*4 Compost:*  
 You have by now read more than enough about Compost.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/ef/3a/ef3aacde0c53dc2658bceb93cdb3b8742c6761fa3c2997859c71bf0927ede392.jpg)*3 Roar of the Wurm:*  
 Our most precarious situation seemed to be against beatdown, where Rug's mana base could stab an otherwise very powerful deck in the back. Against beatdown, Fact or Fiction in the 4-slot was not exciting. Despite the fact that we could create nearly impossible splits due to the overwhelming tempo and synergy of the spells, Fact or Fiction itself would do nothing about a disadvantaged board position and incoming damage sources. Roar of the Wurm was a pure swap for Fact or Fiction against g-r. Like Fact or Fiction it was an almost guaranteed two cards, and was sometimes four cards. Unlike Fact or Fiction, we would not generally have to take a point to cast it, and it had a dramatic effect on the tempo on the board.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/f3/58/f358979a53a9451b892269b50c7efcf00cc367fb5f63cff55c8cc5f88bcd460c.jpg)*4 Gainsay:*  
 In Kibler's sideboard, these were Disrupts, which were also good vs. black control. Our contention was that we would beat ZevAtog if they could not resolve Upheaval, so why not just double the cards that could stop Upheaval? We did not consider black control to be a real deck, and the fact that this card also had utility against Psychatog and Fact or Fiction made it our choice.

*3 Upheaval:*  
 Shuler's primary contribution to the sideboard, this card replaced Kibler's 2 Epicenter. The pedigree of Upheaval over Epicenter was threefold:

a. Upheaval joins Flametongue Kavu (best tempo, best creature) and Fact or Fiction (best spell) as the third best spell of the format, being best board control. Why not play all the best cards?

b. At Regionals, you may play against something you did not expect. You will note that we have no Hull Breaches, Tranquilities, Simplifies, Druid Lyrists, or Stone Rains. Basically, if it ain't a creature, it ain't goin' anywhere. Upheaval solves this potential problem, getting rid of Ensnaring Bridges, Teferi's Moats, Collective Restraints, and other non-creature permanents with equal prejudice.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/05/14/0514938b416bd98a5cb7450a0bf5d98d1ce3ba6529ebd74435424c90e4cc1d72.jpg)c. Unlike Epicenter, which also lets Rug take complete control of the board with just one spell, Upheaval also loads up the deck for an instant kill. While Rug is not quite the Wrath-Geddon-Hatred machine that ZevAtog is, an Upheaval will nevertheless always ensure that, provided you have him, Wild Mongrel is swinging for 10 or more the next turn! This is extremely relevant against black control... while they still have the opportunity to cast Innocent Blood, they have only one turn to respond before getting smacked for the probable kill.

*1 Persuasion:*  
 A week before Regionals, this was Roar of the Wurm #4. Persuasion was kind of like Roar of the Wurm, being a card advantage spell that would imbalance the board position in our favor, but had a unique feature against "problem" permanents.

The main problem permanents seemed to be Mortivore and Spiritmonger. The problem was that we would have tons and tons of tempo and card advantage against decks with these cards, but then they could play some huge, unkillable, creature and we could no longer attack without both making their guy better and our army smaller. Bringing Upheaval in against a deck like b-g good stuff or Braids seemed silly. They would invariably be behind on the board, so why reset it when we would be winning so drastically, but for the one huge creature they just played? The answer was to just take that creature and keep swinging with card advantage 4-power men like Flametongue Kavu and Arrogant Wurm. This is a good plan, and works well against other creatures you don't want to kill with red removal as well, like Shambling Swarm.

### The Chinks in the Armor:

As strong a deck as Rug is, it has a lot of problems. As I said before, problems are generally more acceptable in decks with tempo advantage and high damage potential, because you can often kill the opponent before he can exploit the problems. Nevertheless, here are some of the bigger ones:

*Regenerators:*  
 While Mortivore and Spiritmonger can be answered only by racing in game one, even Spectral Lynx is annoying. We can't kill him unless the opponent misplays, and he blocks a good number of the threat cards well.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/49/96/4996b657b30ecacff0c75dce95da832a1220d13fdd57272918be747c553f7fdd.jpg)*Pernicious Deed:* You had better hope the opponent didn't just lead with Duress, because if Deed resolves, it will single-handedly ruin the Rug. 

*Mana Base:*  
 This is a land mix than only the Dragonmaster could love. It is good that so many of the Rug's matchups are blowouts, because even a single mistake in land tapping can - and will - cost you a match in a close game (trust me). There are so many pain lands. All the blue is pain. The best thing to do with that City of Brass is to pitch it to Merfolk Looter.

*Reliance on Madness:*  
 An opponent who counters or kills your Merfolk Looter and Wild Mongrel will buy himself a ton of time. Without good ways to pitch your madness spells, this deck's curve is extremely high and has very difficult color requirements that even the disastrous Kibler land base will not always answer to satisfaction.

All of that being said, Rug is one of the most powerful decks in the current Standard. Though some of its problem cards come in groups, they don't often come in late rounds. As long as Rug is good to you in the early rounds, you can thank your ZevAtog friends from keeping the Spiritmonger decks and their annoying Pernicious Deeds away from the low numbered tables.

Have fun attacking with huge, 2-for-1, monsters!
