Greetings and welcome to the new incarnation of the Wizards ***Magic** Online* column, now on Wednesdays! As you probably know by now, Bennie Smith had to pass on the baton and I will be the one who picks it up. Bennie had been saying goodbye for a few weeks, but had never told you what was going to change, nor even who was going to take over this column or what the new angle was going to be. Those Wizards people like their surprises. I will tell you what's going to be different under my administration soon but first, a little about who I am. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/worlds05/f_karsten.jpg)I am 22 years old and I live in Eindhoven, the Netherlands. Currently, I am finishing a Masters studies in Operations Management and Logistics, but I tend to plan my life around tournaments for the game we all love: **Magic: The Gathering**. I started playing the game roughly eight years ago. A long time friend, Stan van der Velden, taught me the game and when I discovered how much fun **Magic** was, I was hooked. We made sure the people around us got into the game as well and then I happily played as a casual player for one or two years. Eventually, I caught on to the tournament scene. Starting with a monthly tournament in my home town, I then proceeded to attend Grand Prix Trials and Pro Tour Qualifiers farther away. I improved my game, won more and more, and slowly but steadily I went from a casual player to a consistent tournament player. Soon enough, I qualified for my National Championships, made it on the team, finished in the money at my first Pro Tour, then did well at the Grand Prix circuit in the subsequent year and all of a sudden I had 20 Pro Tour Points. 

I have been on the gravy train ever since, but my breakout year was last year. I made the quarterfinals of Pro Tour--Nagoya 2005 and then finished in second place at the 2005 World Championships. I've made quite a few close friends though this game all over the world, I love the Pro Tour lifestyle, and most importantly: I still enjoy the game very much, even after all those years, so I'm pretty sure I'll keep on playing for a long time. 

So why am *I* going to write the new incarnation of the ***Magic** Online* column and not someone else? That traces back to Scott Johns and Ted Knutson, the guys-in-chief on this site, who had thought of a new idea and figured that I would be the perfect candidate. This is mainly because the new idea for this column was in line with the regular strategy writing I did for TCGPlayer.com (or Brainburst, as some people know it, and for those of you wondering, I will keep on writing articles there in addition to this column). And when they asked me, well, I could not say no the honor of writing for the official site.

By now you are probably anxious to hear what new direction I'm supposed to take this column in, so I won't keep you waiting anymore. I am going to address a somewhat different reader crowd than Bennie did, as I will mainly focus on the competitive Constructed aspect of ***Magic** Online*, rather than on the casual side or on the “feel” of ***Magic** Online*. I am more of a competitive player than a casual player and when I play in a tournament, my main goal is to win! I also like to express myself creatively by building and tweaking innovative decks and this column gives me full license to try everything, which I'm really looking forward to. In this column, I intend to focus on describing the ***Magic** Online* Standard metagame, that is, the change in popularity of certain decks with the passage of time and tournaments. Every week, I will give you a rundown of the decks from the previous week's events, what did well, who's playing what, any new cards or decks, etc. From there, I will use that information to do some analysis and suggest what you should consider playing based on what I saw out there. I will focus mostly on Standard, but occasionally I will delve into other competitive formats, such as Extended or Block Constructed, as well. And I will even go back to my roots and cover the casual formats from time to time. So, fans of formats particular to ***Magic** Online* (such as Prismatic, Vanguard, Singleton and Tribal) will also get some love from time to time, and I believe you'll also be seeing more of those kinds of formats in the columns that deal with those audiences now that Into The Aether isn't part of the weekly lineup. 

Anyway, back to the main topic of my column: the online Standard metagame. Why is this important or interesting? First of all, it's ever-evolving, it twists and turns, it marches ever onward, and it's the most rapidly shifting metagame there is. By the time a player adjusts to a trend, the trend has moved on. A deck that is dominating the metagame now might just fall off the radar shortly after and especially on ***Magic** Online*, the metagame swings often and big. This is because of the great number of tournaments that are run online. The amount of Premier Events in Standard on ***Magic** Online* usually approaches 10 per week. And I'm not even mentioning the non-stop 8-man Standard queues. Compare that to your local card shop, where you're lucky to have more than two Standard events per week. My goal is to give you an idea what to expect and advice what to play, thereby keeping you up to date on the format and ahead of everyone who doesn't read this, and perhaps that can break down some of the barriers that might be keeping you from diving into the vast and overwhelming online Standard queues. Furthermore, if you're someone who just plays physical cards in brick and mortar game shops, you can use my information to stay ahead of your local metagame. 

That brings me to the second reason why the ***Magic** Online* metagame is important: often, ***Magic** Online* is the starting point for whatever the next big thing in real life is. It is not uncommon for a new deck to be first seen on ***Magic** Online*, after which it starts to dominate the paper format as well. A good example of that is Solar Flare, the B/W/U control deck that had been building steam on ***Magic** Online* in recent weeks and ultimately took two of the three spots on the U.S. National Team, including eventual champion Paul Cheon. If you do not pay attention to what is happening on ***Magic** Online*, you are always one step behind. Knowing that decks like Solar Flare exist, and how to beat them gives you a great advantage against those people who did not see these decks early. And paying attention to metagame trends will help you in the long run. 

I get my information on the ***Magic** Online* decks mainly by watching replays of the Top 8s of Premier Events. I won't be doing this all by myself; I also have a couple of adepts that are going to help me collect data: Adept\_Yawgmoth and Adept\_Bahamut. If you're doing well at Premier Events with a special deck, don't be surprised if I or one of these adepts asks if you'd wish to share your list. Back to the replays - this feature of ***Magic** Online* is easy, fun, and useful. You can watch the games unfold and make note of the relevant cards played, as well as learning from how the top players pilot their decks in every situation. If you don't know how to watch replays, I suggest you check out this link here, where Bennie Smith clearly explains how to do it.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/da/c7/dac75ede89a49ea5e4f5cbf4851eb2c38f926030ba9bab6de6a20395804eff72.jpg)With all the introductions out of the way, let's move on to the real content. My first go will not be quite different from how a typical column of mine is going to look like. Sure, I *could* write a similar article to what I'm planning for the upcoming weeks - present you with an update on how the ***Magic** Online* Standard metagame had changed from the previous week to now - but I haven't even defined what the current Standard metagame looks like. The die hard players amongst you probably know what the top Standard archetypes are on ***Magic** Online* right now, but that surely doesn't hold for everyone, so I figured it would be wise to start with a beginners guide to the current Standard. When I come back to Standard next week and explain what the big metagame shifts are, at least can place what I'm talking about in a known context. Another reason why it won't be that useful to cover last week's metagame shifts right now is that next week the introduction of *Coldsnap* will shake things up anyway. *Coldsnap* has gone on sale for ***Magic** Online* by the time you read this, and the *Coldsnap* Release Events start tomorrow. Therefore, today I will just highlight the Top 10 Standard decks, ranked in order of popularity, to get you started. I went out of my way to collect data on ***Magic** Online* and noted which players did well with which decks. 

For each Top 10 archetype, I asked the ***Magic** Online* player that had put up the best results with that deck in the Premier Events for their deck lists and insight. Therefore, you are about to see the top lists and new technology from the best online players. If you ever face one of them in the digital landscape, you can be sure you are in for a tough match. Also, for every deck I will give you my thoughts on the potential *Coldsnap* additions. To rank the decks in order of popularity, I came up with a weighed popularity percentage that describes how often a deck has shown up in the Top 8 of Premier Events, adjusting it so that last week's results are more important than the results of the previous weeks and adjusting it so that a tournament winning deck gets more weight than a deck that loses in the quarters. If you do not want to read boring stuff about numbers, then please just skip the next section and enjoy the decks. Trust me, my system for assigning popularity percentages and ranking the decks is accurate enough. .

![](https://i.ormos.uk/45/e8/45e82188faf061b022e333c4378211ee6316234f535ba4a3b3c336f4f31e0814.jpg)For those interested in the system I came up with for this column, I wanted to rank decks in order, so I had to tally up some numbers. I *could* count the number of times a certain deck made the Top 8 in Premier Events every week, and conclude with something like “12 Solar Flare, 9 Hand in Hand, etc.” However, there is a problem with this: a deck that makes the Top 8 with a 4-1-1 record and then loses in the quarters gets just as many weight as a deck that wins the tournament undefeated. That's obviously not fair. I thought of a simple, yet good way to score up the list: a mere Top 8 appearance scores three points (since it's usually a 5-2 record overall, and 5 - 2 = 3 more wins than losses, therefore 3 points), a Top 4 finish scores four points (since it's usually a 6-2 record overall, and 6 – 2 = 4 points), the runner-up gets five points (usually a 7-2 record), and a win scores seven points (usually an 8-1 record). In the case of a concession in the finals, both decks in the finals get 6 points. In the case of a big 4x event, every deck gets one extra point, since it's harder to fight your way through more players and more rounds. 

And as a note, I do not include results of tournaments that have less than 32 participants. The reason for this is that with less than 32, more than one quarter of the field can end up in the Top 8, which isn't representative enough in my book. My system is close to the Pro Point distribution in the Grand Prix tournaments, so I think it is a solid and an accurate reflection on how powerful each deck really is. Now the popularity percentage I will calculate each week is simple enough: let's say that in a given week I had 10 Premier Events. I distribute 32 points to every Top 8, so that's 320 points overall in a week. If a deck got 5 Top 8s, 1 Top 4, and 1 win, that adds up to 26 points. The popularity percentage is then 26/320 \* 100% = 8,1%. Simple enough. So that's the system I will use every week to rank the decks in order of popularity.

This week, I will present you the top archetypes in the format. I *could* just take the popularity percentage from last week and base my deck ranking on that. However, I did not want to do that, because that is a small sample size and heavily influenced by the trend of the week. I also did not want to simply take the average of last month's popularity percentages, since that is too long of a period (on ***Magic** Online*, at least), and it does not give proper weight to the results of last week, which are more important than the results of 3 weeks ago, no matter how you look at it. I eventually thought of a nice compromise: I checked the results of the last three weeks and calculated a weighed popularity percentage over 3 weeks: Overall percentage used to rank the decks in this article today = popularity percentage of three weeks ago \* 3/12 + popularity percentage of two weeks ago \* 4/12 + popularity percentage of last week \* 5/12.

Oh, and for those of you wondering, a week in my book runs from Monday through Sunday and for “last week”, I counted every Premier event up to and including nr. 798152.

At first sight, you might want to classify this deck as an average blue control deck, but it's not. It doesn't even play any countermagic of note! Solar Flare is more of a “hybrid good stuff” deck that just brings the highest card quality to the table. It takes the concept of card advantage to a new level, since almost every single card in the deck provides card advantage of some kind, from Court Hussar to Persecute. The game plan of Solar Flare is to keep opposing threats off the board and then drop a big win condition as quickly as possible. It can accelerate into a turn 4-5 Angel of Despair via Signets or by using the degenerate Compulsive Research plus Zombify draw. The sideboard has cards that allow you to tune the flavor of the deck to your liking: against aggressive decks, more anti-creature cards come in the form of Condemn and Descendant of Kiyomaro. Cranial Extraction and Castigate come in to tweak the numbers against opposing control strategies or combo decks, as the situation calls for.

An interesting feature of topdeck\_cn's version is that he chose not to play any Remand (usually an automatic 4-of for most Solar Flare players), thereby making room to support as many as four Persecutes. He told me that Six signets and four Persecutes are very good against control decks, because that gives a high chance to wreck an opponent's hand on turn 3. He got the idea of cutting Remand from the U.S. Nationals Coverage, where in the [top Standard decks section](/en/events/2006/usnat06/decklists-top-standard-decks) a couple Solar Flare decks without Remand can be found. He tested it and liked it; Remand is not a bad card, but he never quite missed it and because more and more blue decks tend to run Spell Snare maindeck nowadays, it helps not to have many two-mana cards in your deck. Furthermore, when you have 2 lands untapped, your opponent will always think you have a Remand in hand, and that threat is good enough. Topdeck\_cn told me that he got his decklist straight from Pro Player of the Year Kenji Tsumura, and that he had been playing it for over a month, so it's bound to be a good one. He was also about to try maindeck Condemn, definitely a wonderful card. 

*How about strengths, weaknesses, and popularity?*

Solar Flare is pretty good against aggro decks. You can lose to a very fast draw that is followed up by a flurry of burn spells, but usually the anti-creature suite should handle it. To deal with the burn spells, you have Persecutes and you should keep a couple of them in after boarding against aggro. The other side of the coin is that Solar Flare has trouble against control or combo decks. You need a good draw in game 1. Game 2 becomes better when you can replace useless creature removal with discard, but you're still not happy to face off against Heartbeat, Magnivore, or Izzetron. But other than those decks, topdeck\_cn claimed he had all good matchups and it's particularly amazing against Orzhov variants, simply because they're not fast enough.

As for popularity, Solar Flare is number one and it has been for two weeks straight now. It had been a fashionable choice for a while and after its recent success in U.S. Nationals, the deck exploded. It has been hovering steadily around 15-17% and I don't expect its numbers will drop significantly anytime soon. 

*Any *Coldsnap* updates?*

Players might explore Adarkar Valkyrie instead of Yosei, the Morning Star, but I don't think the Snow creature will be better than the Dragon Legend. Flashfreeze in the sideboard might be a decent option, as is Deathmark.

Hand in Hand is an aggressive deck, but not a particularly fast one. Instead of focusing on hitting a one-drop and a two-drop every time and winning the game as quickly as possible, this deck has more of a mid-range strategy. It simply plays the best drops at every mana slot, from Isamaru, Hound of Konda on turn 1, to Ghost Council of Orzhova on turn 4. It also runs eight creatures with protection from black (Hand of Honor and Paladin en-Vec), which can give certain decks headaches. In fact, a Paladin en-Vec carrying an Umezawa's Jitte is one of the hardest-to-deal-with threats in the format. The deck doesn't do anything exiting, but it simply has a solid game against everything and has late game staying power. This archetype can be tuned to your liking and no two Hand in Hand decks will be the same. The above list was originally created by Marcio Carvalho, one of the best Portuguese players, and was piloted to good finishes online by Iluminatti. It is tuned against aggro decks, as is evident from the maindeck Descendant of Kiyomaro and Shining Shoal. Burn doesn't really worry this Hand in Hand variant. According to Iluminatti, discard is useless against most decks, that's why Marcio Carvalho opted for the anti-aggro package. Other Hand in Hand versions can differ from the above one by playing maindeck Castigate, Azorius Guildmage instead of Eight-and-a-Half-Tails, or Phyrexian Arena against control. Besides those tweaks, there are many more valid options in black and white and you can really tune this deck into any angle you want.

*How about strengths, weaknesses, and popularity?*

Orzhov decks have been a mainstay since *Guildpact* hit the shelves, in many different forms. For a while, the more exotic and fancy variants were more popular, particularly Ghost Dad (featuring the interaction between Tallowisp, Thief of Hope, and Arcane/Spirit cards) or Ghost Husk (featuring the interaction between Nantuko Husk, Promise of Bunrei, and Orzhov Pontiff). All of them had something in common: they ran Godless Shrine, Ghost Council of Orzhova, Mortify, and Dark Confidant. It appears that when you start a deck with these powerful cards, you'll end up with a good build no matter what. Nevertheless, the ***Magic** Online* players had abandoned the Orzhov decks with fancy tricks in favor of the above Hand in Hand variant, which just runs solid efficient cards instead of eye-catchers. Three weeks ago, this deck was number one with a whopping 17%. Then, possibly due to the rise of Solar Flare, a mediocre matchup for this Hand in Hand variant, it dropped to around 12% in the last two weeks.

*Any Coldsnap updates?*

White Shield Crusader could replace (or complement) Hand of Honor. It's a pretty close call between the two, but right now I'd stick with Hand of Honor, since it is not as easily killed by Electrolyze or Umezawa's Jitte counters, and it can block an Isamaru, Hound of Konda on turn 2 and live. Deathmark is probably a good pick, since against Boros and Simic it kills just about as many guys as Last Gasp does, and that for one mana less. Krovikan Rot is something to consider as well, giving you some repetitive removal against decks with tiny creatures.

*How does this deck work?* 

This is a land destruction deck that tries to keep the opponent low on lands, thereby preventing him from casting any spells. The ideal draw starts with Boomerang or Eye of Nowhere on turn 2 (not technically land *destruction*, but it definitely gets a land off the table which is good for tempo), followed up with a Stone Rain on turn 3. On subsequent turns, some more land will likely be destroyed, perhaps some card draw to reload, and some countermagic to make sure the opponent will be able to resolve anything noteworthy. A turn 6 Wildfire clears the board, after which a Magnivore the size of a Polar Kraken – the deck plays that many sorceries - comes down to end it all. Playing first, Magivore is a monster and can simply get the “I win” draw that I just described. But even if that perfect draw doesn't show up, Magnivore can play a good game and the high amount of card drawing will make sure the land destruction will keep on flowing. 

*How about strengths, weaknesses, and popularity?*

Magnivore has been a serious contender in the metagame for a while now, and it's actually increasing in popularity the last couple weeks: three weeks ago it was around 10%, two weeks ago it was around 13% and last week it clinched 14%. The good news is that Magnivore has a pretty good matchup against basically any control deck with a fragile mana base, such as the 3-color Solar Flare or the Izzetron deck with 12 colorless lands. Without lands, those decks cannot cast their six-mana Dragon *Legends*, nor can they win a counterwar. Johan80, who gave me the deck, had made a couple of Top 8s with this list, and had been doing reasonably well in 8-man queues. His board is pretty much dedicated to beating aggro decks, since the deck has a pretty hard time doing so game 1. 

Magnivore has problems against quick creatures. It has a feather-light removal suite in Pyroclasm and Wildfire, which is usually not good enough. Picture your opponent starting off with an Isamaru, Hound of Konda plus a Hand of Honor and then you start playing a Stone Rain while he flings a Char in your face. You can imagine who wins that one. So that's where the Volcanic Hammers, Pyroclasms, and Repeals from the sideboard are for. Other Magnivore players have tried out Steamcore Weird - a non-red answer to troublesome Paladin en-Vecs - and Spell Snare in their sideboards against aggro decks, but johan80 found his choices superior. He also wanted give props to Draccon136, “magnivore-master” and apparently the guy who has worked out what really works in a tuned Magnivore list, to Fabian for always lending cards, and to his clan Perpetual Miss.

*Any Coldsnap updates?*

There are a couple options. Icefall could replace Demolish, but since Magnivores aren't meant to die, I doubt the recover can come into play, so I'd probably keep the single-red card. Cryoclasm could be a decent sideboard card, effectively a Stone Rain with a bonus against the right deck. The only problem is finding that “right” deck, since there is no popular White/Blue deck right now, and most decks with Islands or Plains only run 6-10 targets, thereby sometimes stranding Cryoclasm dead in your hand. Rune Snag is another option, but I think I prefer Mana Leak, since it's better in the early game. I'm aware that every Rune Snag beyond the first is better than Mana Leak, but that only matters for the late game and then your opponent is supposed to be low on land anyway. This is a land destruction deck after all.

This deck plays the best snake theme cards and uses them to their fullest extent. It starts with mana acceleration in the early turns with Sakura-Tribe Scout, Sakura-Tribe Elder, and Coiling Oracle, all snakes of course. Then Sosuke's Summons (which never stays in the graveyard for long, I might add) and Patagia Viper make an army of snake tokens, after which Seshiro the Anointed (sometimes fetched via Chord of Calling) or Coat of Arms come down to turn those tiny little one-power Snakes into big, threatening monsters. The deck is very synergistic and never runs out of snakes, due to the card advantage provided by Sosuke's Summons and Ninja of the Deep Hours - it feels particularly nice when this ninja returns Patagia Viper back to your hand. It also runs some permission spells to make sure the snakes are kept alive and slithering. The latest “tech” for this deck includes 1 Indrik Stomphowler to tutor for with Chord of Calling, and Novijen, Heart of Progress to beef up your snake tokens.

*How about strengths, weaknesses, and popularity?*

SystemFailure (Cedric Phillips in real life) told me that he chose to play the deck because it looked fun and it turned out to be very, very good because it has broken draws that can't be beat and because it rarely loses to any aggro deck. It also does very well against Wildfire decks because of Coat of Arms and the card advantage of Sosuke's Summons. He thinks that the best Standard decks are the ones with broken draws and snakes can get those for sure. He claimed that the deck is also very good against Solar Flare. The special tech in his sideboard is Giant Solifuge, which fell off the radar in between PT--Hawaii and PT--Charleston, so he tried it out and it started testing very well again. It's great for the same reasons that it's always been. 

Snakes is a relatively new deck that is definitely on the rise. Three weeks ago, it held 2%. Two weeks ago, it had climbed to 6%. By last week, it had made it to the ranks of tier one decks with an amazing 12%. This deck might be the next big thing in Standard, so be sure to keep it in mind!

*Any Coldsnap updates?*

Yes! This deck will get a huge boost from arguably the best card in *Coldsnap*, which also happens to be a perfect fit for this deck. I'm talking about Ohran Viper. *How does this deck work?* 

This is the best combo deck in the format. It uses Sakura-Tribe Elder and Kodama's Reach to build up to a high land count quickly. In the meantime, it searches for combo pieces with Sensei's Divining Top and transmute cards. The deck usually tries to go off around turn 5-6 or so, starting with a Heartbeat of Spring and then using multiple Early Harvests (and Drift of Phantasms that can magically exchange for more Early Harvests) to build up to an overflowing mana pool, ending the game with a lethal Invoke the Firemind or Demonfire. A simple counterspell won't stop the combo that easily, since this deck can fight back with Remand and Muddle the Mixture. RogierMaaten's version is a relatively special one, because he does not play Maga, Traitor to Mortals. Instead he has Demonfire and that allows him cut black altogether, which improves the mana base. Other special choices include Twincast, which can help out in case you are just short of lands to fire a 20-point Demonfire; a 10-point Demonfire plus Twincast will kill the opponent just as well and it costs less mana. Another strange card is 1 Spell Snare, which appears random, especially since you cannot tutor for it, but according to RogierMaaten Spell Snare is a very good card - he'd like to squeeze in many more, but he simply could not find room for more maindeck. It was simply the 60th card. His sideboard is straightforward, omitting Carven Caryatid in favor of burn and Spell Snare against beatdown decks. 

*How about strengths, weaknesses, and popularity?*

A strength of the deck is its good matchup against Solar Flare, which does not have hard counters, does not have a fast clock, and does have a lot of dead cards in creature destruction. Heartbeat combo also handily dispatches assorted aggro decks. Heartbeat combo was almost non-existent in the virtual landscape three weeks ago, and then it suddenly exploded two weeks ago, probably because players rediscovered the deck as a good answer to the rise of Solar Flare, especially after the deck won Canadian Nats. So two weeks ago it went up to 14%, in second place just behind Solar Flare. Then last week the opposite shift occurred: Heartbeat of Spring appeared to die out, down to 5%. 

I can think of two reasons for this trend. First of all, a weakness of the deck is that discard, countermagic, enchantment removal or a Pithing Needle on Drift of Phantasms can pull apart the fragile combo. Once online players learned that Heartbeat was back in full force, they put sideboard hate back in their decks and were ready to beat Heartbeat again. Therefore, last week Heartbeat did not perform well. Another reason is that Heartbeat combo is incredibly difficult to play optimally. It does not find success in just anyone's hands and perhaps last week the good players were on a vacation. Anyway, this is a good example of the major shifts that can occur in the ***Magic** Online* metagame; in three weeks time, a deck can go from non-existent to dominating to a lingering notion.

*Any Coldsnap updates?*

I'd say if I had to pick a deck for Panglacial Wurm, it would be this one. I don't think there is a deck that looks through its own library more often than this one. Unfortunately, Panglacial Wurm brings nothing to the combo, so barring a transformative sideboard plan with creatures, it won't fit in. Another option is Mishra's Bauble, which can give you an effective 56 card deck - with gives you a higher percentage of combo cards per deck size. Plus, looking at your own library can help a lot when deciding to sacrifice Sakura-Tribe Elder now or next turn.

*How does this deck work?*

Take 21 creatures (ironically, all them two-power), 21 lands, 18 spells (including as many as 15 straight burn spells), put them all in a stew, mesh together, cook for a while, and voila, you have made yourself a Boros Deck Wins. People will always turn up with Red decks and try and burn you out, and the current Standard format is no different. The deck is highly aggressive and consistent, starting with a two-power creature on turn 1, then another two-power creature on turn 2, and 1-2 more on turn 3. Then the Boros player tries to keep the initiative and attacks his opponent down to a low life total, clearing the path with burn spells. After the opponent is down to ten life or less, this deck will just throw some burn spells in the face to end it once and for all. It is also the simplest deck in the format; even if you play badly, you can still win with a good draw. Slow decks that take time to take control will usually die to all the highly efficient burn spells. 

*How about strengths, weaknesses, and popularity?*

The strength of this deck is its consistency and straight forward game plan: quick creatures and burn. The weakness of this deck is that life gain is hard to beat. It won't beat a deck with Faith's Fetters and Loxodon Hierarch. It also prefers to avoid Descendant of Kiyomaro, Umezawa's Jitte, and Shining Shoal. It probably goes around 50-50 against Solar Flare, it's decent against Simic decks, and it's very good against Magnivore. As for Hand in Hand variants, that totally depends on the version. Boros Deck Wins won't be happy to face a Hand in Hand version with Descendant of Kiyomaro and Shining Shoal (such as the list I gave earlier), but it should beat Hand in Hand versions with lots of discard instead. As for popularity in the metagame, Boros Deck Wins appears to face a high amount of variability. Three weeks ago it was at 6%, two weeks ago it had gone up to 11%, and last week it dropped to a mere 1%. This is quite strange, and I can't really account for such a fluctuation. Perhaps after the week where it dominated, many decks put in anti-Boros cards such as Descendant of Kiyomaro and Faith's Fetters to make sure the stupid burn spells wouldn't win again.

*Any Coldsnap updates?*

I would replace Samurai of the Pale Curtain with White Shield Crusader. Cryoclasm can be considered for the sideboard as a burn spell with a bonus. Stalking Yeti might make it in as a Flametongue Kavu for the mirror match, although I doubt it will prove to be good enough.

*How about strengths, weaknesses, and popularity?*

Bliss is platinum against Persecute and they quickly replaced the Stone Rains. Izzetron is slightly below 50% against Heartbeat and any aggro deck, especially those with Tin Street Hooligans and stuff like that. Nevertheless, it can still put up a good fight against those decks. To put it in the worlds of Elegant, “there are like a dozen different decks in the format that are all viable and Tron does quite well against most of them - it's simply an all-round solid choice for the metagame.”

*Any Coldsnap updates?*

*How does this deck work?*

The idea of this deck is to combine cheap threats with counters and it also has a graft theme interwoven. A typical start is turn 1 Llanowar Elves, turn 2 Plaxcaster Frogling, turn 3 Cytoplast Root-Kin. That's two 4/4 guys on turn 3 and those fast openings are backed up with eight tempo counters – Mana Leak and Remand. Vinelasher Kudzu combines well with the graft guys and the deck also has a decent long game with Umezawa's Jitte and five-mana legends. But most of all, the deck just tries to overwhelm the opponent with fat guys as quickly as possible and holds on to the initiative via its countermagic. The Spell Snares in the sideboard give the deck the power to emerge victoriously out of counterwars. The rest of the sideboard consists of good cards against aggro in Iwamori of the Open Fist and Threads of Disloyalty, and all-round answers in Naturalize and Repeal. The Mimeofactures might appear a bit strange, but it can be used in many versatile ways, for example as removal on an Angel of Despair or an opposing Umezawa's Jitte.

*How about strengths, weaknesses, and popularity?*

According to Iluminatti, Izzetron and Hand in Hand are hard matchups, but the deck is good against Boros Deck Wins and Magnivore. Looking at popularity percentages, I notice that the deck is losing supporters. Three weeks ago it was at 9%, two weeks ago it crashed to 2%, and this week it regained composure at 5%, but overall it's still a decline from a while back. Why did this happen? My guess is that most Simic players had jumped to Snakes, which just seems more a synergetic and powerful Simic aggro variant to me. 

*Any Coldsnap updates?*

Ohran Viper could be an option, perhaps instead of Trygon Predator, but other than that I don't see much.

It's a cross-over between straight Gruul aggro and straight Simic aggro, combining the best of both worlds. The deck name comes from combining blue counters (Sea) with green creatures (Stompy). It can come out fast with the best one-drop around, Kird Ape, or accelerate into a quick turn 2 Trygon Predator or Plaxcaster Frogling with Birds of Paradise or Llanowar Elves. It rapidly gets five power worth of creatures on the table by turn 3, after which the permission package of 4 Mana Leak and 4 Remand kicks in. They just stop the opponent's big and powerful spells until you deal the last few points of damage, which can also be done via Char or Electrolyze. The deck is quite versatile, and your opponent can never be sure what to expect next. Ranging from an instant Plaxmanta to an attacking Birds of Paradise transforming into a Ninja of the Deep Hours, Sea Stompy will never fail to surprise. I have even seen other versions of this deck in the replays where a Burning-Tree Shaman carries an Umezawa's Jitte. Not an ideal combo, but it surely illustrates how creative you can get with this archetype. Other decks have been spotted running Moldervine Cloak, Dryad Sophisticate, and Thoughts of Ruin, but Nubian thought those cards are weak. Especially Thoughts of Ruin, which he had for a while, but he always drew it with no cards in hand or when his opponent had plenty of lands.

*How about strengths, weaknesses, and popularity?*

Nubian built this deck himself with his friends from his clan “Team UMK”, which he told me was a new born clan playing competitively on ***Magic** Online* for half a year. The deck seemed to be quite good against all the Solar Flare decks, Izzetron and Snakes. Usually creature decks lose to Solar Flare because that deck has so many anti-creature cards, but Mana Leak and Remand help a lot. They just want to put out one or two creatures, keep on attacking, and have counter back up for Wrath of God. It's a very easy gameplan, but according to Nubian, it works. Sea Stompy has difficulty beating Hand in Hand and Heartbeat combo, but sideboard solutions such as counters and Pithing Needle help to make them around 50-50. A weakness of the deck is the inconsistent mana base; a three-color aggro deck needs a little luck to draw all the colors, although Birds of Paradise can come to the rescue.

The deck appears to be in a rut, unfortunately losing popularity every week. It went from 11% three weeks ago to 4% two weeks ago to 2% last week, a trend that does not leave much to be imagined: Sea Stompy is on a steady decline, apparently losing to important decks and perhaps it's not as good against Solar Flare as Nubian claims.

*Any Coldsnap updates?*

I don't really see anything special. I considered Ohran Viper, but it's not the most aggressive creature around with only one power, so I don't think it will make it into the deck. Perhaps Wilderness Elemental in the sideboard against decks with many nonbasics, but that's about it.

### Deck Suggestions

Now that I have presented basically every Standard deck that is doing well consistently on ***Magic** Online*, let me try to give you some advice on what to play next week. Perhaps you won't feel like playing Constructed this week, because the Premier events are replaced with the *Coldsnap* release events until August 21, but the casual rooms and 8-man Standard queues are still open. I don't think *Coldsnap* is going to cause an earthquake in the Standard metagame. Perhaps it will shake a couple little things here and there, and spawn a sporadic new deck type, but nothing outlandish. That's why it's probably a safe bet to play one of the most popular decks of last week, as they have put up good finishes so they're bound to be good. 

Let's take a look at the Top 4: Solar Flare, Hand in Hand, Magnivore, and Snakes. Solar Flare is okay, but everyone and their mother knows about the deck now, so you're bound to run into a lot of hate decks. Hand in Hand is weak against Solar Flare, which will likely remain popular (even though I don't think it's the best choice), so I won't suggest that either. Magnivore and Snakes seem like decent options to me. If I had to pick one, I'd go for Snakes. It's a deck that has been on the rise for a couple weeks now, indicating that it is actually a first-rate deck, it can get bizarrely powerful draws, and it will only get better once Ohran Viper rolls in to replace Ninja of the Deep Hours! The deck has a lot of synergy and good matchups against most of the top decks, so I believe Snakes with Ohran Viper will be your best choice.

If you don't like netdecking and would like to try a brand new deck that uses the new *Coldsnap* cards in a creative way, I also have a couple suggestions for you. I won't give decklists because the ideas I'm about to present to you haven't been tested and are unlikely to be competitive enough (yet), plus you've seen enough decklists already. I'll just throw out a couple *Coldsnap* ideas for the more casual and creative deck builders amongst you now and I'll try them out myself in the upcoming weeks, perhaps coming back to you with a tuned list if one of these ideas appear to work well.

A) Build a deck around Scrying Sheets, a very powerful card draw engine that doesn't take up any slots in your deck, being a land and all. You can go a couple routes with Scrying Sheets; for example a multicolor green deck with Into the North (to search for it) and Sensei's Divining Top (to make sure you'll always hit a snow card). If you're not into those cards, you should head for a mono-color deck. Scrying Sheets won't fit well in a deck that has two or more colors without any of the delightful green mana fixing, because replacing *Ravnica* block duals, pain lands and Karoos with the colorless Scrying Sheets and basic snow lands is a serious blow to your mana base. You don't like to be color screwed and neither do I, so let's try to keep the mana bases as consistent as possible by staying mono color. As for mono color Scrying Sheets decks, I think mono-blue and mono-black are your best bets. Mono-blue will be a “draw-go” style deck, using lots of countermagic, a couple bounce cards and Scrying Sheets as a harder-to-kill Jushi Apprentice replacement. Mono-black will be an aggressive deck, running Rimebound Dead and Phyrexian Ironfoot to make sure at least half of its cards are snow. Both decks can also run Mouth of Ronom as a land/removal split card.

B) Build a deck around Haakon, Stromgald *Scourge*. My suggestion is to pair it with Gifts Ungiven and Court Hussar. The fun thing with Gifts Ungiven is that you are not required to search 4 cards out of your deck. When a card says “search your library for four cards”, you are not required to find four. You can just pretend to be really dumb and say “I could only find two cards with different names in my deck.” If you Gifts for Haakon and Court Hussar, your opponent will have no choice but to put both of them in the bin. If you still don't believe me, check out [the semifinal](/en/events/2005/worlds05/semifinal-looking-for-an-epic-finish) of the last World Championships. In game 5, I used the “Gifts for two” trick to get a Dragon Legend in the graveyard for sure, after which I could reanimate it with Goryo's Vengeance and swing for the win. Yes, it really works! 

Back to Haakon, if you have him in play and a Court Hussar in the graveyard, every three mana you have turns into a free Impulse, since you'll just let Court Hussar die, ready in the graveyard to be played again. Sounds like a good engine to me! I'd round out the deck with some more Knights (Paladin en-Vec, White Shield Crusader, and Stromgald Crusader come to mind), some White/Blue/Black control elements, and Compulsive Research to dump Haakon in the bin in case you happen to draw him.

### Concluding Thoughts

Which brings us to the end of my first column. It was a long ride, but hopefully it was a pleasant introduction to the breathtaking world that is Online Standard. My future columns will normally turn out to be shorter (thank god), but the angle I will take on ***Magic** Online* will remain the same. The discussion of how the online Standard metagame is evolving, the new popular decks, what's doing well, etc., will remain a fixed part of this column. This column will also remain the main communication channel for changes and issues regarding ***Magic** Online* plus being the main outlet for ***Magic** Online* III news, that's certain. Some of you will love the new angle of this column, others will hate it. Bennie did a very good job and it's going to be hard to replace him. 

This column is going to be influenced by three factors: what Wizards wants me to cover, what I like to write about, and what you guys want covered. And for that I'm going to need your help. I can do little features quite often in addition to the coverage of the Standard metagames and ***Magic** Online* news. I can cover other Constructed formats. I could also check the ***Magic** Online* forums and cover some of the hot topics there in my column, ranging from prize splits to bugs to marketplace issues, and if possible, I'll also get a decision maker to comment on them. I will try to keep as many people happy as I can, so I will definitely also address other topics than Online Standard at times. Please let me know what you would like to read here that Wizards can publish; I'm going to look for your feedback on the message boards, in my email inbox, and in the poll I'm about to put up here. I can't promise to process everything it in my next installment right away, but I'll definitely keep the results in mind and use them in the future.
