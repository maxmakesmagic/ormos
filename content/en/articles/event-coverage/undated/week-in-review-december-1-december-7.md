### Grand Prix-Los Angeles

Traditionally, west coast Grand Prix lag behind the east coast counterparts when it comes to tournament attendance. However, Grand Prix-Los Angeles was certainly not disappointing, gathering about five hundred competitors. That is quite close to numbers posted at the recent GP-Philadelphia. A number of pros arrived from the east coast, Canada and even Mexico. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gpla02/897.jpg)

A day of sealed deck play saw some pretty ridiculous decks rise to the top. In the last round of Swiss, Dave Williams’s double Visara deck lost to a deck featuring Visara and Rorix at the top tables. Booster draft came next, with Brian Hegstad quickly rising to the top of the standings and holding that lead all the way into the Top 8. Peter Swarowski, a brother of Robert Swarowski, who made Top 8 in GP San Francisco a few years ago, came in second place at the end of the Swiss rounds. Following him was Allen Sun, another amateur player.

In fourth place came Nick Eisel. Although Nick is not well-known on the tournament scene, he soon will be. Eisel’s original claim to fame came through **Magic** Online, where he is one of the highest rated players. Eisel proved that he can play with physical cards as well as digital ones by earning a Top 8 finish at GP-Philadelphia. He backed up his finish there with a second consecutive Top 8. He is a player to watch at PT-Chicago. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gpla02/885.jpg)

While I am praising players’ performances, how about Bob Maher’s? He earned yet another Top 8, solidifying his lead in the Player of the Year race and remaining dominant over the third format in a row this year. Maher is definitely on fire and is the first player since Cornelissen to challenge Budde’s dominance of the game.

Following Maher is Ken Krouner – a veteran player who was just good enough to play a lot of Pro Tours but not good enough to get on the gravy train. Krouner seems to have finally broken through, over the course of the last sixth months, with a string of solid tournament finishes. Phil Freneau’s top tournament finish was a Top 8 at PT-Tokyo, where his team ABU dominated the tournament with their red-green deck. This is only the second career Top 8 finish for Freneau, but he has had a number of solid money finishes in the past.

Last, but not least, is Gerardo Godinez. The only player to make Top 8 with 33 points, he edged out two other players on tiebreakers. Godinez becomes the first player from outside U.S. or Canada to make Top 8 in a North American Grand Prix event. Mexico’s Godinez was a finalist at GP-Santiago and finished in the money at numerous other GPs.

The Top 8 draft was a textbook example of why “playing nice” and not counterdrafting pays off in Rochester Draft. Hegstad, Maher, Freneau, and Krouner were working together, while the other side of the table was having a chaotic draft, shifting colors and occasionally counterdrafting each other. In the end, it was the abovementioned four players that made it into the Top 4. Freneau and Maher met in the finals, with Freneau earning the trophy.

### Grand Prix-Melbourne

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gpmel02/834.jpg)

While North American pros battled in Los Angeles, players in the Pacific got to compete in a Grand Prix of their own. Japanese players who traditionally dominate events in that region of the world did not show up, leaving the field up for grabs among competitors from Australia, New Zealand and Singapore. 

The only familiar name in Top 8 was GP-Cape Town champion Ben Seck. Seck did not disappoint, winning his second career title. Perhaps the best-known pro in attendance was Albertus Law, who placed 10th. New Zealand’s young sensation Cole Swannick missed out on playing in Day 2, placing 77th overall.

### Grand Prix-Reims

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gprei02/886.jpg)

The *Onslaught* Limited season ended with those events, and the new Constructed season kicked off last weekend with an Extended Grand Prix at Reims. Considering that this event came on the heels of Pro Tour-Houston, it would be no surprise to see similar strategies prominent at this tournament. Based on the Top 8, this was certainly not the case. Not a single Oath, Reanimator or Rock deck showed up in the Top 8. There was one Psychatog deck, played by Sweden’s Anton Jonsson, but it was eliminated in the Quarterfinals as it got the worst pairing – Sligh. 

The deck that many discounted completely as an option for Houston, Sligh appeared there and posted a number of decent finishes nonetheless. It was truly back in Reims, with two copies of it appearing in the Finals. The Top 8 featured a total of three Sligh variants, Suicide Black, two White Weenie decks, a Draco Explosion combo deck, and Psychatog. Germany and France dominated the Top 8, with Jonsson being the only player from outside the two countries. In the end, Germany’s Alex Mack defeated France’s Benjamin Caumes.

The results of this tournament were unexpected but very interesting, and are sure to affect the metagame in upcoming [Grand Prix-New Orleans.](https://web.archive.org/web/20050122060742/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.starcitygames.com/pages/GPNO.php&origin=sideboard_wir_20021209) Will North American players follow suit and play the aggressive decks that succeeded in Europe, will they fall back on what worked in Houston, or will something entirely new show up? We’ve got a few weeks’ break until we get to find out.

### Draft Workshops

December is here. For the first time in several years, there is not a single Grand Prix, Pro Tour, or any other premier event for a whole month. Combined with the fact that the next Pro Tour is Limited and requires less playtesting, that means a lot of down time for the pros. It is also an excellent time to give something back to the Magic community and help introduce more players to the art of drafting. 

Every Sunday in December, I will be hosting draft workshops over at [Kings Games](https://web.archive.org/web/20050122060742/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.kingsgames.com&origin=sideboard_wir_20021209). During the workshop, we seat eight players around the table for a non-sanctioned Rochester draft, with others just watching. Instead of drafting in silence, each pick is discussed. This helps amateur and PTQ players learn about card valuation, cooperating with their draft neighbors, and everything else they need to know to improve their drafting. 

If you are in New York area, feel free to participate. If you are a pro player who is willing to hold a similar workshop or a series of workshops in other stores, please drop me an email and I will advertise it in an upcoming Week In Review.

### Play of the Week – Fun with Crowns

At Grand Prix-Los Angeles, I managed to qualify myself for both Good Play and Bad Play of the Week status – all in the same game!

It was the first round of Day 2. I drafted a decent but unspectacular red-green deck. My first round opponent was my teammate Zvi Mowshowitz, who was playing a superior red-green deck featuring three Sparksmiths. My deck had few ways to win once multiple Sparksmiths entered play. It was no surprise that Zvi annihilated me in the first game doing exactly that. I managed to win the second game, thanks to a poor mana draw on Zvi’s part. 

Game three was very interesting. Zvi got out a pair of Sparksmiths, but I summoned several five-toughness creatures, creating a standoff for a few turns. I was then able to use Wave of Indifference to deal a lot of damage to Zvi, preventing him from being able to activate his Sparksmiths. However, my own life total was reasonably low. Zvi played a third Sparksmith and a Goblin Taskmaster. I was in a lot of trouble. I had Goblin Machinist and Tempting Wurm in play and Zvi could just overrun me by attacking with everything on the following turn.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/2b/31/2b31bda339454d8f1f23f0fd20b5d9c77b02f0154c7ad7502e9443c1861100b4.jpg)

I drew my card, and informed Zvi that he just lost the game. It was a Krosan Tusker. I cycled the Tusker to get a swamp – which I sideboarded in after the first game – and used the swamp to cast the card I boarded in – Crown of Suspicion. I enchanted my own Goblin Machinist and sacrificed the Crown, destroying all four of Zvi’s one-toughness Goblins. This swung the tide of the game tremendously. Although Zvi still had a number of great creatures in play, including a Silklash Spider, I kept summoning larger monsters and it was only a matter of time until he’d fall.

My Tempting Wurm was enchanted with Crown of Fury but it could not get through because of that darned Spider. I drew a Krosan Colossus, and played it morphed. On the following turn, I attacked. Zvi blocked with three creatures, including the Spider. I did some quick math and unmorphed the Colossus. Zvi was not all that surprised – it was Rochester draft and he knew that Colossus was one of my very few morph creatures. I then sacrificed Crown of Fury in order to make my Colossus a 10/9 first striker, take out his Spider and let my huge monster survive.

“First strike damage on the stack?” I asked. 

“Sure,” said Zvi. “Except there are no first strike creatures in combat.”

![](https://i.ormos.uk/d0/71/d071a22500ab5795b4515d13a3a236d05f96811825aa2e859377840c88b82c6f.jpg)

I did a double take, before realizing that my Tempting Wurm – formerly enchanted by Crown of Fury – was in fact a wurm, rather than a beast. Perhaps the word “wurm” in its name should have been my first clue. 

Despite the embarrassment, I did manage to win the game several turns later. Because of both Crown plays, it was probably the most fun game I played in a serious tournament in a very long time – and certainly felt worth writing about in this column.
