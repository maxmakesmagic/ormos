Black, Black, Black, Black, Black, Blue-Green, Black, Black, Blue-Green, Black Black, Black...

In the early- to mid- part of Day 2 in Osaka, Olivier Ruel was carrying around a copy of the standings with the archetypes being played by the tournament's top competitors annotated beside each name. The purpose of his doing so was to warn him about what each potential opponent might be playing, but in completing the exercise, Olivier saw a pattern appear much like the one above that made one simple fact very clear: The black deck was everywhere.

In the end, Ken Ho made the smart choice, seeing how good the black deck was and playing its antithesis to victory, but much like the Rebel Pro Tour in New York 2000, the end result of the Pro Tour isn't only about who won, but also what dominated, and how the Pro Tour environment will affect the beginnings of the Block PTQ season. If the cards of *Judgment* have no effect of the format, then you might see a lot more of the Ho deck. However, that doesn't seem too likely, so coming out of Osaka, it's the black deck that will be the most important to your preparations.

Here's the list I played in the PT:

Designed primarily by Dirk Baberowski and John Ormerod with help from the rest of Godzilla, this version of the black deck, which we named OS,WM, was in a state of flux for most of the two months proceeding the Pro Tour and stayed that way right up until the morning of the tournament itself. 

In the main deck, one Mirari, the Hunger and one Coffers were still being debated for a third Mind Sludge, one or two Innocent Bloods and another swamp, while in the sideboard, the Fiends, Demises, Bloods and the Swarm were less than 100% certain as we headed to the tournament site on Friday morning.

### Contents

The black deck at its essence is fairly simple: make lots of black mana and exploit it. At the heart of this concept are four cards that force you to play an extraordinary number of swamps in order to exploit them: Mutilate, Mind Sludge, Cabal Coffers and Nantuko Shade. While these cards weren't all four-ofs through our entire testing period, in the end, each proved to be entirely worth the four slots we dedicated to them.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/ba/71/ba71d140bd2ff1b8e63e2ce5c40f104688efae4179b778581514ea04548c9951.jpg)**Nantuko Shade**  
 Up there with Wild Mongrel, Psychatog and Mystic Enforcer, the Shade may well surpass them all when given a mana base of 22 Swamps and 4 Cabal Coffers to work with, with the Coffers offering the possibility of a lethal Shade even when your opponent is at twenty. The black deck is mainly a control deck, and the Shade proves the best choice for kill method because it has more explosive potential than any other card, while at the same time offering a hard to counter early game play that can effectively end games before they begin.

**Shambling Swarm**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 While the Swarm may at first appear to be an additional kill method, it's inclusion in this deck rarely sees it perform this function. Serving effectively as Mutilates 5-7, in addition to wiping out opposing armies, the Swarm offers the controller protection from creatures with haste and creatures that can be cast as instants. Often in our testing prior to the inclusion of the Swarms, the black deck would stabilize only to lose to creatures that could attack before black sorceries could kill them. The Swarm is more versatile than Ghastly Demise and more useful against Reckless Charge and Arrogant Wurm, so it got the nod by a long shot.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/18/1f/181fcadddc79678da90cdd45bf956706f985cf1b4fc44b06bc8a2f101c6099bc.jpg)**Morbid Hunger**  
 At times in our testing, the Hungers numbered two or three in this deck, but in the end, they should have numbered none. Our version of the black deck originally had fewer Rancid Earths, meaning that we wanted a way to kill opponents that couldn't be stopped by Squirrel's Nest. In combination with Mirari, two Hungers were usually enough to kill the opposition once the mana was available, making it a good choice, but as Earth's were added such a kill method became unnecessary. We ended up leaving one Hunger in the main because we didn't have too much time to test alternatives, but while it proved to be a valuable asset in the long game and against decks sporting red mana, in the metagame that emerged it would have made more sense to take Hunger out of the deck entirely.

**Chainer's Edict**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 Probably the best creature kill spell in the format, the Edict also helped shape the metagame as much as almost any card, making single card strategies far less effective then they'd been in Standard over the past few months. There was never so much as a word spoken with regards to playing any number other than four. 

**Mutilate**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 When you stare down a turn four Roar of the Wurm for the first time, you quickly come to realize that this isn't quite the Wrath of God it's been called, but its certainly the closest thing black has had in a long time. Again, we always had four in the deck and I can't see changing that.

**Mirari**  
 With Diabolic Tutor and Tainted Pact firmly in the deck, Mirari was a concept we were very happy with, but again, as the deck sped up, it became less crucial, to the point where we should have gone to one once the second Morbid Hunger was removed from the deck. If you're partial to Faceless Butcher and playing a few more creatures, Malevolent Awakening may be better here, but with Hunger, Edict and Rancid Earth, this can be a monster. Mirari essentially acts as a Defense Grid against blue decks, forcing them to counter two copies of Mutilate, Mind Sludge or Haunting Echoes, which is virtually impossible once Coffers is in play. With Tainted Pact or Diabolic Tutor, Mirari is usually game over. That's a lot of good reasons to include a copy in your deck.

**Skeletal Scrying**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 I've always been a big fan of the Scrying in Constructed, and that didn't change for this Pro Tour. The last sideboard change I made saw me remove the Scrying I had in my sideboard for an Innocent Blood out of fear of Braids in the mirror match up, but this change was short-sighted as the Scrying is often just as important if not more so. If I had it to do again, I'd probably get two more in the board for the mirror match, the blue-black match up and against any deck using Haunting Echoes.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/cc/1f/cc1f096ae6554c6012e40f347790eb90820dfbba3e581b6de0a1a13070fda40d.jpg)**Haunting Echoes**  
 Thanks to the one Echoes in the main deck, this deck often became an uber-control machine whose goal was to resolve Echoes and deck the opponent. Obviously, the Echoes is somewhat weak in the face of beatdown, but if you're playing against either black or blue-green, the resolution of this card usually means victory. Especially good with Mind Sludge.

**Diabolic Tutor**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 Four mana is a lot, but in a deck that is capable of producing so much more while at the same time forking the effect, it becomes more than worthwhile. Definitely a four-of in your more controllish versions.

**Tainted Pact**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 This may be my favorite card in the deck. A lot of players elected to not use Tainted Pact in their Osaka decks, but I definitely felt like this was a big mistake for anyone playing swamps. The Pact puts a card in your graveyard while either getting you that crucial land early or cycling away the cards you don't want to draw late, and once you get it going with Mirari, the game is close to done. Most black players, realizing they couldn't rely upon Coffers for mana early, played twenty-eight lands, but I'd much rather go down to twenty-six and a bunch of Pacts. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/a4/cf/a4cf95fc4036f0d1478d5c86cb93a9df4100a1c9f47a6da486731b9810de1c6b.jpg)**Rancid Earth**  
 I wasn't a huge fan of the four-Earth plan at first, but through time it really grew on me. With four in the deck, you have an advantage in the mirror, where the first Sludge usually wins, while not having to worry about Squirrel Nest much at all. Once you get Mirari/Tutor going, you can start playing your land destruction, with the deck's biggest fear, Upheaval, no longer a factor. The Earths are pretty poor against blue-green decks, but they work very well with the Braids sideboard. I'd go with four if I were playing the PT today. 

**Mind Sludge**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 Ollie Schneider pointed out to me that through Osaka weekend, we were using some of the most powerful effects the PT has seen in a long time. Mass creature kill, draw X cards, mass land destruction, Tutor for two cards and Lobotomy your graveyard were all a lot of fun, but in the end, it was really Mind Twist that seemed the most effective. We expected a little more beatdown so we only ran two, but in retrospect, three would have been the right number for this deck.

### Sideboard

With a main deck that felt so unyielding, the most important thing in picking our sideboard cards seemed to be making sure we had enough to side out for each match up. With that in mind, we ended up with two conversion sideboards: the Braids/Fiend tactic vs. blue-black and black, and the Mind Sludge/Haunting Echoes control against blue-green.

**Braids, Cabal Minion**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 It seems strange for a deck whose main function is to produce massive amounts of mana to use a card like Braids, but when the mirror match almost invariably comes down to top-decking, the best answer is to try and end games before they begin, and Braids is the best way to do that. Additionally, Braids and Rancid Earth were usually enough to keep the Upheaval decks from casting the evil card with enough of advantage to end the game. An absolute must.

**Mesmeric Fiend**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 This was likely the weakest card in our sideboard, but it worked well with Braids in the fact that it could be sacrificed early, could force it through by removing a counter or could protect the girlie legend from Chainer's Edict. Given another two weeks of **Magic** Colony-condition playtesting, I imagine the Fiends would eventually be reduced in number to zero.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/23/4f/234f8b7e5205e440d3b53507451c49f950eca2015179f8d790554bcf607565f0.jpg)

**Ghastly Demise, Innocent Blood**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg) Originally we only had the Demises, but Blood was better against light creature decks, fast starts and Braids, so we decided we should put them in. In retrospect, I'd likely go down to one Demise, replacing it with a Skeletal Scrying.

**Mind Sludge  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg), Haunting Echoes  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg), Shambling Swarm  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)**  
 These were in the sideboard in order to adjust the deck appropriately for each match up without needing to run 80 cards main. 

**Swamp**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 This may seem a little confusing, but with black decks all over the place running Rancid Earth main and Braids in the side, we wanted a little more mana if possible, and the slim sideboard pickings made it so. I also ended up bringing it in against blue-green or green when playing first so I could be sure of four mana on turn four and five on turn five.

### What We Didn't Use

**Innocent Blood**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 We originally had four Blood and no Shades in our more heavily control-based variants, but as the power of the Shade made itself more apparent, we came to understand that the synergy of the two cards was such that it would make more sense to play one or the other. In retrospect, it might make sense to put one or two in the main, but four would definitely be too much.

**Faceless Butcher**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 The Butcher's ability is a strong one, but we found that more often than not, it wasn't doing enough. Pretty much a wasted slot in the mirror match (worse actually; Mutilate kills their Butcher and gets your creature back), the Butcher is good at removing Roar of the Wurm tokens but can't really block anything effectively against blue-green, making it a questionable metagame choice. It also seemed too weak to warrant using sideboard slots for it. Another four casting cost spell is not what this deck needs.

**Laquatas's Champion**  ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/cardback.jpg)  
 We didn't test the Champion too much, but I was adamant leading up to the tournament that this would be the wrong choice for the deck. Let's face it, once you have the mana to cast and protect it, the Champion is rarely as good as the Shade, and a control deck doesn't need more than five (counting the Echoes) real kill methods. I personally think that YMG were very smart to spot the Stalking Bloodsucker, which we never really thought of trying. It feels like evasion is a lot better than regeneration in this format.

### Playing The Deck

To conclude, a quick synopsis of how the big matchups work

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/fd/1b/fd1b445f34356c4bf4453571896984132edcf2b6b2afb2329cea64268f4c15cd.jpg"><br><i>It's a race you have to win.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**Vs. Black:** Unfortunately, this is a bit random. The most important card here is Mind Sludge, with the first one's resolution a huge boost to the caster, but because almost everyone has four Sludges after boarding, you have to cast your spells rather than risk holding them. What this means is that in the extended game, whoever draws the Echoes first often loses, as they have to cast it then wait for the opponent to draw theirs, which later in the game is almost invariably more impressive. The Braids sideboard is an attempt to circumvent this eventuality, with the hopes being that the good draw will finish your opponent off before the game really gets going, or in the least that a couple of Stone Rain style effects will allow your Sludge to be the first one cast. Skeletal Scrying is also very big here, because of its ability to break the game open after being Sludged and because it protects your graveyard from Echoes.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/28/4b/284b76dceeb20aeba721601cd2d9347ee1d26e97f641b0333e95602aa1410370.jpg)

**Vs. blue-green:** The main reason we've retrospectively considered main decking Innocent blood is this match up, because if you can't kill the second turn Mongrel, you can be in trouble if an opponent follows with turn three Arrogant Wurm, turn four Roar of the Wurm. That said, the Swarms are very good here while there is likely not a matchup in which the Shades are more effective. In most match ups, I'd hold the Shade until after casting my first Mutilate, but here you let 'em rip as there's seldom any card in the format that can counter on your turn two.

**Vs. blue-black:**Upheaval is the devil to your divinity, and this is the deck that best exploits that fact. The Braids sideboard is helpful here in making sure they haven't the mana to cast Upheaval/Infestation for a long time, and if they don't get to do that, Sludges and Echoes will finish them off. We thought this was one of the poorer match ups going in, but I went 3-0 against good players who ran blue-black.

**Vs. green:**Mutilate and Shambling Swarm are great here, while Rancid Earth protects you from what would otherwise be their most important card, Squirrel Nest. Chainer's Edict doesn't do too much against first turn Chatter of the Squirrel, so if you expect green mages, you want to be able to go to eight Wraths. I'd suggest that you not cast a non-Tainted Pact before turn four here. I'd also recommend playing first if given the option.

**Vs. red-green:** This is a very pure damage race, and you should use your cards as such. If you're at sixteen and they play a lonely Rootwalla, you Mutilate immediately. If you do end up playing against this deck, you'll regret having taken out the Hungers, but perfection is impossible in this crazy little game of ours.

**Vs. Kibler blue-red:** I went with both strategies here, bringing in Echoes, Sludge and Braids. With 'Kibs' sporting a questionable mana base, the Earths are great, though after boarding you really have to look out for Divert, a card I thought they weren't playing (I learned the hard way). If you have Hunger in the deck, your golden here.
