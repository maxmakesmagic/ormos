The poll last week was very close, with the Hymn beating out Hypnotic Specter by only four percentage points. While these two cards were racking up the votes, Duress definitely had the most vocal supporters. For every email I received about the Hymn and Specter, I received two about Duress. For all that support, though, Duress couldn’t muster half the votes of the eventual winner.

A quick note on last week’s MVP, Nevinyrral’s Disk. I received several emails requesting that I point out a couple of other things about the Disk. There’s so much to say about one of **Magic’s** most versatile artifacts that these slipped by. First, it is notable that the Disk is named after noted science fiction author Larry Niven ([click here](https://web.archive.org/web/20030701000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.amazon.com/exec/obidos/search-handle-url/index=books&field-keywords=larry%20niven&search-type=ss&bq=1/103-3527433-2297466&origin=sideboard_feature_20030701a) to see some of his books) who is famous for his apocalyptic work. Second, several readers wanted me to mention that the Disk is destroyed as a consequence of its activation and, as such, could be bounced and replayed in response. This made a very expensive, but very powerful lock with Capsize, as the Disk could be played and bounced repeatedly.

This week’s poll is about three blue instants. First up is Counterspell. It was a bit of a bombshell to find out that Counterspell would not be included in *Eighth Edition*, as it has been around since Alpha. Counterspell is probably one of the most influential cards of all time, leading blue decks to glory since 1993. Next comes Fact or Fiction. A virtual newcomer, released only a few years ago in *Invasion*, players quickly embraced this powerful and unique card-drawer in every format. Its low cost and ability to find two or three good cards gives it huge swing value. Finally, there is *Impulse*. While it may not seem like it belongs on the same list as the first two, anyone who has played Impulse will vouch for its strength and flexibility. Digging four cards deep and replacing itself isn’t something modern search cards are capable of doing. Which will it be? Cast your vote at the bottom of this article!

### What’s a Hymn to Tourach?

It may sound like a bad piece of classical music, but you aren’t going to get much more for ![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif). After the banning of Mind Twist (which was only recently unbanned), Hymn to Tourach became the touchstone of discard spells. The Hymn is a simple card with simple applications that became a tool of complicated and very sinister decks.

Mind Twist blew the lid off of discard. It quickly became obvious that random discard effects were strong and, in the form of an X spell, too much to handle at that time. When *Fallen Empires* arrived with its proto-tribal theme, one of the first cards to catch players’ eyes was Hymn to Tourach. It’s a mini-Mind Twist that costs a little bit less, mana-wise, with a more difficult cost of two black mana. While, at the very first, judged to be nothing more than an inferior Mind Twist, smart players caught on to the subtle difference that easily counterbalanced the limitations of the Hymn. While the Hymn didn’t have the ability to force your opponent to drop his or her entire hand, it could consistently be a worthwhile play on turn two, when Mind Twist would sit in hand without the assistance of Dark Ritual. The sooner you can play your discard spells, the better. It’s obvious that you want to sink your teeth into your opponent’s hand as quickly as possible, as you have a better chance of snagging the cards they have that would be best against you. Your opponent will be playing their best spells as soon as possible, putting a premium on getting your disruption into play early and often. 

Looking at the Hymn, it’s obvious that it has built-in card advantage. It’s always great to get two for one, no matter what the quality of the cards taken is. Most spells that give you this kind of two for one cost more than two mana. Even though the Hymn gives you raw card advantage (that is, it takes cards directly from an opponent’s hand, as opposed to Wrath of God, where an opponent also had to pay mana to play the cards you wiped out), it’s more than worth it at its bargain basement price. 

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>“You can get your opponent's land on turn 1 or 2. When this happens, it’s good game, and thank you for playing.” -Brian Ty Kelley </i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

What gives Hymn to Tourach its edge is the early random discard. As James Ford put it: “You're going to hurt your opponent, and chances are you'll take at least one or maybe two key cards. And that can include land.” It wasn’t uncommon to see players burning a Dark Ritual (and taking the point of mana burn) to force a first-turn random discard. I’ve even seen first turn double Dark Ritual, double Hymn, taking more than half of your opponent’s cards away before they’ve had a chance to do anything about it. Think about it this way: if your opponent kept his or her opening hand, there’s a good chance that it has a healthy mix of lands and spells, something on the order of four spells and three lands. If you manage to pull out two spells, then your opponent will struggle to keep up with your threats. If you manage to pull out two lands, your opponent really, really needs to draw into lands quickly, or else face a mana screw that will totally wreck his or her strategy. If you grab one of each, then your opponent is forced to play with a nasty double mulligan hand of three spells and two lands. No matter what you force your opponent to discard, the result is the same: an opponent with severely reduced options. It’s like a forced mulligan, with a kicker. The kicker is that your opponent doesn’t have the ability to evaluate a hand that is Hymned the same way he or she can evaluate an opening hand. For example, a player might consider keeping a hand of two lands, two cheap spells and an expensive one on a double mulligan, but he or she might think twice about keeping a more top-heavy hand, or one that’s lighter on land. When choosing whether or not to mulligan, you have an option. When being Hymned, the cards are taken randomly and are just as likely to be your key spells as they are to be your lands or your weaker cards. There is no choice, and that’s what makes Hymn to Tourach such a potent and disruptive card.

### When It Was Legal…

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i> Hymn might be considered an MVP in the sea of MVPs comprising the Necro deck.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Hymn didn’t really find a home outside of monoblack decks, mainly because it needed its double-black requirement on the second turn. You might remember the arguments in *Onslaught* Limited about whether to play Festering Goblin or Elvish Warrior, which cost ![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif) and ![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif) respectively, first and often found themselves in the same deck. The implications are the same. If you want to play the Warrior on turn two, when it is best, you have to play the goblin later, when it will be less effective. If you go the other route, it will be turn three before you can have the Warrior out doing his thing. Either way you go, you aren’t getting the most out of your cards. Even in Constructed (outside of Type I) with multicolored lands, it’s hard to play early spells of two different colors when one of them requires double-colored mana. A few decks featuring Hymn ventured outside of black, most notably Dennis Bentley’s 1996 U.S. Nationals-winning Necro deck, which was base black, with a splash of red for removal cards that were flexible and especially good against opposing Necro decks, which often found themselves low on life. For the most part, though, this strategy was not adopted and the next generation of Necro decks reverted to (or, since the majority never went r-b, remained) monoblack.

Hymn might be considered an MVP in the sea of MVPs comprising the Necro deck. In many ways, Hymn was the first volley in the war. If you never played against old Necro decks, it may be hard to imagine their strategies, especially in light of modern, super-powered Necropotence decks that set up quick kills by drawing tons of cards in a single short burst, followed by a generally unstoppable combo kill. Older Necro decks wanted to strip out an opponent’s hand through discard and attrition, play Necropotence, draw some fresh cards and restart the game with a Disk, this time on a full tank of gas while its opponent stuttered on few or no cards in hand. The Hymn was integral to this strategy for a number of reasons. First, the two for one nature of the Hymn was a natural fit for a deck looking to out-resource an opponent. Second, the early random discards were very strong in such a proactive deck. Third, the Necro deck is full of cards that were vulnerable to cards you saw in lots of decks. Full of tiny creatures with nice abilities, Necro was weak against cards like Contagion, Serrated Arrows, Lightning Bolt and Incinerate, as well as Disenchants for the Disks. These cards are reactive and, therefore, often sit in an opponent’s hand until they are useful. While in hand, they are easily taken care of by Hymns. I can’t count the number of times, personally, when I was playing a great game against Necro, with white pump knights (like Order of Leitbur) pressing the Necro player and a Disenchant in reserve to counter the Disk, only to get hit by a Hymn, knocking the Disenchant out, followed by a Disk to reset the board. The sheer amount of disruption the Hymns could cause created problems for every other deck. It wasn’t that decks didn’t have ways to beat Necro, it was that you couldn’t count on holding onto them until the time was right. Between the Disk and the Hymn, your cards weren’t safe anywhere.

A few years after Black Summer (the time when Necro first appeared and dominated Standard), Hymn to Tourach found its way into another deck, this time using a different ![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif) spell: Pox. The deck operated on a similar theme to Necropotence, siphon away an opponent’s resources, gaining card advantage wherever possible and then, when both players are bottoming out, have an engine or trick that puts you ahead. For Pox, the game was all about reducing your opponent’s ability to play while simultaneously setting up your own strategy, which often revolved around graveyard manipulation and Cursed Scrolls. Artifact mana, like Charcoal Diamonds, gave the deck an out, minimizing the devastation caused by Pox. Here, like in the Necro deck, Hymn to Tourach is a monster, forcing blue decks on their heels early, helping to establish a situation where Pox will resolve or buying more time against the crude combo decks of the time, like Fruity Pebbles.

The trick to this deck is attacking your opponent’s resources. In many ways, this is a predecessor to modern Extended’s reanimator decks (in much the same way as Fruity Pebbles was the grandfather of Cocoa Pebbles. Though the newer deck accomplishes its goals a TON more effectively, it is descended from the older deck). This Pox deck seeks to break your opponent down, forcing him or her to function in less-than-optimal circumstances, while the same circumstances enable your deck to function at its peak effectiveness. 

### Consider This Scenario

Just a little nostalgia from Arthur Pruyn:

> *“I also remember running a “bring your own packs” booster draft in '96 at a game convention (Manafest) where someone brought Fallen Empires (we had a rule that you had to bring enough to make up three 15 card packs). Everyone else at the table complained that this guy getting all of the FE packs first would be unfair because he would be the only one at the table with Hymns. One of the other players had broken out some old Unlimited packs and no one was complaining that he would get first shot at all the Jewelry. Hymn was a monster in its day.”*

### Why Was It Great?

In a metagame with little in the way of good, cheap discard, Hymn to Tourach filled a gap in black’s disruption concept that had been missing since the days of Mind Twist and Sinkhole. With black having a turn two method of disruption that could almost single-handedly end the game, it gained quite a bit more strength. This, coupled with black’s affinity for Nevinyrral’s Disk gave it the ability to put an incredible amount of pressure on opposing decks, as players had to choose between putting cards down for the Disk to destroy or hanging on to them and risking being Hymned out. 

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>&quot;There are few things more devastating in the game of Magic than a first- or second-turn Hymn. A definite must-have in basically any predominantly black deck.&quot; -Matt Librizzi</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Black decks of the time had only a few weaknesses, most of which were reactive, rather than proactive. Thanks to the Disk, and *Alliances* release Dystopia, most proactive threats that hurt black were easily swept away. However, dangerous cards like Disenchant and the slower Armageddon could only be picked off by the Hymn, leaving black decks in a huge position of advantage. The simple existence of Hymn to Tourach was a huge hand in exterminating blue (except the Turbo Stasis deck, which was not a classic blue deck in any sense), and assuring black’s dominance in the summer of Necro. All that from a spell costing ![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif) that didn’t have any effect on the board. 

### The Big Picture

Though the Hymn started off its life as a tweaked Mind Twist, it has spent more time in the spotlight than its predecessor (though, admittedly, this is because Mind Twist spent the majority of its life on the banned list). Hymn to Tourach has been reproduced in many varieties but, over time, the random discard element has been bled out of the cheap targeted discard spell category. This is mainly to reduce the chances of an early discard spell stranding you with only one or two lands. Additionally, most variants have upped the mana cost, giving players a little more breathing room before the cards start disappearing from their hands. The good variants began with Stupor in *Mirage*, which cost ![2 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif) and was a little easier to fit into more decks. It did at least snag one card randomly. It was counterbalanced by the inclusion of Sand Golem (and by the previous release of Guerilla Tactics). Later versions took away the random discard altogether, like Mind Rot, or offered you a just single random discard, like Specter’s Wail. Overall, the discard, especially the two for one spells, is playable, but they are not the powerhouse world beaters that Hymn to Tourach was.
