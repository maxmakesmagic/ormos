### Keeping Your Online Account Safe

* * *

If you read my [welcome article](/en/articles/feature/2002/06-21-welcome-to-the-magic-online-site), you know that I said this web site was going to be different. I also promised articles about topics that are important to ***Magic** Online* players. Well, I'm here to tell you we're going to talk about a community problem today.

Any community is made up of a wide variety of people, and I'm not just talking about people you like to hang out with or not. There are sociable people, quiet people, the people everyone knows, and those who tend to stay in the background. It takes a spectrum of different types of folks to make a vibrant, healthy community, and ***Magic** Online* is no different. With a base of thousands of players worldwide, you'll find people of all different flavors when you wander from room to room.

This also means you'll run into some unscrupulous folks out to get the best of you. Aside from trash-talkers trying to shake your game, there are players (and I use the term loosely) who are going to try to rip you off. Instead of ignoring the problem or letting the Adepts and customer service team handle it, I'm going to educate you on what to look out for. I and the ***Magic** Online* team want your experience to be the best possible and, while we do everything we can to stop people from harming the community, the best thing we can do is tell you how to protect yourself.

### Trading

I'll start with an issue that many new players have problems with: trading cards.

Unfortunately, since the first time two players swapped binders to see if the other had that one card the other needed, there have been shifty traders. Much of the time, this means a person willing to take advantage of the other players lack of card value knowledge; convincing others to give up better cards in completely unequal trades.

***Magic** Online* isn't exempt from these people. Unscrupulous traders exist, ready to take advantage of the unwary. While you shouldn't be afraid of entering into trades with other players, you have to educate yourself first.

So, how do you avoid getting ripped off? While there are some complex answers to that question, there are some common sense things that'll help you avoid the sharks.

**Point #1: Listen to your gut.**  
 So someone you don't know tells you if you give him $2,000 right now, he'll be back tomorrow with a brand new car for you. Would you take the offer? Of course not. Likely, you'll never see that person (or your money) again. And even if he did show up with a car the next day, it's probably stolen property.

While that's an extreme example, the same is true when it comes to trading cards. If a player tells you that if you give him your foil Wrath of God today, he'll be back tomorrow with a Call of the Herd, don't buy it. The player may even have a good excuse. Whatever the argument, if the card isn't right there in that player's collection at the time of the trade, don't do it.

Listening to those nagging doubts can keep you out of trouble when trading and dealing with other players. Just like the real world, if it seems too good to be true, it probably is.

**Point #2: Pay attention to rarities.**  
 Not all cards are created equal. And while some cards of the same rarity are better than others, paying attention to the color of the expansion symbol can save you a lot of grief.

All playable cards in ***Magic** Online* have a color-coded expansion symbol on them. These symbols, at the bottom right corner of the artwork, are black, silver, or gold. A black symbol means the card is common, silver means uncommon, and gold is rare.

Chances are, if someone offers a handful of commons for one of your rares, that person is trying to take you for a ride.

**Point #3: Trade with players you know.**  
 While it's nice to meet new players through trading, it's also good to continue a relationship with someone who's done well by you before. If you're looking for a specific card, check with your buddies first.

Your ***Magic** Online* friends can also tell you whom they've traded with in the past, and whether that person was okay or not. You'd be surprised how many options you have when you turn to a few friends for help.

**Point #4: Check Magic card prices.**  
 If you're not sure what cards are worth, magazines like *InQuest Gamer* have listings of what cardboard **Magic** cards are worth on the secondary market. While it may not be entirely accurate when trading ***Magic** Online* cards, at least you can get a feel for the value of cards you're trading.

This is another case where your friends can really help you out. If you have a knowledgeable buddy, ask him or her how valuable your cards are. You might even want to have a chat window open with a friend when you're making a trade. That way, you can get an opinion on a trade before accepting it.

**Point #5: Check the trade before you accept it.**  
 A new form of scam has appeared in the ***Magic** Online* community: the quick-click artist. Here's how it works.

You enter into a trade with someone and get to a point where you and the other person have the cards listed you're ready to trade. You've selected to confirm the trade and, just before the other person confirms, that player either adds a card to trade for or removes one to trade to you. The player then confirms and hopes you don't notice something's changed.

It may seem a pain, but look at the cards again before you accept. If something's changed, don't accept the trade. Maybe it's an honest mistake, maybe it's not. You might want to ask the other player what happened and see if he or she misclicked something. Otherwise, I suggest you cancel the trade outright and note that you don't want to trade with that player again.

You may upset some folks like this, but you can make up with them later if you find out it was a misunderstanding. It's better to protect yourself because, if you go through with the trade, there's nothing Wizards can do about it. If enough reports come in about a specific player, we'll take what action we can. However, it's hard to prove a trade scam like this happened.

Now, let's look at other ways to protect yourself in ***Magic** Online.*

### Your Account

Your account name and password are your front door keys to the ***Magic** Online* world and should be handled as such. Losing your keys means you can't get into your own home and anyone who picks them up can.

Your entire collection of online cards is protected by your account name and its password. As long as you're the only one who has it, no one can take your cards. Wizards of the Coast understands this, which is why we don't keep that information ourselves. We can reset your password if you forget it, but we can't tell you what it is--we don't know.

And guess what? Wizards doesn't want to know!

**Point #5: No one from Wizards of the Coast will *ever* ask you for your account password.**  
 Yep, we've seen this already. There are scammers out there trying to finagle passwords out of players. Whether you receive an email or talk to someone online, never give your password to anyone. And if someone does ask, tell an Adept or report that player to magicconduct@wizards.com. Even better, get a screenshot of the conversation and send it to us. We want your account protected as much as you do, and we don't like having folks like that in the community.

Speaking of scam artists, an email was recently sent out by someone pretending to be "from mtgo." It asked for the last four digits of a player's credit card to resolve a store problem. Fortunately, no one seems to have fallen for this so far.

**Point #6: Don't put your email address in your Player Profile.**  
 I know many players add this to their information, since it's another way to chat with folks you've met through ***Magic** Online.* Unfortunately, it's also an open invitation for emails like the above one to show up in your inbox. Anyone logged on can right click your name and look at your **Player Profile**. Don't put contact information in it and you won't have this problem.

**Point #7: Don't give out your credit card number.**  
 Yes, this is probably a little obvious. The team at Wizards hates fraud because it hurts our real players. We don't want people to stop playing because they've been caught in a scam. The only thing we can do about that is to ask you to protect yourself and report incidents of people trying to get the information from you.

As for the above scam, there's absolutely no reason we'd ask for your credit card number. Wizards doesn't keep credit card numbers on file, so we have nothing to match against.

**Point #8: Don't give out your coupon code.**  
 Not as obvious as the credit card number, but almost as important (and in some ways more so) is not giving out any of your coupon codes. Your coupon code is cash at the ***Magic** Online* store. And there's even less we can do if someone uses your code to purchase product, since there's no address associated with them like there is with a credit card.

### Conclusion

I'm certainly not trying to scare anyone out there with this information. But, as I said before, I and the rest of the ***Magic** Online* team want everyone to have a good experience playing. We worked too long, too hard to let a few bad people hurt our community. We can't watch everyone all the time, and we don't want to be Big Brother.

What we can do is educate our players. And, though I'm sure other scams will pop up over time, as long as you watch out for shifty dealings, you have nothing to worry about. As a member of the ***Magic** Online* community, I ask you to help keep rotten apples out of our collective barrel.
