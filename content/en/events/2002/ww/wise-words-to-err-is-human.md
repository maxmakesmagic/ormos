Okay, first things first, right off the bat, Mikey P sucks. Yes, I’m talking about *that* Mikey P: Pustilnik to the masses, Pro Tour champ, Masters champ, Grand Prix champ, grand old man of the game. Oh, don’t get me wrong, Mikey can sling the spells with the best of them. He plays his own decks, does so well enough to have won a lo-ho-hot more than he’s lost and he’s on the lifetime earnings top 25… the man can play. The one thing Mikey is awful at, though, is being bad.

A myth has been perpetuated by men like the good Doctor. ‘The pro’, those ignorant to the truth state ‘is better then everyone else because he or she doesn’t make mistakes’. People don’t understand that it’s an advanced understanding of game theory and mathematics, a natural talent for out-strategizing opponents, an advanced appreciation for tourney play, an ability to thrive under pressure or one of any number of other factors that truly makes the pro stand out. In baseball, football, basketball or what have you, amateurs would never dream of challenging the professional player. In **Magic**, when the amateur loses to Kai or Jonny Magic, they complain about luck or what have you because they don’t understand what makes the pros so different from them, and for this grievous error, I place the blame squarely on Mikey P’s shoulders. After all, once I’d read *[Eight Very Bad Plays](https://web.archive.org/web/20030212000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.starcitygames.com/php/news/expandnews.php?Article=4309&origin=sideboard_ww_20030212)*, a fun little article he wrote for Star City a couple of weeks ago and realized that those ‘um, I blocked the wrong guy once in ’98…well, I think it was wrong, details are hazy, but I won anyways’ mistakes he made were the worst he could come up with from almost a decade of non-stop gaming, I could see how the amateur gamer could be seeing through error-free glasses.

In an attempt to put a stop to Mikey’s Pro Tour propagandizing, I put to you, the jury of public opinion, a second case study. A Pro Tour colleague of Pustilnik’s, our subject has been around Pro Tour parts for about as long as the good doctor, taking home less hardware but maintaining his pro standing more consistently over the years. His is a name known throughout the community, but unlike the aforementioned Pustilnik, when he errs, he errs as few before or after will. Ladies and Gentlemen, the subject in question… is Gary Wise.

Today I bear my soul in bringing you my greatest moments of weakness. When I screw up, I SCREW UP. You won’t find any anecdotes here about failed Kyren Sniper pings or questionable signals being passed in a draft. No, my riper moments have rated with the worst in the annals, from Borer’s Boner in Atlanta to McCabe’s double nap to Snepvanger’s unblocked Psychatog. I am a screw up and I’m pretty good at it.

This is a look at my eight biggest mistakes, at least those that have not erased their memory from my mind. I make no excuses (today) I hide no flaw (many). I tear off false clothing so you might know the naked truth. Check these out, and once you have, perhaps you’ll understand that one reason you aren’t pro may be you aren’t screwing up like the big boys:

### 8) 62

**Pro Tour-Osaka**  
**Odyssey Block Constructed**  
**Round 14**

Okay, let’s start with a technical error. In Osaka, everything was going right on Day 2. Heading into my last round, I’d won four straight playing the black deck I felt more comfortable with than any 60-card deck I’ve ever held and had a good match up against Ben Rubin’s u-r ‘Tog deck. Game one, I kept calm, played well and won the game with relative ease. One more win and I’d make Top 8, all this on the coattails of months of reading how a party would be held in Peter Szigeti’s room upon my falling off the train. I’d asked my teammates what to expect in the sideboard and maneuvered appropriately, shuffled my deck and presented. 

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>The scouting reports were inaccurate…</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

As Ben grabbed my deck to shuffle, I realized that for the first time in the tournament, I hadn’t counted before presenting. When Ben was done, he’d made two counts of 62 and 59 respectively, at which point I counted out my board to 13 cards and called a judge, getting a game loss. Crushing. 

I gathered up what little energy I had and tried to force the negativity out of my head and began playing game three, casting Mind Sludge for Ben’s hand on turn six, but the scouting reports were inaccurate and it turned out Ben had brought in Divert, robbing me of my hand. Fortunately, the sun does shine once in a while and I drew Skeletal Scrying, especially nice in light of the Haunting Echoes in Ben’s hand, drawing six or seven and going on to win the game. Sadly, the reports I was playing for Top 8 were unfounded. I finished 11th.

Okay, it’s PT-Boston and Courtney’s Boys are in first place. With Maher and Reeves each going 6-0 Day 1, I’m looking shaky enough as is when I get paired against Josh Ravitz of Stalking Tiger, Hidden Gibbons. Playing red-green against his red-black-green deck, I managed to tap seven mana in my pool while saying “Firecat Blitz for four”, for no reason I can conceivably comprehend. The game ends with Josh doing precisely the thirteen points needed to kill me while sitting on one life. That’s the one life the extra Cat token would have done, never mind the point of mana burn bringing me into lethal range. Good thing Neil won a few minutes later.

### 6) Burn, Burn, Burn, Baby, Burn! Burn! Burn!

**Grand Prix-London 2002**  
**Odyssey Block Constructed**  
**Round 13**

Playing a winner-makes-Top-8 match against relative rookie Pedro Velhinho, I was feeling confident. I’d just lost on an error in judgment (in hindsight) at table one in the mirror but my deck, monoblack, was pretty well-configured for the mirror and if nothing else, I was the experienced hand while Pedro had never won a PTQ match for all I know.

Game one was pretty easy, with Nantuko Shade taking the man down. Game two, though, was another story, with both of us playing tons of lands (including Cabal Coffers). I’d used up all my Sludges and so had he and this allowed us to build up our sideboarded hands. Thing was, I’d already ripped his board apart with hand destruction and land destruction, so I was pretty much in control. I started building up creatures, eventually getting up to three Mesmeric Fiends and two potentially lethal Shades, so with him holding five cards to my five creatures, it seemed the right time to go for the win.

I tapped two swamps and a Coffers for 19 mana, figuring whatever was left over could just be siphoned through the Shades, and cast my first Fiend to find three Chainer’s Edicts and 2 Innocent Bloods, three of which had been drawn over the last three turns. I was somewhat shocked, with the result obviously being an inability to go off after all. I thought about whether there was any point in playing any more creatures now, as they’d all die anyways and decided it better to wait in the hopes of drawing more threats in the following turns, and said go. Pedro went to draw when I told him to stop.

I still had seventeen mana in my pool. Did I mention I was at 2 life? I scooped up my cards, at which point Velhinho informed me that he had no way left to kill me (he’d sided out his Echoes), proceeded to win the next game and move on to Top 8, while I won my 14th round only to finish a much deserved 9th. Rough beats.

### 5) Foothills

**2003 Chicago Masters**  
**Round 1**  
**Standard**

If you haven’t heard about this over the last few weeks, believe me, I’ve heard enough about it for both of us. Paired against Brian Davis, a very strong player who, like me, is known for the occasional pressure-packed screw up, I got the dream match up: Beasts vs. red-green.

I’d chosen to play Beasts at the last minute because I didn’t want to play ‘Tog without having practiced it and because of the way the metagame had been shaping up throughout the day. Brian was among those who helped me shape it in my mind, as he was testing red-green with Brian Kibler and Eric Froehlich on tourney tables throughout the day. Needless to say, when I got my first pairing, I was very pleased.

We played game one and it was a joke, with my fat, life gaining beasts proving too much for BDavis. Not entirely knowing what I was doing (these were my first games with the deck), I took out my Living Wishes to speed the deck up a bit, bringing in the three Naturalizes I had for what turned out to be his three Ensnaring Bridges. The game got under way and my men were dominant, smashing him in the face and gaining life, finally forcing him to play his first Bridge. Sadly, I had no immediate answers and I started working on controlling the table, using Contested Cliffs to clear his men while Worship and the presence of Ravenous Baloth and around eight Beasts ensured that his burn would never do the trick. Turn after turn, I drew and said go with Brian finding his second and third Bridges, still no Naturalizes in sight. I asked how many Bridges he had, then told him straight out I had the three solutions. I never found them.

Speeding up my play for no reason I can muster, while still sitting at one life, I drew Wooded Foothills, played it and sacrificed it, assuming Worship would prevent the damage as it would with City of Brass, pain lands or what have you. I put the Foothills in the graveyard, picked up my deck and then (and only then) decided to read the card and figure out what it actually did. The funny thing is that none of the people watching the match had a clue either, so you can understand Brian’s shock when I scooped up my cards. Lesson for you gamers out there: if you have Worship and Ravenous Baloth and many, many beasts, SACRIFICE ONE OF THEM! Otherwise, who’s going to protect you from yourself? Fortunately, the match up being what it was, I was able to manage game three.

### 4) Turn 2: Go.

**2000 Pro Tour-Los Angeles**  
**Round 12**  
**Urza’s Block Rochester**

Igor Frayman is one of the best players on the Pro Tour books that no one’s ever heard of. He doesn’t participate much nowadays, but way back when, he was hanging out with Brian Hacker, John Yoo and Truc Bui when those guys were the cream. Igor was in the middle of one of the best non-Top 8 streaks in memory, posting Top 32s and 16s all over the place. We paired up for the first round of a draft in which 2-1 would likely put a player in Top 8.

Drawing first, I played mountain and cast Goblin Patrol, a more-than-fair start. Igor’s deck, as always, was built for speed and as I sat there nervous, dreams of Top 8 glory resonating, reminding myself to pay the echo. He made a second-turn play and passed the turn. Carefully, I untapped, paid echo, drew and attacked. Pleased that I’d remembered (I was wiped out at this point and everything was difficult), I passed the turn back to him and then realized I’d made a slight error.

Really though, who needs a land on turn two anyways.

Igor played another creature and attacked and I started trying to come back, playing one of the three lands in hand on turn three then another on turn four. My draw was strong, almost strong enough to overcome the time walk I’d given my opponent, but he managed to finish me off the turn before I was going to do the same to him, winning game two in similar fashion. By forgetting to play that land, I’d essentially removed myself from Top 8 contention. Crushing.

### 3) I Didn’t Need the Money Anyways

**Barcelona Masters**  
**Round 2**  
**Invasion Block Constructed**

I normally have a rule concerning **Magic** tournaments that says I can’t introduce any intoxicating elements to my system within three weeks of a pro-level event. Such poisons affect the mind (at least according to my subconscious) and I prefer to not take chances.

Three weeks before Barcelona, I traveled to my former home, Hampton Court Palace, for the first time, completely unprepared for what awaited me. I mean, Ben Ronaldson had told me he lived in a palace and all, but I didn’t realize it was a big touristy deal and the most beautiful in England. Overwhelmed by where I was, I was reintroduced to Ivan, Ben’s brother and at the time, a bit of a lunatic where such toxins were involved and for the next two and a half weeks I threw caution, and eventually thousands of dollars as a result, to the wind.

Still recovering from my time in England, I played Domain in the Barcelona Masters, beating Satoshi Nakamura’s red-green deck in the first round to advance to my Top 16 match with Jay Elarar, with whom I’d always been friendly to that point. What followed though was one of the ugliest matches I ever played and a precursor to some of my more memorable matches with Ryan Fuller and Chris Benafel.

Games 1 and 2 were split down the middle. I think I was mana screwed in the one I lost as this was a very good match up for Domain. Game three, Jay’s back against the wall, he started talking… and talking… and talking some more. ### 2) Drain You!

**PTQ Paris in Toronto**  
**Quarterfinals**  
**Type II (I don’t think the term Standard had been created yet)**

Definitely my favorite in retrospect, the fact it took place in a PTQ instead of a higher profile tournament is the main reason it’s not number one. 

I’d just finished getting slaughtered at my first two Pro Tours, Dallas and LA. With Standard the PTQ format of choice, I went back to an old favorite: the Skull. If you’re young or new to the game, that means Necropotence. It was a poor environment for the deck, but its power couldn’t be denied. I breezed through the Swiss and prepared myself to play against Elijah Pollock (yes, the same one) who, back then, was definitely the more established of the two of us and was running Prison.

### 1) Shock

**Grand Prix-Philadelphia**  
**Round 14**  
**Onslaught Block Rochester Draft**

Well, most of you likely know about this one. Sitting in seat one for the Rochester right behind Mike Turian in seat eight, I managed to draft a godly red-white deck with Jareth, two Sparksmiths, Slice and Dice, Tephraderm and the like. A 3-0 performance was needed to make Top 8, and I’d won the first two matches, beating Chris Manning then Turian himself to make the final. In the other half of the bracket, Team TOGIT’s Patrick Sullivan, the other white player on the table, was advancing to face me.

Game 1 was where it all went down. Pat got off to a fast start before I stabilized, getting down Tephraderm then Jareth, who eventually started attacking. With Pat at six and me at ten, he had one card in hand and ten power worth of creatures in play. I made a snowball of a mistake that turned into the most embarrassing avalanche of my career, attacking with only Jareth when I should have attacked with more creatures to force chumping to occur. Patrick went to two.

Sullivan untapped, drew a second card and cast Dirge of Dread, a card I hadn’t accounted for, mostly due to a lack of experience with the set. Knowing I was about to die and having only three mana untapped, I cycled Secluded Steppe in the hopes I’d find a card that would buy me just enough time to attack with Jareth one last time for the win. I found Shock.

“I’m a very lucky man”, I said as I turned the Shock over, looking only at the creatures in play, completely forgetting the life totals. I finally targeted one of Pat’s men, trying to kill it and seal me the game. Turns out the card he’d just drawn was Inspirit. I won game two when confusion led to Pat’s unintentional drawing of a card, but game three was not to be. I finished sixteenth.

Now, I’m sure everyone’s had a little chuckle at the expense of my vanity here, but there is a point. Of late, playing on ***Magic** Online* with my identity hidden, I’ve been re-exposed to the patterns of abuse and bitterness from players who either are frustrated by the game’s luck factor or don’t understand that sometimes, when they blame that luck factor, it’s not the reason they’re losing. I had one match where, with a six to nothing creature advantage purely as a result of outplaying my opponent, he told me I was “the luckiest person he’d ever played”. Give me a break. Some people whine too much.

Next time you get frustrated by **Magic**, and God knows its going to happen again, think about this: I’ve made every one of the above errors and countless others and I’m still at every Pro Tour, making money for high finishes in the toughest fields in the world. No, I’m not in the Budde-Maher-Baberowski stratosphere, but I’m a good **Magic** player, one of the better ones, and I make a lot of mistakes. Next time your opponent errs and wins regardless, as the anger builds inside you, stop. Think about the fact you knew there was a luck factor when you paid your entry fee, that there are a lot of levels to this game and you may not have considered them all and how you’d feel were you in the other chair. There are a lot of new players entering the game because of ***Magic** Online* now. More players mean more money for Wizards, which eventually will mean increased prize support and a healthier community. Every time you let your competitive drive overrun your emotions, you run the danger of driving those people away from the game. Stop shooting yourselves in the foot. Soon, the game will move on and you’ll be left behind. 

It’s a game. It’s supposed to be fun. Being forced to absorb the brunt of an opponent’s anger just isn’t. Remember why you started in the first place. Oh, and if you screw up, shrug it off. You’ll live to play another day.

Have a good week.
