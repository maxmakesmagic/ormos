Playing casual **Magic** --which usually, but not always, means multiplayer **Magic** -- is really about storytelling.

“I played Bifurcate on my Spiritmonger and just swung away!”

“There was a Pandemonium and a Furnace of Rath and a Repercussion on the board…but when I played my Pangosaur, he played Equal Treatment!”

“*He* played Swords to Plowshares on my Shivan Dragon, so *I* Radiated it…then *she* countered the copy of Swords for her Spirit of the Night!”

“With 10,000 squirrel tokens attacking me, I played Evacuation!”

Quick, which of these stories doesn’t belong?

Games where one or more players don’t do a darn thing are not exciting games, from a casual play perspective. People build and play casual decks for many reasons, but underlying all of those reasons is the basic idea that you will get a chance to show your friends what you’ve created.

If you want to build a multiplayer deck that uses principles of card advantage…if you want to demonstrate the power of Cephalid Snitch… if you want to lock five pieces together so that Sphere of Duty actually contributes to a win condition… no matter why you built the deck, you need mana to run it.

Most casual players I’ve met don’t mind losing. (For many of us, it’s a way of life.) What we all do seem to mind, though, is never getting the chance to see if the cool idea in our deck works or not. It’s very frustrating to have two lands in the right colors on the opening draw and then never see another land for ten draws.

This attitude leads directly to the problem with mana denial decks in multiplayer. While strict land destruction (e.g., Stone Rain) in group play is generally ineffective, there’s a small and growing pool of cards (e.g., Devastating Dreams) that threaten universal manascrew really well. But is this what you really want from your recreation? If your four friends wanted games like the kind you create when you play your supposedly cool Limited Resources deck, they would just put two lands in their decks, auto-draw them with five other random cards, and then let their eyes glaze over while you just did whatever you wanted.

“Lack of fun” constitutes the basic case against mana denial decks in multiplayer games. Remember it carefully, because as strongly as I believe it, now I’m going to talk about why (and when) mana denial in group is *good*.

### HARD LANDING

If cards like Limited Resources, Armageddon, and Wildfire are disruptive for casual play, why on earth would I talk about them in an article that’s supposed to be about “fun” **Magic**? Is there a time and place for even kill-joys like Stasis?

For Stasis, the answer is *no*. (Don’t bother emailing me about this; I have a Stasis deck that I use occasionally, too.)

For all other mana denial cards, I would say *yes*, and there in fact are three specific situations where this strategy works in your group. And by “works,” I mean “won’t get you 'disinvited' from future games.”

**Time and Place One: When the mana denial strategy takes time to develop.** What makes Limited Resources so offensive is the fact that it can hit on turn one, when there are maybe five answers total in the game for it. (Force of Will, Foil, Erase, Abolish, and Emerald Charm) The chance that someone has one of those answers ready to roll is rather small. Once on the table and restricting a five-player game to a double-land contest, the universe of answers shrinks pretty much to small corners of two colors -- green, which might have elves; and white, which may have Disenchant. If one of those answers doesn’t show up by round three, then the controller of the hated enchantment probably has a lock: Boomerang or Stone Rain a Plains or Forest; lay down a land; repeat as necessary until no lands exist but her own.

But imagine a world where Limited Resources could not be played before the fifth turn. (This is when it becomes “effective” in most duels, which is why it never caught on in tournaments.) This would be more acceptable to many players, since with five lands staying out, they have a chance to counteract the enchantment, with everything from Tranquility to Nevinyrral's Disk to four Goblin Grenades on the same turn.

Of course, no one expects the guy with Limited Resources to give everyone a head start. That’s not my point. My point is that if something even as extreme as this card comes late, folks don’t mind so much. Remember, casual play is about seeing all the different decks show off a little. So let’s turn our attention to late-game cards like Desolation Angel, Epicenter, and Obliterate.

When lands get reset on the seventh or eighth turn, the game goes longer. But it’s not a hopeless fight. (Well, okay: for the green mage facing down a 5/4 black flier, it’s a hopeless fight. For everyone else, there’s a ray of sunshine, since they get a few turns to recover while that green mage gets his head kicked in.) A late mana reset actually *helps* the fellow who hasn’t drawn as many lands yet as anyone else, because statistically speaking, he’ll draw lands and recover faster.

So when a mana-hoser hits the table, don’t go into pout mode just yet. Carefully consider the threat behind the land-sweep -- is there a card trapped by a Faceless Butcher that will reemerge after the Apocalypse resolves?... a Psychatog that will eat the remains of Upheaval?... a Security Detail that will pick up the pieces of a Jokulhaups? -- and figure out if there’s a reason to float mana. (Boomerang, Swords to Plowshares, Beast Attack, and Terminate are all excellent reasons to float mana.) If you don’t have any of these cards…well, then, read the next reason why mana denial is good.

**Time and Place Two: When the Group Needs to Evolve.** There comes a time in every play group’s history when every single player is going overboard in the over-expensive bomb department. An Armageddon is a good way to get people to focus their attention on the basics.

So every group *should* have to face down an Armageddon. Every group *should* learn how to react to Wildfire. Every group *should* even, yes, learn what to do when a Limited Resources hits the table. Consider it a growth experience.

After all, the answers to mana denial are very cheap and/or easy to find. Green, which has no fantastic answer for land destruction after it happens (unless you consider Terravore, Cartographer, and Groundskeeper "fantastic"), has plenty of answers beforehand: common elves like Priest of Titania, common druids like Yavimaya Elder, and weird enchantments like Eladamri's Vineyard. Blue can turn from villain to hero in an instant (pun intended), and since most land denial decks are combo-dependent, a Counterspell blow is often fatal. White, black, and red are all capable of cheap removal that can patch the board until lands recover.

Beyond each color’s own capabilities, there is simple artifact mana. The *Mirage* block (now *Seventh Edition*) Diamonds are still among the best uncommon cards in the game. The *Invasion* Cameos are perfectly solid in casual play.

So if you find yourself asking hotly, “Why does that jerk keep playing those land destruction cards?” then ask yourself just as intensely: why aren’t *you* playing *anti*-destruction cards? (A reflexive “jerk” is optional.) After all, artifacts and countermagic and elves are useful in all sorts of situations, but land destruction carries a terrible risk with it. The guy who absolutely needs Limited Resources to win is begging to lose. Who are you to deny him?

Once the land destruction deck that has terrorized your group for so long goes down in flames, it will get played less often… and may evolve, in turn, into something more interesting. Which brings us to our third situation.

**Time and Place #3: When the mana denial is creative.** I’ll admit this isn’t my bag, but fortunately I know people who *live* in said bag. And the creations can be spectacular. You don’t mind losing as much to decks that obviously took a lot of thought and time. Land Equilibrium and Mana Vortex, Natural Affinity and Pyroclasm, Pardic Miner and Lifeline, Army Ants and Groundskeeper, and Awakening (see below, but be warned: this is just for laughs…you can’t drop a land every opponent’s turn, so its best shot is in very small groups). The possibilities are endless, though they typically fall into fragile combo territory, *and* require difficult rares. If a group can afford cards like these, they’re still counterable by some of the best-known commons in the game. If your group can’t afford cards like these, well, then you don’t have to worry.

### THE EAGLE HAS LANDED

If a group can evolve through the steps I’ve outlined above, the problem should take care of itself. Mana denial appeals to a modest minority of casual players who, just like anyone else, want to pull off their specific trick. Take away the easy trick (Limited Resources or Stasis), and they’ll have to resort to more innovative tricks. Less predictable tricks are generally more expensive, which means more time for any given game to evolve past the deck’s “lock” threshold. So they’re easier to stop than the quicker tricks, and the deck’s controller will have to move on to greener pastures.

Before you know it, the mana denial geek is trying to make Fallow Earth, Radiate, and Oath of Druids into a viable deck… and isn’t that what you *want*?
