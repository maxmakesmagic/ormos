It's all about the Disk. While, of the three, Black Vise is the biggest offensive threat and Winter Orb could lock an opponent completely out of the game, players gravitated towards the board-clearing power of the Disk. The email clearly reflected that while people loved the power of the Vise and the control offered by the Orb, they had fond nostalgic memories about using the Disk. I received many stories about being beaten by early Vises and being frustrated in their opponents' Winter Orb/Icy Manipulator locks, the stories I received about the Disk were all “I had the Disk. I had control”. Nevinyrral's Disk makes players feel powerful. What more can you ask for in an All-Time MVP?

This week's poll is all about cheap discard, excluding the heavy hitters like Mind Twist. First up is Duress. Many people, including myself, were surprised to see Duress reappear in *Seventh Edition*, considering how much power you get for a single black mana. Duress takes good hands apart and really punishes bad hands. Then there's Hymn to Tourach. One of the brightest spots of *Fallen Empires*, the Hymn can absolutely wreck a player in a way that Duress can't – by pulling lands out of an opening hand. A couple of early Hymns could easily spell the end of the best deck. Finally, there's Hypnotic Specter. The Hippie is seriously old school and the touchstone for all discard creatures. A 2/2 flyer for ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif) would be considered a steal by today's standard, and that's without the discard ability or access to Dark Ritual for acceleration. Which will it be? Cast your vote below!

### What's a Nevinyrral's Disk?

Even by Alpha standards, Nevinyrral's Disk is a powerful card. While the original set had many cards that destroyed a whole class of permanents, from the well-remembered Wrath of God (creatures) and Armageddon (lands) to the more specialized Flashfires (plains), Tsunami (islands), Tranquility (enchantments), Hurricane and Earthquake (flying and non-flying creatures, respectively), only the Disk destroyed multiple types of permanents. There are two other distinctions. While most of these sweepers cost four mana (with Tranquility at ![2 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif), Earthquake and Hurricane variable), the Disk effectively cost five. Additionally, it is a permanent, instead of a spell. This distinction simultaneously strengthens and weakens it. As a permanent, you can invest the mana and leave the Disk in play until it is needed. This is especially good for blue decks, as compared to Wrath of God, which often leaves a blue player with less mana than he or she needs to counter the barrage of creatures a smart opponent has in reserve. With the Disk, the one mana activation cost is very affordable for blue. With five mana up, blue can use the Disk and still have mana for two Counterspells. With that obvious advantage comes the disadvantage inherent in all permanents: your opponent can deal with them before you get full use. Though in today's artifact-light metagames, we don't see a lot of artifact removal, but in past metagames, where the Disk (and the Vise, Orb, Icy and many other artifacts) was prevalent, cards like Disenchant were in every deck. Additionally complicating the Disk's activation is its “comes into play tapped” clause. This little delay gives opponents a chance to counter the effect of the Disk even after it has been played. Once it untaps and “turns on”, it's very unusual to for a player to get another shot at destroying it without forcing its activation.

### When It Was Legal…

Because the Disk has been around forever, only bowing out of Standard in Sixth Edition, and owing to its broad application, the Disk has been a feature in many, many decks. The Disk's most notable appearance probably came in the 1996 Necro deck, epitomized by Mark Justice's and Henry Stern's World Championship decks. I've used Stern's deck for my example of Justice's, because Stern's is slightly more reliant on the Disk than Justice's.

In the early days of Necro, people were beginning to realize how strong it was, but they still hadn't fully grasped how to use the card. These days, everyone understands that you want to get a Necropotence out and use it to draw a bunch of cards until you win the game. Early on, people thought it would be best to remove the Necro after a couple of bursts of cards, going back to your regular draws a little lower on life, but with great board position and a full hand. This is where the Disk comes into play. It can destroy enchantments. Because of that very fact, the Disk served multiple purposes in the Necro deck. In addition to creature removal and Necro disposal, the Disk often served to clear out nasty white enchantments like Circle of Protection: Black, Land Tax and Karma, as well as opposing artifacts, like Serrated Arrows.

The Disk was also heavily played in blue, where it found its most natural home. While Nevinyrral's Disk is a great way to sweep the board, it is just a way to sweep the board. It's important to play the Disk in the same types of decks where you would want a Wrath of God-type effect. You certainly aren't going to want to Disk when you have five creatures in play. The Disk is a blunt instrument, not a precise tool. As such, it was happily adopted by permanent-light, countermagic-heavy blue control decks. And really, there is no feeling quite as good as sitting on an untapped Disk with a hand full of countermagic and Whispers of the Muse. In blue control decks, the Disk serves two purposes. First, the Disk is a great turn four play against a creature rush. Imagine, having your opening barrage of creatures destroyed on turn five, by a blue deck that now has a clear board and four islands untapped. It's not a pretty picture. Not only did you lose card advantage and board position in the early game, you now have to deal with an opponent who is perfectly set up to trade you card-for-card and can easily outdraw you. Blue decks like the late Disk in order to make riskier plays or focus on drawing more cards, rather than countering spells. 

### Why Was It Great?

<table><tbody><tr><td><table><tbody><tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/65/b4/65b44cec90bda938bbb278a79acba1f455a38a19b714807bea68c253b77de30d.jpg"></td><td></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/dd/0a/dd0a617788694ee0e82eed58b0746ab9dbdbdb6e709ee5390dcfb32556a6990e.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/96/f5/96f5d53e3ef958055c4fdf5bf835be99797bd18449a68a882c96ab3801bae612.jpg"></td><td><a><img alt="Compost" src="https://i.ormos.uk/a4/37/a437ea83910bee2b1aebc7c7b0b8cd4e2df17f5ec73b11d372d88281e8e6cd59.jpg"></a></td><td></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/a0/a9/a0a91e69ef087e17e689f758224146196f0a29fad934eb2639d2fb73d4cf3c75.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c0/e2/c0e24bff2fa206011911cb4b5cb20b4559cb82301a77e757b6e86de3c1ae659a.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/fa/ab/faab219b94a5d6a8405ff77aefe6f815ba2bba52fc447652a633eec85e227836.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table><br><i> Imagine having a Disk now…</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Nevinyrral's Disk was huge because of what it represented. I'll let Christopher Carbone explain:

> *“No card in **Magic** is such a pervasive answer to each color's most notorious problems. Black can't handle artifacts? Pop the Disk. Red has trouble removing enchantments? Pop the Disk. Green and white have no creature removal? Pop the Disk. Blue can't deal with permanents? Pop the Disk. The Disk is the bridge allowing whole colors to cheat their own limitations. No card in **Magic** has EVER been such a boon for each color; no mass removal spell has ever been so ‘splashable'.”*

Giving decks that have an otherwise insurmountable weaknesses a way around their problems is the Disk's bread and butter. Monoblue control decks could never have coexisted in a field populated by aggressive, blindingly quick creature decks, if not for the cheap reset button the Disk provided. Even taking into account every blue card ever made, there are very few solutions to a creature rush strategy. Imagine today's monoblack control decks with the Disk available. Black's biggest problem, Compost, would cease to trouble it and those situational Mutilates would turn into a much more potent force. 

### The Big Picture

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>&quot;Every time you have one on the table, you know that things are under your control. The mere threat of popping the Disk can cause players to make foolish mistakes, which the Disk will mercilessly punish.&quot; -Benjamin Huston</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

As **Magic** has evolved into a much more contemplative game, Nevinyrral's Disk has fallen out of favor. The main reason for this is its status as an artifact. Its power level is comparable to Wrath of God, which has always been and is still legal in Standard. But as an artifact, Nevinyrral's Disk is able to slide into any deck, giving every color some limited, but powerful, access to creature, artifact and enchantment removal in a nice package. This has proven to be disruptive to the nature of the game, as it nullifies the primary design methods used to punish colors, especially blue and black. Blue's lack of creature removal should make it rely on another color. The Disk gives blue some powerful creature removal. Enchantments like Compost, Karma and Circle of Protection: Black should give black fits, but with a giant-sized, all-purpose Disenchant (with other great features), those enchantments aren't a huge threat. Overall, the ability of the Disk to unbalance the colors detracts from the game's flavor and that, more than its power level, is why it is no longer Standard legal. 

Nevinyrral's Disk, with its theme of blowing up the world, has been mimicked many times. The closest iterations have been permanents. Powder Keg, released in *Urza's Destiny*, gave players a cheap, limited Disk. Though it can't remove enchantments, the Keg does hit creatures and artifacts. It's half the cost of the Disk, but it has a drawback to boot: it only hits creatures and artifacts of a single casting cost, and you have to build up counters to hit the cost. That drawback wasn't enough to keep Powder Keg from being one of the most influential artifacts of its time (and that was a time when artifacts were really, really dangerous). More recently, *Apocalypse* introduced Pernicious Deed. For ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif), you got an enchantment that was basically a Disk, but you had to pay ![X Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/b1/6bb1152b0a3305d0a7c2e2eb725b8ebd18620607efc62f6f38b0642b97c8501f.gif), essentially requiring you to pay the casting cost of anything you wanted to destroy. This seems like a drawback, but it really ended up being more of a benefit. You could use it in conjunction with high casting cost spells and wipe out their side of the board, leaving yours untouched (or sometimes, just with a regenerating Spiritmonger in play). The other variants of the Disk have taken it from its status as a permanent and converted it into a sorcery. Most recently, the Disk has popped up as a white sorcery, Akroma's Vengeance. The Vengeance wipes the board for ![4 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/36/29/3629288966daa042decf01d3752789ab6676fddd67c3679fa580cd3baecae158.gif)![White Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif)![White Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif), but, unlike the Disk, it requires the whole payment up front and during your main phase. 

Nevinyrral's Disk is not a great candidate for reprinting in the current field. With modern day **Magic's** emphasis on the colors' strengths and weaknesses, the Disk strongly ignores the limitations of the colors. Though its overall power level is not super-duper, like some of the cards I've featured in this column, it does have quite a bit of power and certainly a great deal of influence on any given metagame, strengthening blue and black decks, especially.
