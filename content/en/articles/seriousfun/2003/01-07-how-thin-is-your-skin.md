The "morph trigger" abilities are the latest variation on "ability-tweaking" that Wizards likes to do with its sets. Because readers have now seen Stone Rain and Dark Banishing versions of morph creatures, I doubt many of the other commons in the set will surprise folks. (Of course, not all of these morph-trigger cards will be common! Hold onto your hat.) What I'd like to do this week is talk a bit about how this new generation of morphers affects casual and group play as a whole—and then we'll swing by and take a closer look at He Who Thins Skins. 

### MY, HOW YOU'VE BLOSSOMED

In an expansion that pushes innovative creatures and creature abilities, the "when this is turned face up" mechanic for morph creatures is perhaps the most intriguing. It's a pretty slick way of equipping this expansion with familiar utility, without feeling like you're covering old ground. Turning a morph creature face up (or what I'll now call "coming of age") is not at all like coming into play. There are at least two differences between morphing and coming into play, as far as the vast majority of group players care. 

*   A face-down creature usually has to withstand a round of play face down before the mana is available to flip it over, which can mean surviving up to a dozen unfriendly players.
*   These cards will never work optimally in an Equilibrium-Aluren deck.

That said, having two or three creatures face down on the board with free mana is now not unlike having Aluren in play and a few cards in your hand—given the right conditions, you can set off quite a few effects at instant speed. 

This can play one of two different ways, depending on how paranoid your **Magic** friends are. On one hand, you may find that you get a bit more respect when you have a face-down creature in front of you—you're sending a signal that you may be able to wax a creature or a land (or do something else nasty), and so if all other things are equal, people may avoid you. 

On the other hand, the fact that morph creatures may have an effect immediately upon turning over makes "Morph Nation" a bit more of a threat. Exalted Angel and Blistering Firecat were scary enough; now all sorts of things can happen outside the combat phase. Every face-down creature will get a deeper frown from each player who has something to lose. 

My true and—rumor-mongerers take note—*completely uninformed* hope is that the *Onslaught* block will have morph-trigger creatures that account for Shock and Mutilate. Perhaps some will have triggered abilities that activate upon hitting the graveyard, or a few will come back to your hand easily. Normally, even three expansions aren't enough to explore all the neat things you can do with a mechanic—witness cycling—but a built-in tool to give opponents more pause before they Smother these poor little fellas would be handy. 

In whatever way this all plays out, tapping out for morph creatures will be riskier, and keeping mana open for your critters on the board will be smarter. Once again, slower group games, in which mana has time to build up, benefits more from morph than, say, Standard Constructed environments. 

### MATURE, RINSE, REPEAT

Just as with comes-into-play effects, a natural temptation will be to get one of these morph creatures to repeat its ability multiple times. My gut tells me this path won't be worth following for any reason other than scientific curiosity (and who am I to impede the reckless tide of progress?), especially as repeatable effects are the firmly held territory of comes-into-play creatures like Flametongue Kavu and Bone Shredder. The process of generating and spending mana to play a creature face down, then to flip it over, and then to flip it back down as many times as necessary seems hopelessly complex. Graveyard recursion is even worse. 

Now, if there's a morph creature or two out there in the *Legions* set that can do something an existing comes-into-play creature can't do, then we might be onto something. *(Twiddling thumbs with innocent expression.)* But, I expect most players will get more out of their maturing morphs if they play them differently from how they've played in the past. Think land mines, not machine guns.

### PLAYING MORPH: THE NEXT GENERATION

Which brings me to my recommendations on how you tackle this new generation. My advice includes the assumption that you and your group will be equally likely to play the original *Onslaught* creatures with morph as you would these new arrivals: 

**1) Morphs that do not attack early have more cover.** The *Onslaught* set gave us a few creatures that we'd like to turn over as soon as possible, like Skirk Commando and Exalted Angel, but it also gave us some that are openly looking to bait opponents, like Disruptive Pitmage and Quicksilver Dragon. This latest generation of morphs helps the second batch more than the first. Why? Consider this: you have a face-down Silent Specter (with or without the mana to turn it over). Do you (a) attack with it and bait someone into destroying it, or (b) keep it back, and let people guess your capabilities? Most people will choose (b), even with morph mana ready, until the late game. Now, put a face-down Skinthinner next to that face-down Specter. Does it act any differently? Not really. It'll hang, attacking only where there's a clear opening (and probably only in retaliation) and waiting for a chance to take big game down. 

In sum, it's easier to disguise a Skinthinner as a passive creature than as an active one. That means a deck using such a creature would find more synergy in reactive, not proactive, strategies. Put even more simply: think control, not aggression. I believe most readers will find the same is true of the other comes-of-age morph creatures in the *Legions* set, but of course nothing is absolute—control cards often find homes in aggressive decks. Just be aware of what you're doing, and why. 

**2) Math is hard.** Haste is one of those abilities that doesn't get enough respect in multiplayer—it throws off combat math and does valuable, unanticipated damage to overconfident opponents. Comes-of-age is another mechanic that I'm not sure will get the right amount of respect, but here it is: you and Opponent B get into a tiff midgame of a five-player chaos. Both Opponent B and you are sitting at 2 life. Opponent B decides that it doesn't matter if he or she loses, as long as you get taken down. Opponent B attacks with three Grizzly Bears to your two face-down creatures. 

Before the *Legions* set, you would probably have been out for the count—with the exception of Ironfist Crusher or Daru Healer, your creatures wouldn't be able to fend off enough of the assault. But now you have more options—with something like Skinthinner, you can flash it to nail a creature before (or after) you block a different one. 

This scenario works on offense, too: an opponent holds back a creature, thinking it will stop your single face-down attacker. But after you declare attackers, you flip the Skinthinner and watch your opponent's hopes fall, as he or she can't avoid the last 2 lethal damage. 

**3) Pyroclasm and Infest are still good.** It's an obvious and cardinal rule of multiplayer that mass creature removal is helpful. (Note that at least two *Legions* cards may make the strategy a bit less reliable—but the central point holds. I'll get to those cards later in the month.) The question is, how good does such removal need to be, and can you get away with something cheaper than Plague Wind? 

With many casual groups looking to make use of as many new cards as they can, read the number "2" in the rules text of the card as "good enough." Two damage, -2/-2, it's all the same to you—it kills morphs dead. Remember that if you have someone who likes white, Massacre is probably superior to Infest (and will help you leave mana open to morph your own stuff)! 

**4) There is a new window for response.** While turning a creature face up doesn't use the stack, the triggered ability that happens "when this card is turned face up" *does*. This means that if someone plays Cruel Revival on your Skinthinner when it's face down (recall face-down creatures are typeless in that state), you can respond by turning it face up. So, as a Zombie it won't die—but when you announce to your opponent that his or her Blind Seer is the target of the Skinthinner's ability, your opponent can activate the Seer's ability to turn it black and invalidate your target. (So bottom line for this scenario is that nothing happens, though your opponent can still bring back a Zombie card from his or her graveyard as part of Cruel Revival's effect. If any of this confuses you, email Rune for his Saturday column. . . and tell 'im Anthony sent ya for free coupons worth up to $5 at your local Kiss the Judge booth!) 

Speaking of rules, make sure you and your group know the answers to the following questions: 

*   When can I respond to a morph-trigger ability?
*   If the face-down creature leaves play (for example, Astral Slide) and then comes back into play face up, does that trigger a morph-trigger ability?
*   If a face-down creature turns face up, does turning it back face down at instant speed (for example, Backslide) prevent the morph-triggered ability from resolving?

If you're not completely sure of all three—or any other rules questions you might have about *Legions* cards—just keep on eye on **MagicTheGathering.com** over the next few weeks. The *Legions* FAQ will be released in a couple of weeks, and you can send questions to Rune for his Saturday column. Your local judges are also a great source of answers. Checking the rules resources at your disposal is a good habit to get into, and more useful to you than if I just spouted out the answers. It's easier to have fun when you're not quibbling or confused about the rules! 

Okay, because it's just plain mean to leave anyone hanging, the answers are: while the triggered ability is on the stack (see Cruel Revival example above); no, because the creature wasn't in play when it was turned face up; and no, because once an ability triggers, there's no way to stop it from going on the stack.

### HOW MANY SKINS WOULD A SKINTHINNER THIN IF A SKINTHINNER COULD THIN SKINS?

One of the surprising things about Zombies to date is that they haven't had a really good single-card executioner among their legions. Sure, there's been conditional kill (such as Zombie Boa), and trick cards like Tombstone Stairwell that might set up a nice cascade, but the best straight-out killers these undead have been able to manage so far are Plaguebearer, who has a hard time against the big boys, and Zombie Assassin—hardly inspiring! As a result, Zombie-themed decks have had to resort to barely-in-theme kill like Terror and Dregs of Sorrow.

But no longer! Your group can now disallow such thematic mediocrity. (Or you can raise the bar yourself, champ.) On the heels of the instant Cruel Revival, you now get a creature card that can buttress your creature control and give you the theme deck you've always wanted. Here's where I'd start:

My thought here is to start with the tried-and-true Sarcomancy-Umbilicus Zombie engine, and then find all the different ways in which Skinthinner might be useful. As a Zombie, it helps you fight Sarcomancy's drawback, helps the mana denial strategy of Zombie Trailblazer, and is a candidate in your graveyard for Cruel Revival. As a morph creature, it's indistinguishable from Haunted Cadaver. As creature kill, it can clear a path for an alpha strike using Zombie Trailblazer and/or Dark Triumph.

Theme purists will have to pull out Umbilicus (for recursion like Aphetto Vulture or Lord of the Undead), Dark Triumph (for mass creature generation like Zombie Infestation), and possibly even Drain Life (for Shepherd of Rot). Of course, I'm only hitting the monoblack choices, and only a fraction of them at that. From Feast or Famine to Soulless One, there are more Zombie cards than ever out there—each will bear the unique mark of its own, personal Dr. Frankenstein. (If you go green, don't forget Strength of Night!)

### BEYOND EATING BRAINS

Even if Skinthinner wasn't a Zombie, it would still be useful—it does, after all, kill. Draft and Sealed enthusiasts, whether casual or tournament, would do well to pick this card fairly high—you're not going to see a ton of spot removal in the *Legions* set that's better than this. It's a fine surprise the first time you flip it, and a decent threat thereafter. In a casual Constructed deck (it's probably too slow to bring to a Constructed tournament), I would recommend finding a way to fake people out—perhaps by slipping a Grinning Demon or two into your deck, letting one Skinthinner do its job and die, recurring it to your hand, and then playing out the 6/6 in disguise. 

And bear in mind that there's nothing particularly wrong with a 2/1 for two mana (or a 2/2 for three), in a pinch. In group play, trading it in combat for another creature lets it do its job anyway, when you're short on mana—so don't hold it back for too long! 

Bottom line, don't expect the Skinthinner to last long. It gets out, does its job, trades for a card, and then it usually dies, in combat or in retaliation. Recursion can feed the beast for only so long—as I implied earlier, Bone Shredder will often be a superior choice for those who want to spot-kill repeatedly. Skinthinner is about nuance—finding the right time to morph, nailing the right creature(s) for the right price, and sending a signal to the rest of the board that you've got game. 

Finally, Skinthinner gets my Fine Writing Award™ for card title in this set. (I'd list the other nominees, but Aaron will kill me if I slip too much more into this article.) It evokes a great image for a card that can fit into multiple creative decks. Thin skins don't last long in this game, and players who take that lesson to heart have a new weapon.
