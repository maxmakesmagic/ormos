It's a classic problem for **Magic** theory that the game changes so frequently. This is not to say that dynamic elements and mechanics are a weakness of the game - rather I think it is one of **Magic's** most attractive and sustaining attributes - but it sure makes talking about something like Booster Draft, as a static entity, difficult. Still, being not only a **Magic** devotee (a title I get to use as opposed to “addict” because here I am, sharing my ideas instead of doing another draft), as well as self-righteous and confrontational, I'd like to let loose some of my ideas on Booster Drafting *Onslaught* Block.

First of all, despite all the bickering about Timberwatch Elf and Sparksmith, this is a great format. I think only *Invasion* Block compares in just how carefully decks need to be tailored and built on a case-by-case basis. I've seen many interesting ideas tried, and a lot of creativity rewarded. Most prominent is the difference I notice now, between a first-rate player (say 1900 rating and up) and a second rater (say 1700), in how much more sensitive a better player is to the needs of the particular deck she/he is drafting. More than ever, picks depend on which cards you have in your pile, not on some immutable or trendy pick order. Never before have I seen picks depend so much on the archetype being drafted, with cards like Mistform Wall being 2nd or 3rd picks in some archetypes, and relegated to the sideboard in others. On that note, I have some ideas to put forth on drafting a few of my favorite archetypes.

First is black-blue, a coupling of colors packed with opportunities for odd synergies and deceptively strong combinations - many of them resulting from cards not highly valued by other archetypes. To clarify, I do not mean to discuss zombies with a splash of blue in this article. That's a perfectly viable deck to aim for, and one that I draft when the opportunity presents itself, but it's hardly the same as talking about my usual black-blue deck.

So how would I classify black-blue? Well, it depends, but generally it's more of a control deck; not looking to overpower right away, rather I intend to stall the ground and fight in the air. Unlike some decks, this one has access to answers to most problems (Tethers/Dirge for hordes of blockers, bounce and Terrors for oversized creatures, removal for ‘Smiths and Elves), and can even be offensive on the ground until the opponent gets his larger creatures into play. For these reasons, fliers and quick damage sources are not as valuable here as in a deck like white-blue soldiers where racing is often the only solution to problems like Sparksmith and other unremovable cards that can rarely be beaten in a long-term plan. I consider the goal of this deck to be a three step process:

1.  Get control of the ground
2.  Establish an advantage in card economy or board position. This could mean drawing cards with a Rush of Knowledge or threatening unkillable blockers with a Crypt Sliver. It could also means something temporal like Essence Fracturing a few creatures, or just having a better mana curve than the opponent. The point is that you don't want to race until you have the advantage. Until then, play defense.
3.  Attack back before the opponent can use any bombs that you can't deal with, or before you are beaten with a Timberwatch Elf or Sparksmith.

Now, don't let me give you the impression that each draft turns out the same, but I find my black-blue decks often playing along these lines. To further augment the way this deck ought to be drafted and played, let's look at what I consider the proper pick orders for each set.

There are commons I didn't list here - it's because they are cards I seldom play. As far as my pick order, I hope it goes without saying that these depend on the peculiarities of your deck in the draft so far. The key, I think, is balance. Cards like Mistform Wall, Zombie Cutthroat and Raven Guild Initiate are particularly important in this deck, but do you really need six of them? I would play all six, but they certainly go down in value as you get more of them. You may have noticed that I rate removal very highly, putting Swat over Nantuko Husk. This is because removal outside of red is very scarce, and there are definitely creatures you will want to remove. You may have also noted how high Crypt Sliver is, he's there for good reason. Combined with Mistforms, Imagecrafter (a very underrated creature that, again valued for his low casting cost, will be sure to have a substantial effect on the game in your favor) and whatever uncommon and rare slivers you can get, he can totally dominate a game. And with the very low amount of available two drops, I find him, Lingering Death and Goblin Turncoat (as well as the occasional face-up Skinthinner) invaluable to augment an aggressive draw, or quell your opponent's early rush.

It should also be apparent how much better *Scourge* is for you than *Legions*, and, considering the small size of the expansions, how much better *Legions* is than *Onslaught*. To draft this deck means starting modestly and having faith that you will be rewarded later in the draft. Speaking of the small size of the expansions (which means that getting a particular card is not so bad to rely on, notice how much more often you lose to Timberwatch Elf than Sparksmith), I think it's worth listing how I feel about the uncommons in *Legions* and *Scourge* - especially since you've probably had less opportunities to try these cards out than the commons.

3) Cabal Interrogator - If you get removal and quick defensive cards, this guy will take the game over. Get your defenses set up (somewhat modestly), then go to work on their hand. It's hard to lose if this gets started early.

I hope the message of how to build this deck comes across in my lists. Draft for the early game, and knock ‘em down before they get a chance to be in it. Remember that if you go to the late game, cards like Wellwisher, Timberwatch Elves, Sparksmith, Needleshot Gourna and countless uncommons and rares are a nearly uncontrollable nuisance. So rather than trying to stock up on Frozen Solids (clunky and unreliable answers, to be sure), race! Pound for pound, no deck can match u-w's efficiency and synergy when it comes to creature combat. You might wonder about why Rush of Knowledge is so low in my pick order, but consider what situations merit this expensive card drawing spell. One is a stalemate, something that white-blue desperately wants to avoid. Why? Because as good as your creatures are, very few of them break stalemates. And spending a turn to draw three or four cards with Rush of Knowledge probably won't save you quite like it does in other decks. Now don't get me wrong, refueling is nice, and sometimes excellent, but what you will usually be drawing into is land and creatures, and if you weren't winning with those already, there's a good chance more of the same won't help you. Another reason you might need to draw cards is in a battle of attrition. But the truth of the matter is that wars of attrition don't often take place for this deck. Your creatures simply don't die! Very rarely will you trade, instead their men have to get out of the way of yours until one of them is big enough that you can't attack through it. This is when you need something to break through. Deftblade Elite, Daru Stinger, Echo Tracer, Aven Redeemer, Gempalm Avenger, Pacifism, Piety Charm, Choking Tethers and Dragon Scales are the best solutions, and that is why they are such high picks.
