A 2/2 flyer for 4 mana is nothing new for the last couple of blocks. Even the much-maligned color white can show us an Aurora Griffin... and not even your Wash Out theme deck would play one of those. If we walk back a few years, red had the Dragon Whelp, a card that would carry eventual Fire God David Price to a PT-Paris qualification, but that color hasn't had an evasion threat of comparable cost in years. With six or fewer cards in the yard, Fledgling Dragon isn't even a bad Dragon Whelp, and for the current Standard for red creatures and four mana... it certainly isn't a 4/2 creature with type "Kavu".

So obviously the Fledgling Dragon was meant to exploit its threshold ability. 

There are two ways for red to efficiently go with the threshold plan. The first one is Book Burning. While I think Book Burning is one of the best and most interesting red spells in some time, I am instead going to discuss the other route, which is to pair Fledgling Dragon with blue.

The u-r deck in question is going to want to finish with our efficient Fledgling Dragon; the blue component is going to be dedicated to putting cards in our graveyard as quickly as possible, and to help keep us alive while we race to threshold. The most efficient way for a u-r deck to dump seven cards into the graveyard is to utilize blue's instant cards, not just reactive permission spells, but cantrip cards. The very nature of cantrip cards, of quickly looking at more cards than usual at the cost of 1-2 blue mana, not only helps us to get to threshold, but it helps us to "cheat" on lands, playing fewer than we normally would for a long game deck of this style.

### The Page Turned Back

The first great cantrip deck was played to the finals of the 1997 Southern California Regional Championships:

Turbo Xerox 

Alan Comer

You will note that Comer's deck plays only 17 lands. This is because he had two huge advantages. The first one is that in the early game, his full compliment of Impulses, Portents, and Foreshadows allowed him to find islands for the first 3-4 turns. On turn 4, having looked at many more than 11 cards on average, he would be able to tap out just once to play Waterspout Djinn (hopefully with Force of Will backup). The Waterspout Djinn would mean that Comer could - actually had to - stay at 4 lands for the rest of the game. The fact that he was less likely to draw lands with only 17 in his deck would now become an advantage. He would only have to attack five turns because of the Waterspout Djinn's incredible efficiency. Meanwhile all that library manipulation would then be used to keep finding "answers"... in Comer's deck even generally weak permission like Power Sink were thrown in. He would be looking at so few lands and so many answers with all those cantrips that playing 1-for-1 with permission - almost any permission it seems - for five turns was a very strong plan.

Now Comer had a lot of other advantages in his deck as well... He could generate tons of cards in hand with cute tricks like Portent + Foreshadow and Memory Lapse + Foreshadow. He could paralyze a green deck with his Dream Tides, or go "machine gun" with Dream Tides and Suq'Ata Firewalkter. But for our purposes, the lesson of Turbo Xerox is twofold: for every 4 cantrips in your deck, you can play 2 fewer lands, and a fast clock is synergistic with a redundant combination of answer cards and quick card drawing over the course of the short number of turns required to win (usually via race, rather than total board control).

The Comer Xerox principle was later improved by players like Jon Finkel and Brian Schneider, specifically with the printing of a persistent source of card advantage - Ophidian - in *Weatherlight*, and has been resurrected to varying degrees of success several times over the years.

Most recently, Brian Kibler gave us Enforcer-Go at the San Diego Masters Gateway tournament.

Enforcer-Go 

Brian Kibler

At 23 lands, Enforcer-Go plays many more lands than Comer's simple 17 islands... but then again, Brian had to support three colors and had many more expensive cards in his deck, including 4 mana spells like Fact or Fiction and Wrath of God. Furthermore, he would never be in a position to tap out for a 6/6 Mystic Enforcer on turn 4 with Force of Will backup... it's just not 1997 any more. You can nevertheless see how a deck like this is still playing "land-light", and like our forthcoming example with Fledgling Dragon, is using its blue cards to get an off-color finishing card to threshold.

Builds like Enforcer-Go have subsequently been replaced in Standard by the ubiquitous "best deck" ZevAtog. Control decks need to plug all predictable holes that the environment presents or a threat will get through and kill them, and Repulse as the only spot removal is just not good enough in a format with Spellbane Centaur and Yavimaya Barbarian cropping up as anti-Psychatog strategies; ZevAtog answers by making the opponent pick up all of his permanents. Furthermore, Mystic Enforcer plays a poor game-ender in a format where Chainer's Edict comes with Duress and Mesmeric Fiend cover support, especially when itself lacking Upheaval.

So why should our Fledgling Dragon threshold deck fare any better in Standard than the now-defunct Enforcer-Go?

The first thing you are probably asking yourself is "Where are the two best cards in Standard?" Yes, this deck, despite being both blue and red, lacks both Fact or Fiction and Flametongue Kavu. This time around I wanted to go the cantrip/threshold route, and it came down to Compulsion vs. Fact or Fiction going into the long game; for this deck there were too many incentives for Compulsion to ignore it (the red spells specifically), and in a deck with only 21 lands, Fact or Fiction seemed a bit expensive, especially in the opening hand.

On the other hand, Flametongue Kavu would be awful in a deck so creature-poor. More often than not, it would simply give the opponent an opportunity to Flametongue back, negating any tempo or card advantage that most mighty of Kavu had generated for this deck. Nevertheless, this deck runs the double duty cantrip and early game Birds of Paradise/Merfolk Looter-slaying-machine Fire/Ice, and has a ton of additional answers going into the long game.

The reason I think this deck might be competitive in post-*Judgment* Standard is because it plugs the holes of the Enforcer-Go deck, and aggressively goes after decks that Kibler's design didn't, at the time, even have to consider. If the opponent is running Spellbane Centaur and Yavimaya Barbarian, this deck can easily deal with them... and will many times draw a card while doing so. Against Braids with Chainer's Edict, this deck can both protect the Dragon with its 12 efficient counters, or just go the burn route, plowing into the opposing deck's life total and simultaneously digging for more direct damage with Compulsion.

With Judgment, we can also expect a resurgent green fatty plan, something we haven't seen since the Fat Crank decks that came out right after Odyssey was released. Red removal is historically weak against animals sized 4/5, to 6/6, to 7/7... and we can certainly anticipate oversized threats from an invigorated cadre of g-w, g-r or even g-u creature decks. While even the mighty Violent Eruption seems ineffective against such enormous questions, quick red removal is historically great against the 0/1 and 1/1 team that pumps out the fatties, giving us a precious turn or two to erect the permission wall. At that point, the beatdown player can expect to walk into Memory Lapse + Predict, or just 2-for-1 goodness in the form of Circular Logic. If an enormous animal or so gets through, the presence of Repulse will certainly be welcome. As bad a defense as relying only on Repulse and its kin can be against certain green creature decks, one mixing up both blue and red board control should be effective against any deck relying on creature beatdown.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/ae/a4/aea46a5a41ae2f0a88914a71e7e1648b206f4959b39a04e7dc6a17aa9fd57995.jpg)

Last, but not least, there is the matter of ZevAtog. What can I say about "the best deck" besides always watch the Grudge Match listings? Spring after Spring, the best Standard decks seem to be born from Neutral Ground's side of this competitive series. The next time a ZevAtog player with a powerful, proven, deck list slams down turn-2 Standstill and grins, play Compulsion back and see what happens. Especially in a deck with so many counters, and the ability to play so many end of turn threat spells, the "free Ancestral" you just gave the ZevAtog player is going to pale in comparison to your ability to set up over the next several turns. Once you activate Compulsion a few times, he can forget about lifting all the permanents in play to ensure a Psychatog kill.

And let's not forget the guy who got this ball rolling. Fledgling Dragon in the midgame will take down literally any opposing creature not named Spiritmonger in a fight, and will often live to tell the tale. Especially with burn and permission backup, he will fly over many a frustrated mage's team of blockers, and race almost any opposing threat (though let's face it... a post-UpheavalPsychatog beats everyone).

By no means do I think this alpha build is the best deck post *Judgment* (or maybe even the best u-r deck), but I hope it gets your creative juices flowing, and inspires you to take a look at the impressive new monster that is Fledgling Dragon.
