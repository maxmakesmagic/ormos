**Magic** isn't just about the current block. It's a game, deep and complex, with trees of possibility, tactical maneuvers, and strategic considerations. A lot of people talk about luck's role in **Magic**, and I think I understand why.

![](http://www.wizards.com/images/magic.jpg)

It's pretty much accepted that in the classic game of rock-paper-scissors, only luck is involved. But many would also be surprised to learn that there are people in the world that can beat you and almost everyone else 80% of the time. Maybe not 4 times out of 5, but in that very important long run, you have already lost.

So leave whatever preconceptions you may have on the doorstep, because in my world, when you lose it's your fault. Did you get too many bad matchups? Pick a different deck. Too many bad draws? Mulligan more. Sit next to a hate-drafter in Rochester? Adapt.

This is all crap right? Jon Finkel loses to Gary Wendelhaven of Iowa sometimes. But it isn't luck you see. Everyone has the same factors affecting them. If you have played 100 matches, people will know exactly how many of your losses are due to luck, and how many of them you screwed up yourselves. No one cares if you got mana-screwed.

That's the trick. Ignoring luck. That's how you get better. **Magic** is a game of plateaus. People who get caught up in luck blind themselves to the increasingly complex elements of the game.

To truly study the game, one needs a certain attitude. Never complain about mana screw, even to yourself. You must not care. Care about winning, not about losing. Don't tell people you lost because you got unlucky. Think about the Might of Oaks that you should have played around or the hand you should have mulliganed, or the hand you should have kept. Concentrate on what you can control. Always look at your opponent at the end of any game you lose, and say good game. And mean it. 

Here is a glimpse at how to do just that:

This is not organized in order of importance, but each one will build off the other until we have a full picture of **Magic**.

We will define safety as the odds that you have of losing the game at any given time.  
 We start with safety for a couple of reasons. First and foremost, you can't win a game when you are dead. Secondly, however, most people worry too much about safety.

The components of safety include life total, opposing deck configuration, and board position.

The lower your life total, the more likely your opponent is to kill you at any given time. In the extreme case, if you are sitting on one life, any deck has a good chance of killing you. Red can burn you out, black can use some kind of drain effect, blue can use some kind of Tim effect or enchantment like Hermetic Study, green can give a creature trample, and white can give a creature protection from a color for that last point. And if you ever get outnumbered in creatures, you are doomed. 

It seems realistic then that life is worth at least some level of tradeoff.

Now, how important this is depends a great deal on your opponent's deck and the current board position. Against a red deck, the board matters less because your opponent can just burn you out at any given time. That is why you must sacrifice more resources for safety against red.

In terms of the other colors, you can often sacrifice life for a favorable board since they usually must attack for the win. 

That's just it: safety is a resource to be traded off for other advantages. If your opponent is mounting an attack of four 3/3's against your four 2/2's, you must make a decision on whether to chump block them all, or try and double block and trade in some life.

It's also important to realize that life is valued on an exponential scale. That first point is worth a much less than the last point. Therefore, you should be eager to trade in your first few life points for an advantage in tempo or card advantage. But flashing back Deep Analysis is a lot more costly going from 7 to 4 than 20 to 17.

Too many players chump block far too early because they are afraid of going from 20 to 15. You should wait for a better opportunity usually.

Guard your life total, but be ready to turn it into some high-reward dynamic benefit later. Maybe sometimes you want to take a few hits from their creatures while you spend some time activating a Cephalid Looter. There's something very beautiful about a game of **Magic** if you fully understand what's going on.

Some see one player attacking, and one person Looting. Then they see the player who was Looting start to dominate.

What's really going on though is one person is funneling their resources into cards and then turning the cards back into life advantage later. It's really a very intricate resource war. A true master of the game will look at a game, and see exactly when the game is won and lost. Imagine watching a tennis match. Some spectators see a great serve and a few volleys, before the server puts the opposing player away. Really, the point was decided on the serve, when the one player was put just a little off-balance. Many hitter and pitcher matches are won on the first pitch too.
