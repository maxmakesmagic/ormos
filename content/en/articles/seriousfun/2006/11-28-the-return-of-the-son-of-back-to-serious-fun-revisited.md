The beautiful but devious MaryJanice is watching television with me. Well, not so much watching – we each have our laptops out and are working on our respective books. Our children, meanwhile, are cleaning out the garage and moving patio furniture inside. The dog is alternately running between their legs and rushing off to sniff sidewalk strangers. It's what we call “family time.”

After a couple of hours of near-total silence, she breaks the mood. “So I'll bet you're glad you have all this free time to do your book writing,” she says. “You never could get that much done when you were doing weekly articles for Wizards.”

“Actually, I'm doing an article for Wizards next week.”

She shakes her head as if trying to lose an aftertaste, but keeps typing. “Next week? I thought you were done.”

“I am. But I'm coming back next week.”

The keyboard tapping stops. “Coming back?! What, you just left and now you're going to start up again?”

![](http://www.wizards.com/global/images/mtgcom_daily_tf10_picMain_en.jpg)“Not forever,” I assured her. “Just for one week.” Seeing her blank stare, I went on to explain. “It's Back to the Future Week next week. Or maybe it's Blast from the Past. Or Thank Goodness They Don't Write Here Anymore Week. One of those.”

“So after this week, you're done.”

I cough. “Well, you know. Scott may re-run one of my articles at the end of the year, which means I'd have to supply an intro or two…”

“So at what point does not writing for Wizards actually entail *not writing* for Wizards?”

“I'm thinking 2007.”

She sighs, a sure sign a change of topic is on the way. Her fingers begin sliding back over the keyboard. “So who's doing your column nowadays. You know, when you're not doing it *for* him?”

“The Ferrett.”

She nods, pretending that doesn't sound ridiculous. “And does this Ferrett do a good job?”

“See for yourself.” I call up the web site and flip my laptop around so she can see it.

She squints at it for a while, and then finally shakes her head. “First, that cartoon isn't funny. Second, it has nothing to do with **Magic**. What the hell is ‘[Home on the Strange](https://web.archive.org/web/20061128000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.homeonthestrange.com&origin=mtgcom_daily_tf10),' anyway?”

“Whoops! Wrong web page.” I spell out the right one in the address bar. “Okay, try this one.”

After a few minutes of scrolling, she sighs. “Well, I suppose.”

“You don't like it?”

“I don't like **Magic**,” she points out. 

“Fair enough. But surely the writing itself – ”

“Beats yours like a moldy tarp,” she interrupted. “But really, is that the point? He's not writing next week. You are. So just do your best, honey. I'm sure the readers won't be *too* disappointed.”

Energized from her pep talk, I set to work immediately upon the article…

…by getting the our family pet to kick things off.

Well, after Turquoise demurred, I tried a succession of other potential substitute authors. As I've said in the past…I *am* pretty busy. So I thought of all the people who might be available and asked them. Rejections followed from Mark Rosewater (“busy” running a contest of some sort), Donald Rumsfeld (too expensive), the entire Minnesota Vikings offense (fumbled it), and even the apparently ungrateful staff I supervise at the Minnesota Department of Employment and Economic Development (unionized). 

![Jovens_Ferrets](http://www.wizards.com/magic/images/cardart/HM/Jovens_Ferrets.jpg)That left just me, so here I am. Now, I know a bunch of people out there will be disappointed. After all, this is the guy whose friend moved out to Ohio…and lost to The Ferrett's play group! The Ferrett! Geez, he used to have a guy in his play group named *Sheldon*. How tough can this crowd possibly be? Pretty darn tough, it turns out. Apparently, four or five of them are enough, when put all together like some sort of inept, creaky Voltron, to beat one of my friends. (Now, I've never enjoyed The Ferrett's hospitality, and he's never enjoyed mine. But I can assure you that were he or one of his friends to come north for a visit, why, we'd roll out a red carpet and let him win every game. Really!)

But I'll try to make it all better by introducing a new casual format. As a bonus, it's a format where Stuffy Doll, the latest and greatest multiplayer weapon, can shine.

### Stuff It!

*The basic idea*: There is a Grand Melee in play at all times. All creatures have vigilance. Whenever a creature attacks or blocks, its controller reveals the top card of his or her deck and then puts that card either on the bottom of their deck or in their graveyard. That creature gets +X/+X, where X is the converted cost of that card.

*The expectation*: With all creatures attacking every turn, a Stuffy Doll should have lots of work to do! There are certainly plenty of other creatures that shine in combat, if you have no Dolls. But you should get four anyway.

*Suggested deck restrictions*: While the format can probably work fine in most groups without modification, some groups will try harder than others to circumvent the spirit of the event. In such cases, I recommend allowing only creatures and lands in decks. (Note that doing this not only eliminates the threat of something like Ensnaring Bridge, but also reduces the chances of a -X/-X effect getting rid of a Stuffy Doll.)

*Strategic notes*: Assuming you're following the more restrictive creature/land decks, I would focus on two types of creatures: those that can influence combat, and those that can remove other creatures (and perhaps bring them back into play, e.g. a Stuffy Doll reset).

In the first category (combat tricks), I'll remind readers of choices such as Rimehorn Aurochs and Living Inferno. In the second category (removal and resets), I think simple tricks like Man-o'-War and Faceless Butcher would suffice.

As far as lands go, consider the newly “timeshifted” Safe Haven.

*Other variants*: If just making creatures bigger during combat is not enough for you, you can raise the stakes by adding any one (or more) of the following rules. Note to [Great Designer Search](/en/articles/greatdesignersearch/undated/the-great-designer-search) survivors – avoid these ideas at all costs! I didn't score nearly as well as any of you on the [Designer Quiz](/en/articles/feature/2006/10-09-the-great-designer-search-the-test)…

*   At the beginning of each player's upkeep, that player reveals the top card of his or her library, loses X life where X is the converted mana cost of the revealed card, and then chooses up to X opponents. In play order, those opponents take turns sacrificing non-token creatures until X creatures have been sacrificed.
*   At the end of each player's turn, that player chooses an opponent who lost no life this turn. That player loses X life, where X is the number of creature cards put into the graveyard from anywhere this turn.
*   Whenever a creature card is put into a graveyard, that creature's controller searches his or her library for a creature card, puts it into play, loses life equal to that card's converted mana cost, and then chooses an opponent to do the same.

That should be enough to get groups to explore and discover new twists they can add on their own. As always – if you want to spend the time overthinking this format and finding all the different things that can go wrong, feel free to generate your own perfect construct and share it on the message boards.

Meanwhile, I'm moving on to a new topic, while I still have the floor!

### What About Teferi?

One of the more potent discussions that has come up on the Serious Fun message boards since I signed off with a Stuffy Doll preview has been about Teferi, Mage of Zhalfir. The discussion boils down to this: Is Teferi too powerful to be a “fun” multiplayer card?

Those of you who have read Serious Fun regularly should already know my take on this. But it never hurts to remind everyone, and since I have this golden opportunity to reintroduce myself to everyone… well, here are the important parts of my multiplayer philosophy to remember, as they apply to Teferi:

1.  **Unless a card shuts down a mana base altogether, opponents should save their energy for finding solutions instead of whining.** This is about whether Teferi is “fun” because it stops opponents from doing what they want to do. What I'm struggling with is the idea that I might run across opponent with a deck full of cards that are trying to be *helpful* to me. Folks, *every* card in my opponent's deck is trying to get in my way. One card might make my job difficult by rendering my instants impotent. Another might make my job difficult by disallowing an attack or block. Another might make my job difficult by killing my favorite creature, or putting something like a 20/20 out, or hitting me in the head for 15 damage. Teferi is not substantially different from any of these cards.
     I suspect that what's at the bottom of this argument, past all the false rhetoric about “having fun,” is a basic human emotion: People tend to have more fun when they win, or at least when they have enough time to play out their strategy. A card like Teferi wrecks a ton of complicated, clockwork-style decks, from boring combo machines to really interesting Johnny creations.
     I can accept that a card like Obliterate, which generally involves a complete shut-out of any activity at all, is not fun. Heck, you can not be playing **Magic** at all, and have no lands, if that's what you want to do!
     But I keep seeing intolerance of mana denial seep into other denial strategies – discard, permission, burn, and now this. Heck, why not ban Teferi? And while you're at it, ban mass removal like Evacuation? Why not Propaganda, too – your deck hates that card. And hey, since the road isn't quite completely paved smooth, how about banning all bounce as well? Aw, hell, ban blue mana altogether.
     There – now your deck wins all the time, and you didn't have to work hard or anything. Fun, indeed!
2.  ![](http://www.wizards.com/global/images/mtgcom_daily_tf10_pic1_en.jpg)**Powerful cards that “attract attention” are, by the nature of Magic, impossible to avoid**. This is not about whether Teferi is fun – but whether it can last. Some people believe a card that dies right away (or attracts other negative attention) is no good at all. “Teferi's a huge threat!” they scream. “I'd never play with that! I'm gonna sneak under the radar instead.”
     Set aside, for a moment, the laughable idea of sneaking under the radar at any table with intelligent players. The fact is, if you know multiplayer **Magic**, you're always assessing threats. Every card is a threat. If you start taking away the most powerful cards from the table, you still need to make a threat assessment from what's left. If there's nothing as dangerous as Teferi, you'll look for a Mahamoti Djinn. If there's no heavy like a Djinn, you'll look for a Man-o'-War. If there's no jellyfish floating around, you'll look for a freaking Grayscaled Gharial. Failing that, you'll suspect the guy with an untapped Island (Curfew? Force Spike?) You cannot reliably avoid attention at any **Magic** table where the group knows what it's doing. Any effort to do so is a waste of your deckbuilding skill.
     Another problem with the “It's such a big threat, it'll die right away” argument is the shallowness required of the definition of *threat*. A threat can be a single card that looks really flashy. To more experienced gamers, it can also be a collection of smaller threats (e.g. a weenie army), an opponent who has won in the past with “sudden death” decks, or even someone who is holding back their strategy and adding to your uncertainty. No matter what you do, or how many cards (or innocuous tokens) you use, you are going to have to face the fact that *not everybody wants you to win this game, and you're going to have to earn it*.

The irony of some of the discussion I've seen is that some people actually try to make both false arguments mentioned above, at once: they say that the card is no fun, and that no one would ever play it because it's too easy to kill and you'll die right away.

So which is it? Is Teferi so powerful that he stifles play, rendering everyone impotent as he laughs into old age? Or is he a risky maneuver, a shooting star good only to remind everyone to play Wrath of God (or dozens of other spells he can't stop) on their next turn? Folks, we either have the answers, or we don't. And that's true of every card, in every game. And the answer has nothing to do with stomping your foot and sticking out your lower lip. It has to do with preparing your deck, and your play style, to handle a full range of reasonable threats.

Play your threats out. Don't apologize for them. Back them up. Play your game. Worry less about what your opponent is doing that's “unfair,” and more about why you weren't prepared. Next time, come to the table ready to play.

All this is to say, I agree with The Ferrett (and Laura Mills, who chose the card as #1 for multiplayer in *Scrye* magazine) when he rates the card high. And if my agreeing with The Ferrett isn't enough to make you all pay attention…well, hell, I don't know what is.

So is Teferi better than Stuffy Doll, for multiplayer? The 4 for **Rattlesnake** reflects both a smidge of traditional element (warning that you may play, say, a Man-o'-War at any moment) and a bit of the “white” part of that element, which simply shuts down the argument by denying instants their speed. It's comparable to Arcane Laboratory or Dream Halls, which also have 4s on this dimension.

The 5 for **Gorilla** means the effect is pretty darn meaningful, but not overwhelming. Hyperbole notwithstanding, this truly is the case. Against an already well-established army, Teferi does darn little. Against a mitt full of potent sorceries, Teferi does even less. But in a game with lots of actions and reactions, Teferi can be amazing. For comparison, see Cowardice or Zur's Weirding. (Come to think of it, 5 may be a little high for Teferi. But I'll remain generous, since I'm only at this for one week.)

The 8 for **Spider** gets at the core of what Teferi's all about. Teferi gives you plenty of opportunities for surprises – and with flash, he's a surprise himself! And he takes away virtually any surprise your opponent may throw at you. The card advantage is more of a timing advantage than anything else; but that's more a limitation of my definition of *spider* than a fault of the card.

The 2 for **Pigeon** is a reflection of “average”, or what a generic card would get for simply not working actively against the element. Teferi doesn't really care how many opponents are in play.

![Giant_Cockroach](http://www.wizards.com/magic/images/cardart/9ED/Giant_Cockroach.jpg)The 4 for **Cockroach** is pretty standard for a permanent that lets you do something over and over (play creature spells at instant speed), but for a pretty big cost (in this case, the cost of the spells themselves). Neither tremendously impressive nor anything to sneeze at, the durability and repeatability of Teferi's effects depends largely upon how good you are at using him and protecting him. No surprise there.

Enough on Teferi – it's just a card. There will be plenty more cards like it, in the years to come. What's important is that you all maintain the tools to evaluate them honestly and play with and against them while enjoying the spirit of the game.
