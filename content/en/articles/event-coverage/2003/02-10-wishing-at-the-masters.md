The Masters Series is not exactly my favorite topic. Thanks to some decent performances in Nice and Chicago, my overall win percentage is at least close to 50%. Still, Masters isn’t exactly my strongest tournament, no matter which format. After the last Constructed one, I actually decided not to playtest any more as, after all, it is single elimination and usually someone who doesn’t playtest gets lucky and wins anyway. I do like to play ***Magic** Online* and I also like Standard in general, so I ended up testing a lot anyway.

I tried all sorts of decks, including strange stuff like Ben Seck’s black-red reanimator and even went to the extreme and tested aggressive creature decks like green-blue, green-white and green-red. Testing those decks on ***Magic** Online* wasn’t exactly fun. Testing in the casual play rooms doesn’t really work out and the eight-player tournaments are single elimination. Whenever I played one of the creature based decks, I would eventually run into a cycling deck, which you can’t really beat with aggro. Green-red, especially, has a hard time beating a deck full of creature removal, life gain and Exalted Angels, protected by Astral Slide. I kept losing round 1 or round 2 with the creature decks and that didn’t exactly boost my confidence in them. What I did realize was that green-red is easily the best of the aggressive decks. I was surprised that the deck posted a winning record against blue-green madness but green-red deck has a better early game and the wurms had to stay on defense, giving the green-red deck enough time to win with burn.

During all this ***Magic** Online* testing, a funny thing happened. Marco and Dirk were running the green-red beatdown deck and played against Brian Fox, who crushed them with his green-white-red beast deck. After losing twice without having a real chance, they figured his deck didn’t exist outside of ***Magic** Online* anyway and just left queues when he joined. In the end, both Marco and Dirk ran the green-red deck in the Masters Series. Gary Wise, who did quite a bit of testing, eventually found himself without a deck, as he didn’t like anything he came up with and asked Brian for a listing of his beast deck. Both Gary and Marco won their first round and, in round two, Marco had to face a copy of the deck that he couldn’t beat at all. It’s a small **Magic** world after all…

I didn’t really want to play beatdown, as it just isn’t my sort of thing. The obvious control deck was Psychatog, but there was one other deck I’ve been toying around with since I saw a block version played by Jan Doise in PTQs: a deck based on Cunning Wish-Mirari recursion. I gave my listing to Jens Thoren when he didn’t know what he should run at the Standard portion of last year’s Invitational. He did okay with it and I’ve been playing it with very good results online ever since. The thing is that in an environment filled with anti-creature strategies like the Astral Slide decks and different versions of green-white decks, the Wish/Mirari deck is a predator. Out of roughly fifty matches against the Slide deck, I lost exaclty once and that was when I took a double mulligan in game 1 and a single mulligan in game 3 and proceeded to miss land drops from turn 3 on in both games. Yes, the matchup is that lopsided. The Slide deck cannot possibly win. It is not that easy against the green-white decks but it is still very, very favorable.

The problem is that your worst matchup is Psychatog. If the other guy knows what he is doing and has a decent sideboard (Duress, Compulsion), he should win like 70% of the time. Having such a bad matchup against a tier 1 deck is pretty bad, I have to admit that. But there is some good news as well: the Wake deck has positive matchups against pretty much all other decks. Green-blue madness can be a pain if they run both Counterspell and Circular Logic, but they usually don’t and then it definitely is better then 50%. Against Sligh and green-red, the maindeck Renewed Faiths are very much needed, as they improve those matchups a lot. Without Faiths, they will just deal some quick damage with creatures and eventually finish you with burn. However, as soon as you get a Mirari into play and copy Renewed Faith for the first time, it is time to go on to the next game. Sligh just isn’t capable of dealing more then 30 points of damage.

But still, having a bad matchup against what would certainly be the most-played deck in the Masters, Psychatog, worried me. The last two weeks before Chicago, I pretty much played Psychatog exclusively and liked it a lot. It sometimes loses to the cycling deck, but it happens very rarely. The matchups against the different green decks are worse, but I’d much rather play a Psychatog mirror then with Wake against ‘Tog.

On the day of the Gateway, I was still undecided what I was going to run. I didn’t like green-red all that much, as it still had problems with a turn 3 Psychatog. Overloading that guy is not all that easy. I built copies of both my ‘Tog version and my Wake deck. I really wanted to play the Wake deck, but when the Gateway was about to finish, there were almost exclusively ‘Tog decks left standing. Eventually four ‘Tog decks ended up claiming the slots for the Masters. Fortunately, I was in the same position as John Larkin, who tested with us and also had a tendency towards playing the Wake deck. We discussed the problem for some time and eventually John went ahead and said two words that the editor would most likely delete if I wrote them here and decided to run Wake. That made my decision a lot easier, as now I wouldn’t be the only idiot playing Wake in a field of roughly 20 Psychatog decks.

I used Circular Logic over Counterspell in the beginning, but Counterspell is the correct card. Against creature decks, you want to have a cheaper counter, as sometimes you have to cast Wrath of God and back it up against their Circular Logic, or just Wrath and then counter their next spell. That doesn’t work out that well with Logic. Obviously, casting a Counterspell on turn 2 won’t happen all that often, but the threat of countering a turn 2 Wild Mongrel is a good thing. The big step from the deck I gave Jens to this version was reducing the number of Mirari’s Wakes. There was a time when almost everyone on ***Magic** Online* played Sligh and I knew that I had to move all four Renewed Faith into the maindeck if I wanted to beat that deck consistently. I couldn’t cut any of the card drawing and I think going below seven counterspells is a bad idea as well. The only thing left was the engine itself. Cutting the fourth Wish was something I did quite some time before that; it just isn’t needed and you don’t want to see two in your opening hand. When I sideboarded against Sligh and green-blue madness, I noticed that I really wanted to remove some of the high-casting cost cards. Initial hands with two or even three cards for four or more mana just won’t win. I started to board out Wakes and I noticed that the deck doesn’t really need them as badly as I thought. Something like ten lands and a Mirari is good enough against decks that don’t have countermagic and so I started to board down to two Wakes. Eventually, I just removed them from the deck and started to board down to one in a lot of matchups. Especially against decks that have access to Ray of Revelation, tapping out turn five for a Wake isn’t a good idea at all. With the Faiths and some Teroh’s Faithful in the board, the Sligh matchup was suddenly very favorable as well.

The only thing that I am still not comfortable with is the sideboard. I used the following at the Masters:

This is heavily metagamed against ‘Tog. The Seedtimes are there to counter Mana Short. They Short you in your end step and you respond with Seedtime, which a) wrecks their plan and b) hopefully leaves them tapped out when you go into your extra turn. We figured there would be so much ‘Tog that we wanted to have three copies of it. Other then that, the board is fairly standard. The problem is that you can’t really board all that much, as most of the cards are dedicated as targets for Cunning Wish. Elephant Ambush, Flash of Insight (to remove counterspells so you can Wish for them again), Krosan Reclamation (in case both Miraris are destroyed), Ray of Revelation (necessairy against Slide decks and similar stuff), Ray of Distortion (in case your opponent resolves Mirari first) and Opportunity are all important and should not be removed.

In the actual tournament, my matchups turned out to be a little bit different then I expected. I met Eric Froehlich in round 1 and he ran a green-red deck very similar to our design, with extra burn instead of Elephant Guides. I had pretty good draws and should’ve swept Eric 3-0. Unfortunately, I made a very bad judgment call in game 2. I boarded down to 2 Cunning Wishes (which is definitely correct) and then used one of them to get a Circular Logic on turn 3 as I didn’t have anything else to do. Mistake #1. Then I dropped the first Mirari and he got rid of it with Naturalize. I figured that he had only two in his board and cast the second Mirari on the next turn but his last card in hand turned out to be his second Naturalize. With both Miraris and one Wish gone, I had only two Elephant Tokens left to win with. Eric destroyed those with burn and I lost a game I should have won by all means. I got another good draw in game 3 and it didn’t matter.

In round 2, I faced Carlos Romao. Surprisingly, he was running Psychatog. He played a pretty techy version running Shadowmage Infiltrator and Cabal Therapy maindeck. The real problem was that his game plan was not to Mana Short me. That meant that the Seedtimes were not very useful against the reigning World Champ. It didn’t really matter, as I just proceeded to mana screw Carlos in game 1 and game 3. I got eaten up by his sideboarded Thieving Magpies and later on a ‘Tog in game 2, but ‘Tog can’t really win with two or three lands and I outlanded him really badly in the other two games.

Round 3 was probably the most interesting matchup. Ken Ho ran a version of the blue-green threshold-Upheaval-madness deck that he used to win PT-Osaka in 2002. The good news for me was that he had no access to enchantment removal at all. The bad news was that he had two maindeck Upheavals. That card just beats Wake on its own. In general, I would board out countermagic against blue-green, but I couldn’t do that in this case as coming back from an Upheaval is virtually impossible.  
 The first game was pretty interesting. He went first and played a turn two Wild Mongrel. I used Memory Lapse to delay the following Standstill and dropped Compulsion on my turn. He answerd with both Merfolk Looter and Standstill. I started to cycle through my deck with the Compulsion while Ken attacked for two each turn and used the Looter. I couldn’t find a Wrath but broke the Standstill with Cunning Wish which got Seedtime. On my turn, I cast Mirari’s Wake. Now, if he had three Circular Logics, there was no way I could win, but if he had only one or two, he couldn’t afford to counter as that would give me a free turn. He chose to let it resolve and with the Wake giving me extra mana, I managed to resolve a Mirari on my next turn and went into recursion, which he couldn’t stop.  
 The other two games didn’t go as well. Ken went first in game 2, mulliganed down to five and I kept a hand without blue mana. His first play was a turn three Basking Rootwalla, while I dropped a turn four Mirari, drew an island turn five and played Compulsion. He dropped his 6th land on his 6th turn, cast Upheaval and madnessed the Rootwalla back into play. Now we had a new game in which I could play my first land first, but he had a Rootwalla in play and I started on 12 life. It was close but I lost. Third game, I didn’t have a fourth land after a mulligan and he just walked all over me with a Wild Mongrel, a Rootwalla and an Arrogant Wurm protected with Circular Logic.

Still, 2-1 is decent and a lot better then I had hoped for. But I guess the real reason you are reading this is for the deck and how I would modify it. As I mentioned earlier, the maindeck should not be touched, in my opinion. The sideboard is limited as you need the mentioned six slots for the Wish targets. That leaves you with just nine slots to work with. One Seedtime is needed, down to eight. Then you need some cards against control. I think two extra Circular Logics and the fourth Compulsion are the way to go here. Against decks like Psychatog, it is also important to give them threats that they have to counter, otherwise nothing happens and eventually they will defeat you with Mana Short. That is why the third Wake is in the board. If you can resolve both Wake and Compulsion, it is pretty much game over. Maybe it should be a Disrupting Scepter, but I don’t like the idea of tapping lands in my main phase against a deck with Upheaval.

 1 Opportunity

 1 Krosan Reclamation  
 1 Ray of Revelation  
 1 Ray of Distortion  
 1 Compulsion  
 1 Seedtime  
 2 Chastise  
 2 Teroh’s Faithful  
 1 Mirari’s Wake  
 2 Circular Logic

The only deck that this sideboard doesn’t deal with as much as I would like is green-blue madness. I haven’t found a good sideboard card against it. All the cards that would be good against it are artifacts or enchantments. Meekstone, Circle of Protection: Green and maybe even Ensnaring Bridge would all be good, but then they board Ray of Revelation/Naturalize anyway and it feels wrong to walk right into that. The Circle and Meekstone both fail against Wild Mongrel, which isn’t really satisfying either.

If I had to play this deck tomorrow in a regualr (non-Masters) tournament, I would run the above-mentioned sideboard and use it like this:

**vs. Psychatog**  
 +1 Compulsion, +2 Circular Logic, +1 Mirari’s Wake  
 -2 Wrath of God, -2 Moment’s Peace
