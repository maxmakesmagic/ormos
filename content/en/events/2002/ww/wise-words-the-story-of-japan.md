Sitting here, on the bus to Yokohama, driving through Tokyo, I can’t help but get a little excited. Japan has been good to me, with finishes of 7th, 11th and 31st in my three Pro Tours there, providing me with writing opportunities and proving time and again that the Japanese are among the most generous people in the world, both with their culture and their time.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>When you’re the big dumb Canadian in a foreign locale, things can be tricky.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

When you’re the big dumb Canadian in a foreign locale, things can be tricky. Try asking for nothing on your Big Mac in Italian and you’ll get my gist. The Japanese seem to speak less English than anyone, but more than any group of people I’ve ever encountered, they’re willing to give you their help. In most countries, ask for directions and they’ll point a finger. Ask in Japan, they’ll grab your luggage and lead you there. It’s indicative of an attitude that sees the good in things: they help one another for the greater good.

Skaff Elias, creator of the Pro Tour, once explained to me the Japanese attitude towards American imports like **Magic**. The Japanese are practically devoid of cynicism and sarcasm, and therefore when told something’s good, they have no reason not to believe it. At the same time as their perception of our culture comes through the tunnel vision created by the limited information they have access to. The Japanese, who are more passionate about their hobbies than we are as a whole, see those who sit atop their respective hobbies as equals. In other words, Kai is Barry Bonds or David Beckham. They love this hobby, he’s the best in the world, and he’ll be treated like the best in any other field.

Japan’s history in our game is a long one now. A little under four years ago, the 1999 World Championships were held here, back before Kai had won a Pro Tour, when the Masters Series was a mere glint in our collective eye. A lot has changed in that time, with Japan, the world’s second largest national M:tG community, becoming an annual host to the Pro Tour, but the passion the people here show for our little hobby has remained constant: nowhere on the planet do GPs attract more players, or PTs more spectators.

With another chapter in the Japanese history of the Pro Tour about to be written, it seems appropriate to look back on the past at what has happened here in the land of Toshiki Tsukamoto, Satoshi Nakamura and Tsuyoshi Fujita. Sit back, relax and enjoy the ride. We’ll get to the hotel soon enough.

### The First of Many

Kai Budde has won seven Pro Tours, but none was more spectacular than the first. Coming into 1999 World Championships, Casey McCarrel and Jon Finkel were in a heated race for Player of the Year, while the young German had started turning heads with his multiple European GP wins. Playing Wildfire in Standard and Dark Tide in Extended, Kai made his way into a solid Top 8 that included Jakub Slemr, Matt Linde, Mark LePine, Nico Herzog and I. Kai’s Semifinals win over Italian Raffaele Lo Moro clinched the come-from-behind victory for his first PotY honors before decimating LePine in the finals for what, to date, has been his lone World Championship.

There was another major story of note. Randy Buehler, playing in his last World Championships, managed to go 12-0 in Constructed, but missed the Top 8 due to an 0-6 draft performance in which he earned the only warning of his Pro Tour career. Sitting next to Sigurd Eskeland in the first draft of Day 2, Randy understandably expected cooperation from the Big Viking, who was on a one tournament sabbatical from his Mogg Squad ties due to his working a clinic with Randy before Worlds. With four cards left in the final pack of the draft, Randy became infuriated when Sigurd hate drafted a card that would have made Randy’s deck. As the draft ended, Randy stood in a rage, pointed at the Norwegian and shouted “\[expletive deleted\] you if you think you’re getting an Extended deck!” Randy’s 0-6 was only barely bettered by Sigurd’s 1-5 performance that day. Perhaps their mutual breakdown was the incentive Randy needed to back off on his threat.

As the first Pro Tour held in Japan, Worlds was an awakening. Until this tournament, spectator support for Pro Tours had been sporadic, a few stragglers occasionally glancing at matches between rounds, but this was different. All weekend, thousands upon thousands of bodies filled the venue, lining the gallery where PT matches were being played and swarming the Feature Match area. For those who were there, the fan support alone may have made it the best Pro Tour to date.

### 14-0

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTTOK01/739.jpg)

In the history of the Pro Tour, only Ryan Fuller has managed a perfect record in the Swiss rounds of a Pro Tour. Playing *Invasion* Block Constructed at 2001 PT-Tokyo, Fuller piloted his red-green deck to the perfecto before bowing out of the tournament in the Quarterfinals. Ryan’s success was a mere symptom of his team’s greater success, with ABU placing four players in the Top 8. None of the four — Fuller, Chris Benafel, Lucas Hager and Phil Freneau — made the finals though.

Despite the obvious dominance of red decks in the tournament, the two finalists, Zvi Mowshowitz and Tsuyoshi Fujita, didn’t have a mountain between them. Fujita’s appearance made history, as it was the first time a Japanese player had broken through to a Top 8, but in the end, it was Mowshowitz earning the victory with his heavily metagamed Solution deck, the crowning achievement in the Constructed specialist’s career. 

As if the 14-0 weren’t enough, Fuller’s weekend got even better with Team ABU’s victory in the Masters. Fuller, Benafel and Noah Boeken grinded into the tournament on Thursday before defeating Potato Nation Thursday night. In the final, they faced the unheralded Panzer Hunters, led by Itaru Ishida. ABU became the first Gateway participant to win Masters. The Hunters would return to the Masters final a year later in Osaka.

### The Colony Meets France

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i> It was a shining moment for the resurgent nation…</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

A whole lot of hype surrounded the lead up to Pro Tour-Osaka when eight Americans and Canadians – Chris Benafel, Brian Hegstad, Ken Ho, Jeff Fung, Jeff Cunningham, Terry Tsang, Brett Shears and Peter Szigeti – decided to lock themselves in seclusion Survivor-style for a month before the Pro Tour. The idea was that the group, who would be aided by Brian Kibler and Ben Rubin from the outside, would playtest non-stop for a month, break the format, and share the details on Sideboard every step of the way.

Things didn’t quite work out that way, with only two articles appearing and the Canadians breaking off from the group. Most of the team failed to make Day 2, but the exception, Ho, carried the team banner into the Top 8 and all the way to the win with a heavily metagamed blue-green deck whose primary goal was to destroy the black decks that made up a large portion of Day 2.

In the final, Ho defeated Olivier Ruel, one of four Frenchmen to make Top 8 along with Nicholas Olivieri, Christophe Haim and Sylvain Lauriol. It was a shining moment for the resurgent nation, which had been among Magic powers in the early years, but quieted down through the late nineties. Osaka, while a slight aberration, showed that the new generation of French players, including Gabriel Nassif, Amiel Tenenbaum, Antoine Ruel, Franck Canu and a host of others, was here to stay.

### The Master’s Curse

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/MASTERSNY00/images/552.jpg)

That same weekend, Kai Budde finally broke his Masters curse. In the first Masters in New York, Kai got the “easy pairing”, getting matched with Jason Zila, who many assumed had merely shown up to collect his $2000 check after two years away from the Pro Tour, but the old Hitman took the Juggernaut down, which was the beginnings of a rough pattern for Kai, who time and again would make his way to the Masters only to fall victim to that Series’s single elimination format. 

With Phoenix Foundation teammates Dirk Baberowski and Marco Blume in tow, Kai finally managed to shake the curse, with the team, who until this year’s Venice Masters were undefeated in sanctioned team Rochester drafts, defeated hometown favorites Panzer Hunters in the final. It wasn’t easy though, with Blume losing his match to Reiji Ando and Budde and Baberowski down one game each before they made the comeback to take it all. 

### Chapter Four

A few stories that are developing for this weekend:

**The Final Masters** – Yes, the top 28 pro point players in New Orleans will be receiving $2000 checks in the mail, but for all intents and purposes, this weekend, Yokohama will play host to the final installment of the Masters story. With Worlds the only other Premiere-level international event left after PT-Yokohama and the end of year payout system coming into effect next season, this will be the last opportunity the Pro Tour’s finest will have to capitalize on the cutthroat nature of single elimination. With World Championships using the same Extended format (though Worlds will include *Scourge*) as the Masters, expect the decks that emerge as dominant this weekend to define the Day 3 metagame there.

**Yoko No-Shows**\- Due to Yokohama failing to contribute to either future Masters events or the first end of year payouts (to be handed out at the conclusion of World Championships 2004), the expense to travel here and the perceived threat of SARS, there is potential for a number of regularly seen players choosing to not make the trip. Neil Reeves, who has been touted as a favorite and hot tip in the Fantasy PT, informed me Saturday night he’d decided to stay home and attend his fiancee’s graduation, while Matt Rubin and Gerard Fabiano were both unlikely to make the trip. Keep a close eye on names missing from the standings this weekend.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTTOK01/654.jpg)

**Role Reversal** – Where Japanese players usually have a harder time dealing with the rigors of jetlag than most due to the distances they travel for the Pro Tour, the Japanese Pro Tour sees the opposite proving true. Fujita finished second in Tokyo, and then followed it up with a Top 16 performance in Osaka, where he was joined by Tsuyoshi Ikeda. Not only do the Japanese thrive on their hometown sleep schedule, the rest of the Pro Tour has to contend with drastic time zone changes. This can have an equally drastic effect on the standings. Why have I been immune? Simple: I never sleep anyways.

I’m hoping I won’t be eating those words seven days from now. Have a good week.
