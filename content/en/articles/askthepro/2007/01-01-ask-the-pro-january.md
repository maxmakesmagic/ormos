Wizards of the Coast welcomes Hall of Famer Raphaël Lévy as the featured player here at "Ask the Pro." A fixture on the Pro Tour for the last eight years, Raphaël is uniquely positioned to answer your questions about the life of a professional **Magic** player, give a historical perspective on the game and high-level tournament scene, share stories about travelling the world, and talk about the role **Magic** plays in his life.

Seventh on the lifetime Pro Points list, Raphaël began his Pro Tour career back at Pro Tour-Paris in 1997. He became a regular on the tour starting at the 1998 World Championships. Since then he hasn't missed a Pro Tour, an astounding streak of 49 consecutive events. He was recently honored as a member of the [**Magic** Pro Tour Hall of Fame](/en/articles/event-coverage/undated/tournaments-events-trading-card-game-magic-the-gathering-2)'s 2006 class and was inducted at the 2006 World Championships in Paris. Send your question, along with your name and location, via this email form. Answers will be posted every Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday. 

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>January 6, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: I wanted to know how much you think <b>Magic</b> is skill-based and how much luck plays a role in it. My experience showed me that even players who are not that experienced or still make some crucial mistakes can win a bigger tournament (even a PTQ), and some players whom I totally respect for their skills end up with a 2-2 or even a 1-3 record at Friday Night <b>Magic</b>.<br> -Wesimo Al-Bacha<br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> That's a question every <b>Magic</b> player asks himself hundreds of times. How much &quot;luck&quot; is involved in a <b>Magic</b> game? </p>&#10;<p>To answer this question, we first have to define what &quot;luck&quot; is. For now, let's say that &quot;luck&quot; is composed of all the random factors you encounter. In a tournament, that means pairings and in-game parameters.</p>&#10;<p>The pairings in Constructed tournaments are a major factor of one's success. There are always decks against which you don't want to play and whether you play against them or not is totally out of your control. Of course, the whole point of a Constructed tournament is to play with the statistics, and play a deck that will give you the best odds to beat your opponents (metagame choice). </p>&#10;<p>In a game, &quot;luck&quot; includes the sequences of cards in both libraries. Players call it &quot;luck,&quot; but &quot;randomness&quot; would probably be more accurate. &quot;Luck&quot; would define more how &quot;randomness&quot; gives advantage to one player. To try to find out how much &quot;randomness&quot; matters, let's take a few examples:</p>&#10;<p>1. Two players, with exact same skill level (hypothetically), playing the same decks. The outcome of the match will depend 100 percent on how the cards are distributed.</p>&#10;<p>2. Two players, playing the same decks, with different skill levels. The outcome of the match will depend on how big the difference is between the two players, and how the cards are distributed. </p>&#10;<p>In the long run: </p>&#10;<p></p><li>Between two high-class players, the better player will have between 56 percent and 63 percent chance to win the match.</li>&#10;<li>Between a high-class player and a casual/&quot;OK&quot; player, the odds would be more 65-85 percent depending on how &quot;bad&quot; the latter is. </li>&#10;<p></p>&#10;<p>Where do I pull these stats? Where does 58-63 percent come from? Check the Hall of Famers' career statistics tab <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20070101000000/http://www.wizards.com/default.asp?x=mtgevent/hof/profiles">on their profile pages</a>. All of them are among the best players in the world (just pretend I'm not in there, for the sake of this answer!). Jon Finkel, undeniably the best player of the 10, <a href="/en/articles/event-coverage/undated/jon-finkel-magic-pro-tour-hall-of-fame">has won 63.3 percent his matches</a> in premier tournaments (Nationals, Grand Prix, Pro Tours, Worlds). If you check the other profiles, you'll see that the &quot;worst&quot; stats don't go below 56 percent. </p>&#10;<p>Integrating ALL the random factors into one single equation is probably impossible (and wouldn't fit in my column anyway!). Let's just say that the in-game &quot;luck factor&quot; depends on the play-level difference between the two players – a worse player needs more help from random factors in order to win a match against a better player. </p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>January 4, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: During your amazing streak of attending Pro Tours, have you ever experienced being mis-judged? Unfortunately this just happened to me at Worlds. Luckily for me, other judges managed to prove I was innocent and talked to the head judge to change the call, which prevented me from being DQ'd. But it was still the worst moment I ever had in my life when I was &quot;declared guilty&quot; by the head judge while I knew I was innocent. As an experienced Pro <b>Magic</b> player, what would you do, or what do you think is the right thing to do in this kind of situation?<br> - Wu Jia<br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hello Wu,</p>&#10;<p>I was once in your case. It was in Barcelona in 2001, semifinals of the Masters' Gateway. I was deck checked. I realised that I had forgotten to de-sideboard and I told the judge right away. I knew what kind of sanction I could expect (game loss for failure to present a legal deck). However, the way I reacted looked suspicious to the judges, and after an hour of debating my case, they decided to disqualify me. They were not sure it was intentional, but just in case.</p>&#10;<p>Of course, I felt it was unfair. I told the truth from the beginning. And that's what you're supposed to do. Hiding information or telling inaccurate facts is going to work against you in the end. I've seen many players being disqualified, not because they were guilty, but because they were not telling the truth. Events at Worlds this year and Grand Prix – Malmö (I'm not talking about Olivier here) come to mind. </p>&#10;<p>Try to defend your case as truthfully as you can. Try to see if there were witnesses. Try not to invoke the &quot;why would I do something so stupid&quot; argument, because it's usually not a valid one.</p>&#10;<p>You are basically bound to whatever the head judge decides. Just like in a courthouse, the verdict sometimes is unfair, but you should know that it's not a pleasant decision for a judge to disqualify a player either, especially when the player claims he is innocent. </p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>January 2, 2007</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: How much do popular decks affect your Constructed decisions? Do you have any colors you would rather play than others? How much does <i><b>Magic</b> Online</i> affect your evaluation of the metagame for such events as Worlds?<br> - Eugene<br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> When it comes to choosing your deck for a Constructed event, you have to take into accounts various factors:</p>&#10;<p></p><li>The metagame: In other words, what is everyone else going to play or what do you expect to play against. <i><b>Magic</b> Online</i> is the worldwide playing ground, and you can see there which decks are popular. Frank Karsten has been analysing the phenomenon in his column and has <a href="/en/articles/onlinetech/2006/12-13-the-online-standard-and-extended-metagames">done some interesting statistics</a> on the difference between the Standard metagame at Worlds and the Standard metagame on <i><b>Magic</b> Online</i>. I invite you to take a look at it and see how much similar both fields were. I would say that the field on <i><b>Magic</b> Online</i> is about 65 percent reliable, which means that 65 percent of the decks you're going to see at a given competition are the decks you see on <i><b>Magic</b> Online</i>. So how much should you consider <i><b>Magic</b> Online</i> in your preparation? 65 percent.</li>&#10;<p></p>&#10;<p></p><li>The playtesting: Playtesting is not about all about tuning the deck you're going to play, it's about integrating the field. You need to know what to expect when you play against a popular deck. You need to have played a couple of games with a given deck to see what its weaknesses are. That way during your games, you can put yourself in your opponent's shoes and figure what would be the most annoying play you could do. Some players manage to do it thanks to their intuition, others by playtesting all the decks.</li>&#10;<p></p>&#10;<p></p><li>Your style: This has been repeated by everyone a thousand times. You should play a deck you feel comfortable with, a deck you like, a deck you trust. The worst feeling after a tournament is when you tell yourself: &quot;I shouldn't have played this deck.&quot; You can have many reasons for that – the deck could just have been too hard to play for you because it's not the kind of deck you usually run, or you didn't enjoy playing your deck and that made you lose focus and make mistakes. If you don't know what your &quot;style&quot; is, take all the decks you have been successful with and try to see what they have in common.</li>&#10;<p></p>&#10;<p>Just as an example: I like to play either combo decks (Mind's Desire, Replenish, Project X, 12 post), or control-aggro decks (Rock Aggro, LLL). I don't like to play straight aggro decks and straight control decks (I've never been a huge fan of countermagic). That's what would define my style.</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>
