In the past year or so, I’ve written a total of four tournament reports. One was my recent report from U.S. Nationals ([part 1](/en/articles/event-coverage/2003/07-18-nationals-report-day-1), [part 2](/en/articles/event-coverage/2003/07-28-nationals-report-day-2-and-beyond)), where I tore through the Swiss before falling in the Quarterfinals. Another was from GP-Boston ([part 1](/en/articles/event-coverage/2003/02-28-grand-prix-boston-report-champion), [part 2](/en/articles/event-coverage/2003/03-03-grand-prix-boston-report-champion-day-2)), where despite my lack I preparation I came home with my second GP title and my first big trophy to drink out of. Before that was from the [Invitational](/en/events/2001/mi/magic-invitational-report-brian-kibler-2), where I scarcely missed the Finals, and before that was from last year’s World Championships ([part 1](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/09-04-the-brian-kibler-world-championships-experience-part-1), [part 2](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/09-06-the-brian-kibler-world-championships-experience-part-2), [part 3](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/09-16-the-brian-kibler-world-championships-experience-part-3)), where I came up a few tiebreaker points short of playing on the Sunday stage. I’ve played in every Pro Tour and nearly every North American Grand Prix in that period, as well, but my reports for those events – if they exist at all – are at best half finished on my hard drive.

So why those four? What do all of those tournaments have in common? It’s no mystery, really. They’re stories that I really wanted to tell. They’re tales of victory – of struggle and success. It’s easy to get excited about writing a report recalling one’s successes – it’s a lot harder to do the same when the story’s high point is round four, and it all goes downhill from there.

Ultimately, though, it’s all part of the same story. The successes must be framed by the failures or else the whole tale isn’t being told. What is the story of Jon Finkel’s comeback this season without his struggle that nearly saw him fall off the Pro Tour last year? While I may wish that my own tale was one that jumped from peak to peak without dipping into the valleys in between, the reality of the situation is that sometimes you just don’t win. Shocking, I know, but if I am to offer an honest portrayal of the life of a Magic pro, I need to write about what it’s like to lose in addition to what it’s like to win.

That’s what English majors call “foreshadowing”, I suppose – or perhaps that’s a term left to the realm of high school English classes. Regardless, I’m sure most if not all of you have seen the results of the 2003 World Championships in Berlin by now and know that my name wasn’t one of those framed in lights and held up next to dollar signs, so even if you don’t know just how I ended up, you know it wasn’t quite in the winner’s circle. Secretariat I was not. And though you may know how the story ends, that doesn’t mean it’s not worth telling – so let’s head back to the beginning…

My preparation for Worlds began pretty much as soon as Nationals ended. While for some such a near miss would be a source of frustration with the game, for me, if anything, my performance there only stoked my desire to do well at Worlds. Though my performances over the past few years have at least shown that my previous successes weren’t flukes, I wanted to establish myself more firmly among the top echelon of players by following up my Nationals performance with a solid result at Worlds. Pro Tour historian that I am, I’m keenly aware that my single PT Top 8 alone doesn’t put me on that list. While many seem to feel that I am among the top players in the game, I’d like some more top performances that prove it.

Testing was somewhat hectic, as my schedule is quite different from most members of the waking world, to say the least. Being functionally nocturnal has its upsides, but in this case it turned out to be a bit of a problem, as both Zvi and Justin Gary stick to more “normal” sleep schedules. I tend to wake up around mid-afternoon, shower, eat, work out and such, and by the time I’m ready to game, Zvi only has a couple hours of wakefulness left in him. This meant that most of my playtesting took place against Dave Humpherys, who is best described as a ***Magic** Online* addict. I managed to get games in against all of the online members of YMG, but most of my practice was in the middle of the night between drafts with Dave. Hardly optimal testing conditions, but you do with what you can.

I’ll explain precisely how we came to the decklist than Justin Gary and I played in the Standard portion in another article, but ultimately I played the deck because I felt that I wanted to run something somewhat off the metagame radar that had good matchups against the aggressive decks in the format as well as game against control. We predicted far more u-g and goblins and far less Wake than actually turned up, which meant that in our bubble b-w was an incredibly powerful deck. I’m notorious for my optimistic perceptions of my decks’ matchups at times, but going into the tournament I really couldn’t think of any decks that I felt were really all that bad for me. Wake and Reanimator were really rough in game one, but I had very powerful sideboard options against them in Cabal Interrogator and Coffin Purge, respectively.

Much of my pre-tourney thoughts and adventures can be found [here](/en/events/2003/worlds03/the-pre-worlds-diary-of-brian-kibler), so I won’t rehash all of that. After I got that article in, however, my fortunes seemed to go downhill. My body’s internal clock was still very much out of whack on Tuesday, when my roommate Dave Humpherys had to wake me up at 3 PM to make sure I got up at all. Mark Rosewater said I looked like I was still comatose when to the site to register. Not only that, but the annoying cold that I’d had when I got on the plane to Amsterdam seemed to have developed into a full-blown sinus infection. Apparently, I wasn’t able to breathe through my nose the entire time I was sleeping, which lead to my lips getting horribly chapped and cracked by the time I got up. For any who have not had experience with such, I assure you it is quite uncomfortable and not at all what you want to deal with when you’re trying to concentrate to play **Magic**.

I spend the rest of the day finalizing my deck and sideboard and finding all of the necessary cards to get it together. After scrounging for Cabal Interrogators to supply both Justin and myself, I finally head to sleep – or to attempt to sleep, rather. My confused body can’t get to sleep until at least 3 AM, making our 7:30 wake up call all the more unpleasant. I hate alarms to begin with, but when they’re blaring in my ear mere hours after I finally dozed off, my hate for them burns with the fire of a thousand suns. Any guesses how many times I went to my MWF 9:30 Latin class?

I roll out of bed, get dressed, and head downstairs for the free breakfast. If the venue for Worlds this year had only one thing going for it, it was the breakfast – and I loved the venue, so just imagine how much I loved the breakfast. Buffet style breakfast foods spread out over two levels as far as the eye could see! I always hate walking from my hotel to the site and not having enough time to get a decent meal in before the tournament starts. Not only was this tournament held in a site connected to the biggest hotel in Europe, but they fed us too! Oh, the simple pleasures of an ex-wrestler...

I’m sitting in the venue sleeving up my deck with the Ringbearer, Gabe Walls, when Bob Maher walks up and asks if we have a Standard deck. Not a list – an actual deck. Apparently, Bob had some trouble getting cards together due to some miscommunications and was just scrambling to find something to play. Gabe offers him the extra u-g deck in his room, but it is unnecessary, as Bob manages to scrape together what he needs for his intended deck – Wake – from Gaming Jim. With my hundreds-of-games-played b-w-control-deck, I’d mailed Ed Fear a list of cards I needed to borrow weeks in advance of the tournament. Looking at them, sitting in my hand, I realize just how different Bob and my perspectives are on this game.

Finally, the seating for the player meeting goes up, and I prepare my music selection for the tournament: Paul van Dyk’s “Global” disc, for those who are curious – restarting to track one “We are Alive” before each round. It’s strange, really, how for certain tournaments my music selections seem like a natural fit and for others they feel somewhat forced. For this tournament, I stuck with Global because it’d been my almost perpetual soundtrack for the weeks before the event, but once I started in I realized that the track timing was somewhat off to start as pairings go up and end as the round begin. “We are Alive” was too short and the second track wasn’t the sort that I tend to use – it had no good natural stopping points. I didn’t realize this, of course, until I was shuffling up for my first match – and then, of course, it was too late to go back.

### Round 1 – Maarten Ferguson

I know going into the round that Maarten was on the Dutch team, and I’d been told by his compatriots who’d tested at a colony of sorts that they felt their deck was clearly the best in the field. I prepared for the worst, as this was the crew that had Bob Maher looking for Forsaken Cities the morning of PT-Houston. A turn one Krosan Verge assuaged my fears of something crazy, but looking at my hand – a boatload of removal and nothing much else to speak of – I resigned myself to win two straight after sideboarding. Things went pretty much as expected from that point, with Maarten getting Compulsion and Wake into play and overrunning me with soldier tokens.

Sideboarding, however, changes all of that. If I’d realized how much Wake there was going to be at Worlds, I certainly would’ve included a full four copies of Cabal Interrogator in my sideboard – as it was, I at least had three, as opposed to the two Justin Gary ended up with. Cabal Therapy strips a pair of Compulsions from Maarten’s hand and pretty soon my little zombie wizard buddy comes down for the rest of his cards. Dragons and angels of various sorts get mop-up duty. Game two goes similarly, with an early Interrogator just completely dominating the board. Call of the Herd comes down on Maarten’s side, but I just pick his hand apart and play Exalted Angel. With no hand and facing down the best Grey Ogre ever, Maarten is not long for this world.

### Round 2 – Alex Mack

I avoid making any comments about Alex being a Nickolodeon star in his youth and shuffle up to get my game on. I know some other Germans are playing Wake, so I assume Alex is as well and keep a very solid hand against control without much early removal. I’m able to pressure him early with Cabal Therapy and flash it back off of Decree of Justice tokens to strip some of his gas, but I have no pressure to go along with it – as is often the case in this deck – and he’s able to draw his way back into the game. With Wake and Compulsion in play, Alex hard casts two Deep Analyses and leaves himself with four lands open. He sits to think and I realize his only real decision at this point is whether to play a Mirari or not, so I go for it.

A Cabal Therapy takes care of the former, and once I play a Decree of my own for two angels, Alex has only Deep Analysis and Mirari left in his deck for spells that can affect the game in any way. I play an Exalted Angel of my own and he decides his time is better spent conceding.

### Round 3 – Sigurd Eskeland – Feature Match

As I sit down on the stage and shuffle up, I mention to Sigurd that we’re meeting in a bit better circumstances than last time we played round three of Worlds. That last time was in Toronto, where we’d tested together and met at the 0-2 table. Even worse was the fact that in previous years, round three had been preceded by the opening ceremony, which meant that if you were 0-2 you just got to sit and watch the flag procession and stew about how miserably you were doing the whole time. That time my Pygmy Pyrosaurs had taken down his Skies deck with a little help from Slingshot Goblin out of the sideboard – and no, I’m not kidding – and I was looking for a repeat.

### Round 4 – Jens Thoren – Feature Match

The last time I’d played Jens was the Houston Masters series, where his Shepard of Rot forced us to play five games, since he drew two of them right before he’d die each time. Jens pulled that one out, as you might know given that he won the event, and I wanted revenge – that could’ve been me in the seat that opened Starstorm, Menacing Ogre, and Tephraderm in a Masters Top 8! Going into the round I know that Jens is playing Goblins with Clickslither – which is both good and bad news, since goblins is a great matchup for my deck, but Clickslither isn’t quite as well shut down as the rest of his team by my Spheres of Law. This knowledge about his deck definitely helped, since I’m able to Cabal Therapy more effectively knowing what his major threats are. This match is covered on the Sideboard [here](/en/events/2003/worlds03/round-4-jens-thoren-vs-brian-kibler), so I won’t go into detail. I won, though. Another turn one Krosan Verge. Sigh. I’m not unhappy to play against Wake again at this point because it’s a bad match up, but rather because I’m so exhausted and it’s a very complicated match to play. Having played for many hours already that day – and having gotten only three or four hours of sleep the night before – I’m getting to the point that all I really want to do is sleep. Game one I lose, and when I double mulligan for game two, things are already looking bad. My five-card hand has land and Cabal Interrogator, though, so I can hardly complain. My buddy comes down on turn two and gets to work. On turn four, I play my fourth land and Cabal Therapy away Pedro’s Wrath of God, leaving my Interrogator safe to do his dirty work.

And dirty work he does, stripping Pedro’s hand of anything of consequence. At one point Pedro’s grip is a full four Mana Leaks, so I start casting spells to let him double Leak them in order to cut back the time and mana my Interrogator requires to do his job. I manage to keep his hand down to nothing, but I still haven’t drawn any real threats of my own, with all of my Decrees and Exalted Angels apparently hiding deep in my deck, and eventually Pedro rips a Compulsion and cycles into a Decree of his own. This forces me to Vengeance, and then off the top for the Brazilian comes Deep Analysis, undoing much of what my little buddy had accomplished. Thankfully, though, I draw into an Angel and a Decree of my own and put Pedro on a very fast clock. He manages to Moment’s Peace one turn, then cycles a Decree of his own and hits me for a bunch after Wrathing. I play out a dragon with him at four, and take one hit from his soldiers before swinging back for the win – but he has Renewed Faith in hand and takes the hit without dying, killing me on his counterattack.

I felt like fatigue was a definite factor in this game, as there were a number of complicated situations in which I felt like I couldn’t analyze all of my options both accurately enough to make the correct play and quickly enough to win with time for a third game. I’m fairly certain that I could have won the game with more precise play, but given my sheer exhaustion, that simply wasn’t a possibility. 

Matches 4-2 Games 8-6

Slipping from 4-0 to 4-2 isn’t a good feeling, but I don’t have much time to dwell on it as I head to dinner with YMG and then directly to sleep. Zvi is ranting that we need to work on Extended, but all I hear in my half-asleep fog is a voice that sounds something like the adults in all of the Charlie Brown cartoons. I head back to the hotel as soon as possible and crash, hoping to remedy my mediocre day one performance with a solid draft day.
