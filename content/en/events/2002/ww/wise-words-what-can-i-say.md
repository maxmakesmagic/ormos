### 

What can I say? I just got a few bad bounces is all. Everyone seems to be asking me “what happened at Nats?” after I received what I thought were some pre-emptive congratulations at the end of Day 1, but really, it came down to what may have been one subtle mistake in the second draft (which I’ll address next week. That, by the way, is known as a “teaser”. Read on!) and some of the luck factor we’re all anxious to forget is a part of this game.

Enough with the lamentations though. I had some of the better times I’ve had at an event in recent memory at Nationals, thanks to Josh Rider’s unlikely victory, Pete Radonjic’s amusing but futile attempts to pull one over on me in the verbal jab arena, the beauty of Montreal and sheer joy of playing for the country, but more happened this weekend than my personal winning and losing streaks and I have only so much time and space. Let’s get to it:

### The Champ

![](https://i.ormos.uk/45/3e/453ed5d42d2fa443e3aff6b55ffd742db006edcda808e078acaaed1316e2fda4.jpg)I’ve got to be honest here, when Josh Rider, Radonjic and I were driving to Montreal, I didn’t think it would be the kid in the back seat who’d take home the silverware. Josh Rider’s been a part of the Toronto **Magic** community for years, a truly good kid (well, he’s 20 now, but look at that face!) who time and again would come close to breaking through that PTQ barrier. Josh had never made it to a Pro Tour until this weekend changed everything for a guy who, coming into the weekend, was finally looking to sell his collection and move on from the game.

Funny how things can change in a day, huh?

Now, Josh has a ticket to Germany, and unlike last year’s winner (and former Rider roommate) Jurgen Hahn, he fully plans on attending, as do newfound teammates Nathan Braymore and Richard “hot streak” Hoaen. Is it a strong team? Not the best to emerge from assorted Nats, paling to the French, German and Finnish entries at least, but they’ll put up a solid finish.

### Would you Like to Concede?

An interesting situation arose in the final round of the Swiss when Boyd Hardie, a long-time member of the Canadian competitive scene was paired up against Brian “the Lion” Bonnell. Hardie, still mathematically alive, was hoping that Bonnell would concede the match as the latter had already been eliminated, but when he made the request, Bonnell essentially replied “I came to play”, going on to defeat Hardie, who as it turns out would not have made Top 8 even with the win.

I talked to Bonnell after the fact and he expressed his personal belief that one should be forced to earn their way into that coveted Top 8 slot, and that more than any other was the reason he elected to put Boyd in the pressure cooker. After the loss, Hardie, who’s about as shy as Jim Carrey in a facial elasticity contest, was visibly angered by the entire thing, which lead to the question of why it is we’ve grown accustomed to the concession as status quo, and whether or not we should be looking at it that way.

Personally, put in Brian’s spot (assuming they don’t know one another. If Brian doesn’t like Boyd for some reason it might change things a bit), I’d have conceded, and here’s why: yes, in a perfect world, this would be a luckless game where all rewards would be earned in as true a nature as can be mustered, but while I think that generally speaking the Top 8 will consist of those players who have performed best in a tournament, placing a tournament’s final outcome on one best two of three match where any number of factors from a bad match up to double mana screw can occur is “unfortunate”.

The argument that the concession in such a situation throws off the competitive balance is an inaccurate one. With many other tables usually taking the intentional draw and or seeing similar concessions, being paired down against someone with a fierce enough sense of justice to force the playing of such a match actually unbalances the unfortunate pair-ee’s tournament in a pretty hefty way. While anyone else with Boyd’s record was either playing or drawing based mostly on their personal whims, Boyd wasn’t given that opportunity because of the unfortunate but necessary reality of Swiss pairings, that being that sometimes there aren’t enough players with equivalent records to be paired against one another.

Am I saying that Brian was wrong? No. Brian believed strongly in the idea of earning one’s way to Top 8, and for him to concede in the face of that belief would have seen him betray his personal code of ethics, and I’m sure not going to be the one to say “screw ethics”, as a lot of the time, that’s all that separates us from chaos. I’ll even go so far as to say Boyd was wrong to think that he “deserved” that concession. That said though, I don’t want to be the one to strip a person of their hopes and dreams with nothing to gain from a personal victory. I’ll go as far as to say that I think that, if Brian’s opponent in the 12th round had been his brother or girlfriend, he’d have conceded in a second (I have not asked Brian about that, but it’s common sense really, unless he hopes to be disowned or sleep on the couch), which in turn suggests that, again, the pairing against Boyd actually unbalances the tournament structure. I may have an overdeveloped empathy for strangers, but I only know that if I could do something to give someone the opportunity for self-realization at no detriment to myself (this statement, of course, ignores the betrayal of one’s one code of ethics, not that such a thing should be ignored so much as it may overwhelm the gratification involved in doing something for someone else. There’s value to be found in committing a selfless act for a stranger), I’d take it.

### The Deck to Play: Whatever

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/fc/d3/fcd303e216c832a96e5657bf356203e657af86193465daa8f87aac71517049b4.jpg"><br><i> If you like Dr. Teeth, play him. Just know your deck.</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Before the big crash and burn Saturday, I had a pretty good run Friday, finishing the day 5-0-1 in third place, including a 3-0 record in Standard (Canadian Nats played three rounds of Standard, then two three-round Rochester drafts, then three more rounds of Standard) playing monoblack control. I can say without a doubt this was the right deck to play in light of the metagame. Why? Simple: I knew how to play it better than I did other decks.

Right now, the Standard metagame is ridiculously broad with a good half dozen tier one decks (‘Tog, Wake, u-g, r-g, mbc, Reanimator) and a bunch of close-behind tier twos (elves, zombies, Slide, etc), with everything losing to something else. When it comes right down to it, with that kind of balance in the environment, deck and match up knowledge has to be key. Whether you’re going to U.S. Nats this weekend or just trying to figure out what to play at your local retailer’s, when the options are this evenly matched, trust me when I tell you that the single most important card in Standard is not Wild Mongrel or Withered Wretch or Compulsion, but the card with which you have the most experience. Know your deck like it’s an extension of yourself and I promise you, the results will reflect that fact. Of course, that’s not to say that knowing your Vampire-Angel deck back and forth is going to earn you some tournament victories, but once you’ve verified that your deck in and of itself is tournament viable, knowing it better than the rest will get you the Mox at the end of the tournament. 

### Miser

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/natcan03/898.jpg)You all know by now that Richard Hoaen made the National Team, but you probably haven’t heard how unlikely he was to get there. Considered one of the favorites coming in thanks to his Top 8 in Yokohama, Hoaen started the tournament 1-2 before drafting what he called “a horrible deck”, with which he managed to muster a couple of victories before tragedy struck.

Richard, facing a 2-0 match up against Limited specialist Nick Page’s insane blue-green deck (really monogreen with three Rushes of Knowledge) found that his Constructed deck was missing, saw that his next match up was virtually un-winnable and decided to call it a day at 3-2, informing Scott Larabee that he wished to be dropped from the tournament. With the fifth round still underway though, Richard, buoyed by friends, decided to fight the good fight and asked to be reinstated, which is totally legal as the next round hadn’t begun yet.

Problems arose when the sixth round pairings didn’t include Hoaen. The round started, but Richard wasn’t in the pairings. Organizers paired Richard against Adam Whitehead, who had only four points going into the sixth round and would otherwise have gotten the bye. Whitehead, already eliminated from Top 8 contention, whupped Hoaen good, smashing him in two straight, but decided to drop from the tournament and concede to Hoaen. Richard won his next four rounds and found himself in the Top 8 despite his best efforts. If Canada beats your team at Worlds, you can blame any of the above named. 

### The Long Road Back

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i> If your name is Justice, or Hogan, or Dolan or even Rade, hear me now: this is not the game you left behind years ago.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

In spending the week with Pete Radonjic, the difficulty of reviving a dormant pro career really hit home this week. If you’re not familiar with Radonjic, he used to be a great player, placing fourth at PT-Columbus back in the Tour’s initial season and notching a bunch of other Top 16 and 32 finishes. The man is a competitor, in everything from martial arts to golf to hockey to **Magic**. Hailing from Vancouver, Pete was at the center of Canada’s second most prevalent pro community (following Toronto, of course) for a long time, taking a leadership role in a community that included the likes of Terry Lau, Terry Tsang, Jeff Fung, Jeff Cunningham and Ryan Fuller.

Pete’s comeback this year began around six weeks ago, and while he was convinced a strong Nationals was in the cards, after his 1-5 showing, a crisis of confidence to the extreme had the man wondering whether he’d ever be able to reclaim the lofty heights he’d once held. In chatting with Pete, I came up with a few pieces of advice for anyone trying to get back in:

1.  Don’t start your comeback in May. Pete got schooled at Nats by players of a lower talent level than his because of inferior environmental knowledge. Playing a monoblack control deck he hadn’t played before because of his past affiliation with Necropotence, Pete found himself being outmaneuvered by players who just knew the cards when he didn’t and who knew what to do in order to beat any deck thrown at them because of the familiarity that comes with playing ‘Tog or blue-green for a year and a half.
2.  Confidence is the key. Once Pete started losing, there was no way he was going to start winning. Back in the day, Pete had the eye of the tiger; he’d sit down, convinced he was destined to win and proceed to prove it. With the eye replaced by four years of rust, Pete went to the table *looking* to win, not *knowing* he would. Don’t underrate the difference here: confidence is usually the make-or-break between good and great players.
3.  Don’t underestimate the competition. The game Pete left a few years ago was one where the gap between the pro and everyone else was huge, but ***Magic** Online* has changed all that. Now, Joe Shlabutnick from Moose Jaw can draft ten times a day and get the same exposure to the game our best and brightest can (No offense JoeyS), and as a result, where four years ago drafters were still taking unplayables over dragons, you just won’t see that happing at a tournament as prestigious as Nationals anymore.

If your name is Justice, or Hogan, or Dolan or even Rade, hear me now: this is not the game you left behind years ago. The world has caught up to a degree and it just isn’t as easy to pick the game up and win like it’s 1999 any more. Now, you’ll have to do homework, you’ll have to start all over again, and yes, you’ll have to regain the eye of the tiger. If you can do all that, you’ll find open arms waiting for you. You’re going to have to want it pretty bad to get all the way back though. 

That’s about it for this week. Next time, I’ll be taking a closer look at that second draft from Nats. It’s an interesting one that has a few hard-fought lessons in Rochester draft, and should prove useful in your own future drafting. Teaser? Teaser like a fox!

Have a good week.
