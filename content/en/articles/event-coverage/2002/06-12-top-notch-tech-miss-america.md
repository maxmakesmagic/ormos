The summer is a strange time for internet Magic. As the championship season progresses, results roll in from Nationals around the world, and suddenly the competitors in those tournaments are freed from their silence about the Standard environment and make their discoveries known to the rest of us. It happened with Australian Nationals, with Ben Seck revealing the largely unknown Trenches deck after its success in his home country and in New Zealand, and again with German Nationals, as Kai laid down the law about Psychatog, explaining just why Standstill just doesn't measure up to the power of Probe. During the championship season, "tech" isn't promulgated as it is produced, but rather when it is convenient for those creating it to do so. Nationals' competitors have some vested interest in keeping their playtesting results secret, but once the mad dash for the national title is over, everything is free reign. At long last, U.S. Nationals have come and gone, and with it my self-imposed sabbatical from writing about the Standard environment has come to an end.

Unfortunately, I don't have an exciting new deck to write about that posted the tremendous numbers of Trenches or Psychatog in their respective breakout Nationals, as two of the three members of the team that will be waving the stars and stripes in Sydney – not to mention the alternate as well – earned those spots piloting everyone's favorite toothy monster, which is hardly anything for a deckbuilder to get excited about. I don't even have a tale of misery and woe about how I played the "best deck" and somehow managed to get all "bad matchups" like some internet strategy writers proclaim, as my 4-2 performance with the deck against a wide selection of opponents was hardly pitiable, but hardly exciting either. I don't have a plethora of impressive tournament results, like the remarkable 22-1-1 performance of Deep Dog at English Nationals, but then again I only have my own results, as neither Ben Rubin or Eric Froelich – both of whom wanted to play my deck – could assemble the necessary cards in time. What I do have is an extraordinarily fun and extraordinarily effective deck that I'd play again card-for-card if Nationals were tomorrow, as I'm convinced if my tiebreakers were somewhat better and I ended up Top 8 instead of tied for Top 8, the National crown of the red, white and blue would've gone home with me and Miss America instead.

The beginning of my playtesting for this deck dates all the way back to just after Regionals when I sought to merge the power of Meddling Mage and Lightning Angel with that of Merfolk Looter and the madness mechanic. The deck has clearly evolved a great deal from that form over time, and that's largely because even I wasn't willing to play the mana base required to support Violent Eruption and Meddling Mage consistently. Additionally, as can be seen in the choice of many g-u madness decks to play Aquamoeba in addition to Wild Mongrel and Merfolk Looter despite its relative power level, a deck generally wants at least ten discard effects in order to be able to use madness consistently, and beyond Merfolk Looter, there aren't many attractive options in r-u-w. I went as far as to play a full complement of Merfolk and Cephalid Looters, which were very effective against Psychatog builds, but even still the deck just didn't hold up against the more powerful and consistent Wild Mongrels of opposing madness decks.

I put away the deck for a while, focusing instead on developing a better version of Psychatog for GP-Milwaukee, as I expected that field to consist of a great deal of 'Tog and Trenches decks. Eric Froelich clued me in to the build of Psychatog he'd worked on with Kai at PT-Nice, and we tested fairly extensively in the week leading up to the GP, and I managed to post a Top 8 finish. More important to this article, however, was the fact that Eric Taylor won the tournament with a Trenches deck with both Meddling Mages and Lightning Angel in the sideboard. When I asked him after the tournament what he sideboarded Lightning Angel in against, he replied "Everything". The wheels started turning.

I came back from GP-Milwaukee determined to build a deck that beat Psychatog, Trenches, and Opposition – no easy task, to be sure. I would've been perfectly happy playing a particular build of one of these decks that managed to pull of that feat, as I don't have some sort of irrational compulsion to play "rogue" decks, but there is a decided advantage to be gleaned from playing a deck that people aren't prepared for. Not only do people tend not to have nearly as much effective sideboard hate against you, but will generally sideboard incorrectly based on false assumptions about certain cards you do and don't have in your deck, and will often play incorrectly in the first game based on a similar lack of information. Interestingly enough, the predictability of the field is one of the strengths of Miss America, as Meddling Mage's effectiveness skyrockets against decks whose lists are largely known. While I generally prefer newer Constructed formats due to the advantage good deckbuilders have before the best decks are widely known, established Constructed formats have their charms as well, and the opportunity to design decks to beat the expected field is one of them.

Miss America, then, is certainly not a deck that I would play in an unknown field – in fact, it's not even a deck that I could conceive of designing for an unknown field. The strength of Miss America lies in the power of the particular cards in the deck in the context of the current Standard environment. My initial design for the post-Milwaukee version of the deck came from the idea of just playing Trenches with all of the sideboard creatures in the main deck, which is certainly something that most players who followed EDT's GP win probably considered. Meddling Mage is clearly extremely effective in an environment as well defined as current Standard, and Lightning Angel would certainly be one of the best cards in the format if it weren't for the ever-present Flametongue Kavu. Looking at the Milwaukee environment, however – and, more importantly, the environment produced by the Milwaukee and German Nationals results – shows that Flametongue Kavu is no longer the omnipresent threat that it has been, due in large part to the meteoric rise of Psychatog and Trenches decks, against which Flametongue is suboptimal, as well as the switch of aggressive decks from a r-g base to u-g to best abuse madness effects.

This general environmental shift away from Flametongue Kavu moves Lightning Angel from the realm of the decidedly mediocre to that of the simply fantastic. A 3/4 creature for four mana that doesn't tap to attack is a reasonable deal, but add to that haste and the most underrated creature ability of them all in Constructed – flying – and you have yourself a veritable killing machine. Big enough to block Rootwallas, Elephant tokens, Aquamoebas, and more and live to tell the tale, Miss America is a defensive monster even when on the attack, and her flying attack is something that is far from easy to stop. While Psychatog and Opposition decks try to gum up the ground with Familiars and Squirrels, Lightning Angel soars over them all, coming in for three points of nearly guaranteed damage per turn.

Clearly, however, Lightning Angel and Meddling Mage alone do not make for a deck. They make for a great core of a deck, but they certainly need their share of support. The initial concept of Trenches with maindeck creatures proved rather unexciting rather quickly. Leaving open mana to counter – particularly for Counterspell or for Absorb – while maintaining an aggressive stance wasn't especially effective against control or beatdown decks. Mana management was a terrible problem, because so many of the key cards in the deck had restrictive mana requirements that made playing multiple spells in a turn or playing a threat with counter backup very difficult. While this can be mitigated in base green decks with mana creatures and madness costs, no such option was available for Miss America, which led myself and my playtesting partner Eric Froelich to what has become the signature card of the deck: Sunscape Familiar.

Unlike its black cousin, Sunscape Familiar isn't a very exciting creature in its own right. Whereas Nightscape Familiar can provide a solid regenerating defense against non-trampling attackers, Sunscape's toughness of three hardly stops a single creature these days. Invasion block may have been defined by bears, but today's bear is Wild Mongrel, which punches through Sunscape Familiar without breaking a sweat. Regardless, Sunscape Familiar is not here to serve as a blocker – it's here to be a Sapphire Medallion, and it does that job very well. The ability to play Lightning Angel on turn three is one that can't be ignored, and turning Deep Analysis into a spell costing 2, pay 3 life: draw four cards, with the option of staggering that cost over several turns is nothing to scoff at. That sort of card-drawing power is hard for even Fact or Fiction to compete with.

Most of the remainder of the cards in the deck feed off of Sunscape Familiar as well. Memory Lapse, Repulse, and the Ice side of Fire/Ice are all essential elements to achieving and maintaining a tempo advantage over your opponent, and are all considerably cheaper when the Familiar is involved. Lapse and Repulse are also particularly effective in conjunction with Meddling Mage, allowing you to be sure Pikula's chanting is stopping something your opponent has in his hand. Fire/Ice's primary target is opposing Merfolk Looters, but Birds, Elves, Familiars, and the occasional opponent all fall before its mighty sting, and the ability to tap down a key land or creature at an opportune time can't be discounted either.

When you need more than a one-shot tap job is when Opposition comes in. Most decks that use Opposition are generally designed around the ability to lock down all of your opponent's lands and win at your leisure – they are, indeed, Opposition decks, rather than decks that simply play Opposition. Miss America is hardly one of these decks, and Opposition is included simply because it's a very powerful card that gives the deck answers to cards that would otherwise be problematic, like Spiritmonger and Roar of the Wurm, giving your comparably small creatures free reign of the Red Zone. Opposition gives your Sunscape Familiars a nice job to do beyond reducing your spell costs, and has tremendous synergy with the non-tapping Lightning Angel – not to mention Goblin Trenches, which can provide a huge array of Icy Manipulators almost instantly, setting up a near-lock situation that rivals that of true Opposition decks. Trenches is also an enormous threat against control decks that can completely dominate the late game in short order – it's tremendously synergistic interactions with Opposition are merely a nice bonus to its fundamental role in the deck, which is to create legions of tiny Goblin soldiers to overwhelm your opponents.

Perhaps the best part of the deck, however, is the sideboard. The power of white sideboard cards is perhaps the most tempting reason to play Miss America, as they can simply win games single-handedly. Gainsay is an obvious choice, as are the Aura Blasts and the fourth Flametongue Kavu, but the Acolytes and Worships are where the true power of the sideboard shows through. When I was scrounging for Crimson Acolytes the night before the tournament, most people laughed it off, thinking I was playing some crazy Alter Reality concoction, but when I actually played the Acolytes during the tournament, my opponents certainly didn't find them quite so amusing. Obsidian Acolyte is there against Psychatog decks, its ability making your creatures essentially unblockable at times and protecting them from Ghastly Demise and Possessed Aven – not to mention Recoil, whose stock has jumped tremendously due to the number of Squirrel Nest decks in the field. Crimson Acolyte is equally as devastating against decks that rely on red removal, like g-r beatdown, Trenches, and even the CMU g-u-r Opposition deck Eugene Harvey won Nationals with. As I said early in the article, Lightning Angel is a fantastic card against decks without Flametongue Kavu, and in combination with Crimson Acolyte, Miss America laughs at opposing Flametongues. More importantly in many matchups is the fact that Meddling Mage will never leave the table if he's protected by an Acolyte, keeping your opponents most powerful threats at bay indefinitely. Worship is a catch all against aggressive decks, providing a near lock with Acolyte against some decks, and simply a lock on its own against many of the g-u decks in the field that are incapable of ever removing it or any of your creatures.

So there it is – Miss America. The deck has tested favorably against every major deck type, posting winning records against Tog, Trenches, g-u madness, and Opposition alike, not to mention slews of other less prominent decks. If I could make my deck choice for Nationals again, I'd play the same deck card-for-card. If Nationals were tomorrow, even still I'd play the same deck card-for-card. And if my tiebreakers had been just a bit better – well, how could such a patriotic deck NOT bring home the U.S. title? If you're sick of the rest of the predictable Standard environment and are looking for something outside the box, give Miss America a try. Just remember to sing. "Here she comes... Miss America..."
