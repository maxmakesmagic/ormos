Most of my tournament reports start with some sort of story about my preparation leading up to the tournament. My hours upon hours logged playtesting, my thoughts on the format going in and so forth. That’s what’s so strange about my story from Grand Prix-Boston, though, given the eventual outcome – there was none of that. I didn’t prepare for the tournament at all. When Scott Larabee was doing his Quick Questions Saturday morning and asked me what I felt the best color combination was in *Legions* draft, I could only answer “I have no idea – the first time I saw the cards was this morning”.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i> The first time I saw the cards was this morning…</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

And it was true. I’d never touched a physical *Legions* card until deck registration Saturday morning. I have a Pro Tour-Chicago report half-written on my computer that talks about the enormous impact ***Magic** Online* has had on my playtesting for Limited events – that is to say, I actually do it now, as opposed to prior to the program’s release – so for me, *Legions* wasn’t really in my mind as a draft set because it hadn’t been released online. I’ll just let this excerpt from my never-to-be finished Chicago report explain the situation in more detail:

*People used to say that I was bad at Limited. Some still do, I’m sure. A year or so ago, they were right – I was pretty bad. I never played, so all I had to go on was what other people told me was good, more or less. I was a forger – a competent fake – able to ride my general understanding of the game far enough to always make second day but almost never quite far enough to come home with any money for my efforts. I still don’t practice Limited nearly as much as I do Constructed – I just really enjoy the puzzle aspect of breaking formats and solving metagames – but I do actually play and that simple fact, it seems, has done wonders for my results.*  
*Enter Magic Online. Let’s do a bit of a comparison between my results in premiere Limited events before and after the release of this fantastic program. My records in draft at Nationals and Worlds over two years were a combined 8-9-1 and I’d only made money in one Limited Pro Tour and never at a Grand Prix. Since Magic Online hit the virtual shelves, I’ve gone 5-1 in both Worlds and Nationals and have made money in every Limited Grand Prix, with one Top 8 and two Top 32 finishes. A bit of a difference, to say the least.*

Make that two Top 8s and a win. I suppose just the fact that I have a general familiarity with Limited formats now is as important as my practice with specific cards, as I find myself better able to deal with complex board positions and obscure card interactions. I think Ben Rubin put it best when we were paired for the first time ever during round twelve of the Grand Prix and he asked me “are you ready?” to which I jokingly replied “I’ve been ready my whole life”. His apt response was: “we both know that’s not true”. Finally, it would seem, I am ready.

I wasn’t really aware of that coming into the tournament, however. Due to my lack of preparation, I was actually more excited about the Paul van Dyk show I was seeing at Avalon Thursday night than the tournament itself. Granted, I’m a huge PvD fan and I was going to the show with friends from home whom I rarely get to see, but I still generally look forward to tournaments more than I was this one. If only I had known…

My lack of anticipation for the Grand Prix itself is probably best exemplified by the fact that I nearly missed the tournament Saturday morning. My alarm went off on my cell phone, but apparently I turned it off without actually getting up. It wasn’t until scarcely before the tournament was scheduled to start and I got a phone call from Hegstad telling me to get over there that I actually got up and moving. Of course, I got to site with time to spare because the tournament didn’t start on time, but it was still rather stressful.

I sat next to Gabe Walls for deck building, which makes for interesting foreshadowing. My deck took me nearly the entire allotted time to build and Gabe looked on sympathetically as I struggled with nearly every possible color combination. My pool didn’t really have anything warranting the title “bomb” and pretty much all of the powerful cards were spread out evenly across every color but blue. In the end, I went with a w-r-b build, splashing for black removal. The green looked a bit better than the white, but made my curve far too top-heavy. I was not happy about playing a pair of Daru Sanctifiers and a Dawning Purist just to shore up my morph slots, but in sealed deck you do what you have to do. 

I played a few games against Antonino De Rosa for a bit after deck construction, but soon decided that my time would be much better spent asleep. I headed back to my hotel room and went right to bed, making the most of my three byes. Rubin’s alarm went off and neither of us really wanted to get up, but eventually I mustered the willpower. There was still time left in round three when we arrived at site, but soon enough pairings were up – and lo and behold, I’m a Feature Match.

This would’ve been featured in the coverage, but Gerard Fabiano covered the match and thought it would be funny just to make things up, like “in game three, Huey double mulliganed and Kibler morphed an Exalted Angel on turn four, sealing the match”. For one, Huey didn’t mulligan our entire match and two, my deck didn’t even HAVE an Exalted Angel. Josh Bennett caught wind of Gerard’s little joke just before the report would’ve been posted, so the bogus match didn’t go up. Pretty amusing how close a complete fabrication came to making it on the net, however.

What REALLY happened in my match with Huey was about equally as interesting, however. Game one saw both of us having early mana problems, Huey without a fifth land and me without one of my primary colors. I was still able to cast enough spells to put up a fight, however and Huey couldn’t put enough pressure on me to really punish me. He got me low enough that he started firing off his Words of War at my dome, but I ripped a plains just in time and got Starlight Invoker online. Once I’d stabilized, my Riptide Replicator finished him off. Game two was really uneventful, as Huey pretty much drew nothing but land. He had so little action that my hand at the end of the game was still Pinpoint Avalanche and Cruel Revival, with Skinthinner waiting to be picked up from my graveyard. Nothing he could do about it the way his draws went.

Matches 4-0, Games 2-0

I don’t remember much about this match. Normally, my recollections of matches are very good, but for some reason, this round is just a blur in my head and I’m not quite sure why. I do remember that I came out aggressively in both games, dropping Skinthinner on turn two, which Josh was quite reluctant to trade with. The early damage I was able to force through with the zombie and morph creatures gave me the ability to use my removal very aggressively, forcing Josh into a poor defensive position. Generally, this wouldn’t be a great use of Skinthinner, but my deck had only four sources of black mana, leaving me with little ability to use its morph trigger, so I figured the early pressure it could provide more than balanced out the potential for using it as a Banishing effect. Apparently it did, as I won in two straight games with 2/1 beatdown.

Matches 5-0, Games 4-0

### Round 6 – Maurice Lowles

This match was quite strange. Game one Maurice and I reached a position where he had a great deal of flying pressure on me and I had Essence Sliver but couldn’t attack with it effectively because he had the ground fairly locked up. I drew Riptide Replicator and set it to 3/3 black slivers. Pretty soon, I was able to churn out enough Slivers to more than race his air force while keeping my life total high. He wzs keeping it somewhat close with his Mistform Seaswift, turning it into a sliver every turn so he gained life as well and eventually dropped Future Sight. I managed to overwhelm him before the Sight could make a difference, although I had a Daru Sanctifier if things got out of hand.

Game two, the Sight came down on turn five and I decided I have to go EXTREMELY aggressive to have a chance, as neither of my Sanctifiers were anywhere to be seen. I really don’t understand how I won this game – Maurice repeatedly misplayed his Sight, in my opinion, not optimizing his mana and card use and allowing me to stay in the game when I felt like I really shouldn’t have been. He let me get some damage through that he didn’t have to, didn’t attack me as much as he should have and I started building up an army of 5/5 black clerics courtesy of Replicator. Eventually, he tapped too much mana on his turn and my token-fueled Whipgrass Entangler locked down his entire team so I could swing for the win. Future Sight never left play. 

Matches 6-0, Games 6-0

### Round 7 – Jill Costigan

I was rather surprised when this wasn’t a Feature Match, because the *Sideboard* has a tendency to feature female players who are doing well and for good reason – it’s always good to appeal to an underrepresented demographic. What better opportunity than when a reasonably well-known female player is sitting undefeated at table one? But hey – maybe that’s just me being a glory hound. The round took a while to start and I sat listening to my headphones the whole time. At one point, I noticed the player next to me, playing against Hammer, has his life total listed “Me | Shawn”. I couldn’t help but point this out to Hammer, whose store in New Hampshire was where Nate Clarke, Mark Lepine and I cut our proverbial teeth in competitive **Magic**. He seemed amused as well, quipping “how soon they forget you”.

When the round finally started, I take off my headphones and get down to business. Jill’s deck was a pretty tight b-g beater with a lot of early game as well as late game power. Game one was more or less a straight up race and I managed to break through with Wave of Indifference for the last few points. Game two was much more complex and eventually we were in a pretty stalemated position where I had Goblin Dynamo in play. I managed to off her Smokespew Invoker, leaving her with a morph and a Festering Goblin. Fearing Aphetto Exterminator, I never pinged the Festering Goblin off the board and refused to attack the Dynamo into either it or the morph until I had the board locked down with Crowd Favorites. Sure enough, at the end of the game, Jill flips up Exterminator. Maybe I had some clue about what’s in this “Legions” set after all.

Matches 7-0, Games 8-0

### Round 8 – Jeremy Darling

I’ve never played or met Jeremy before, but I’ve heard his name and he’s 8-0, so I settle in for a battle. Game one wasn’t much of one, as he drew only plains and couldn’t cast any of his black spells. It didn’t take long for me to run him over. Game two, however, Jeremy’s draw was fantastic and I struggled to keep up. My hand, unfortunately, was glutted with high casting cost spells, so I took a lot of damage trying to set up. Eventually, I got down Crowd Favorites and managed to clear his board, but he just dropped Jareth and there was nothing I could do.

Game three was much more interesting. I actually choose to play for the first time all tournament – it’s only the second time I’d had a choice, since I’d lost most coin flips and won all my games so far, but when I did win a flip, I chose to draw. Basically, there’s much less incentive to block now due to the lesser presence of Skirk Commando, so being ahead a turn in morphs with mana untapped isn’t nearly as important. It’s a fine line, I think, but in sealed I’d chose to draw all day long. In draft, it’s a different story, and Jeremy’s deck looked much more like a draft deck, so I went first.

Again, Jeremy’s draw was quite aggressive and I took a lot of damage trying to stabilize. He got a lot of damage in with Entrails Feaster, which slowly grew enormous and I couldn’t ever make advantageous blocks due to his Deftblade Elite – two of the most powerful one casing cost creatures in the format. I managed to kill the zombie cat, but he ripped a Cruel Revival, which took out a key blocker AND got him back his Feaster. It was all downhill from there, although I made a last desperate attack with a Macetail Hystrodon to try to set up a Wave of Indifference kill the following turn. He didn’t bite and made the correct attack (which was simply “attack with everything”) and his morph turned out to be Thrashing Mudspawn, so I died.

Matches 7-1, Games 9-2

### Round 9 – David Howard

I was sitting with my headphones on, shuffling up for the match when David mouths something that I recognize as “do you want to draw?” He seemed somewhat upset when I told him no, but I wasn’t about to let myself feel guilty about it. I come to Grand Prix to do as well as possible and drawing the last round of Day 1 – especially when I’m guaranteed to play on Day 2 even with a loss – doesn’t coincide with that plan. I didn’t draw the last round of Day 1 in Chicago this year and I was OUT with a loss. Then again, that had something to do with my opponent randomly hating a Gustcloak Skirmisher from me during the draft. I cut a Silvos from him in a pack with little for me and then knocked him out of Day 2. Let that be a lesson to you hate drafters out there – I’m vicious.

In any case, I’m not playing David in order to be vicious, but simply because I feel that it’s in my best interests to do so. For some reason, my brain registers that Deacon says “other” before “clerics” and when my opponent points out that it in fact does not, I felt like quite an idiot. I managed to stabilize, however, despite another rather unfortunate brain freeze later in the game involving my Goblin Dynamo somehow not taking out two creatures when it died, instead going to my opponent’s dome. Riptide Replicator came down, making enormous creatures and David couldn’t keep up, so I pulled out a game that I apparently tried very hard to lose.

It is quite easy to play creatures and attack when your opponent has nothing on the board, so I managed to pull that one out. Good thing, too, as I think my brain had decided it was time to shut off for the day.

I headed out to dinner at Fire and Ice (or, as EFro was referring to it all afternoon, “Wax/Wane” or “Spite/Malice”), which is an amazing make-your-own grill restaurant, with a crowd of gamers including, but not limited to, Rubin, Benafel, EFro, Fuller, the OMS brothers and more. Most of the table orders these drinks which come in huge cauldron-like glasses, and I’m barely able to finish mine after downing plate after plate of fantastic grilled-to-order food. When the bill came and the drinks turned out to be $21 a piece, I’m glad I at least gave it my all – unfinished alcohol is bad enough, but when it costs more than your dinner, it’s simply blasphemy. In any case, Rubin and I trudged back to the hotel to get some sleep, discussing draft strategy and color preferences, since we’d both be sitting at table one in the morning. Of course, all of this was just theory, because between us we’d done one draft with *Legions* and that was a three-on-three Ben took part in Friday night. We were going to have to rely on our “steep learning curves”, as I put it – or, as some others prefer, “sheer mastery”.
