![Monk_Idealist](http://www.wizards.com/magic/images/cardart/UZ/Monk_Idealist.jpg)Two weeks ago, I began my "Big Idea" series. If you don't know what a "Big Idea" means to me, [go on back and read it](/en/articles/seriousfun/2006/07-25-whats-the-big-idea). Things have become Too Darn Big to rewind it all and explain it again!

The feedback from the first article was overwhelmingly positive, which I appreciate. **Evaluation** – and the related idea that we should *measure the value we add* in all arenas – is a Big Idea we can all get behind! I hope this next Big Idea will measure up as well.

### The Second Big Idea

Why do we care if we have an impact on a given game of **Magic**? Why do we tweak our decks? Why do we feel good when a play group has lots of people showing up, and feel bad when a play group loses people? Why do we pay attention to people we respect when they give us deck advice (or any other kind of advice)?

We feel this way because we feel responsibility. If we take the time to build a deck, we feel we owe ourselves something (to win, to get it to work right for a little bit, to have fun with it, whatever). If we are in a play group, we feel partly responsible for the health of that group. If someone takes the time to pull us aside and give us something of value (e.g., advice), we feel indebted enough to give them time and attention.

This sense of responsibility is not eternal or absolute. But it's there more often than not, usually because we learned it from our families growing up. (We feel responsible to them for feeling responsible.)

All of these actions of responsibility add up to the Second Big Idea: *accountability*. When we feel accountable, we take responsibility. When we don't, things fall apart – decks, games, groups, and a whole lot more.

### Accounting For Your Deck

As we did two weeks ago, we'll start this Big Idea by placing it in one of the most basic of **Magic** frames: a deck.

The root of the word *accountability* lies in the verb *to account (for)*. When you build a deck, the most simple accounting you can do is to make sure it's legal in the format you're about to play. Does it have sixty cards? Does it have no more than four of each card, not including basic lands? And so on.

We all do that sort of accounting quite well, I presume – so well that we don't even consider our actions related to accountability. But we're showing respect for the format and the rules of the game.

A step past legal accounting is accounting for the *performance* of your deck. Does your deck have "enough" lands? Technically, you don't have to have any lands in your deck, and it's still legal in most known formats. But it would be foolish to play such a deck and expect any play value from it.

It's only slightly less foolish to play a deck that has way too many - or way to few - lands for its size. Many writers on this site will push you to consider 24 mana-producing lands out of a 60 card deck. I will, too. (Hint: Dark Depths does not count as a mana-producing land!) 

These writers will also push you to have no more than 60 cards in your deck. Again, I second that motion. (There are reasons to have more cards; for example, if one of the things that makes you really, really happy is having an unpredictable deck. I know players like this. That's cool. They know what they're doing and why, and they accept the consequences.)

After these deck-building basics (and you can find more in [Ted Knutson's Saturday columns](https://web.archive.org/web/20080502022935/http://www.wizards.com/default.asp?x=mtgcom/columnarchive&column=MagicAcademy)), you also have to consider the games your group plays and how your deck should be prepared.

Here's a more advanced example from about four years ago (*Onslaught* block days). Naturalize had just come out, and I was putting it in all of my green decks. While having a conversation with my friend Todd "Kobe-Trippin'" Petit about the deck changes, he told me something interesting.

"I almost never play with Disenchant effects."

"That's not true," I replied immediately without thinking. "I've seen you play white. You play white, you play Disenchant, or cards like it."

"Not really. I have Monk Realists in my Equilibrium-Aluren deck, and that's it."

I paused long enough to scan my memory for a play Todd may have made that got rid of a pesky board enchantment. *Surely*, I told myself, *I can think of a time when the group was suffering under the thumb of a nasty combo or control player, and Todd rode in on a white stallion and saved us all.*

But the more I thought about it, the more certain I was he had never rode in on a white stallion to save anybody, or even a red mule. In fact, *he* was often the oppressor, and someone else was always the person who finally overthrew him.

"You're right," I finally allowed through gritted teeth. "You're a dork. Why don't you play Disenchant effects? You know, for other jerk's enchantments and artifacts?"

A smug smile spread across his features. "I don't have to. You play four in just about every deck. So does Bob. I just wait for you to do the job for me."

At first, I thought this was very "clever" – you know, in the way that my dog Turquoise is clever when she picks lollipops out of a ceramic bowl on the kitchen counter without pulling the bowl so far to the edge of the counter that it falls and breaks. (You can't punish a dog in this situation, can you? I mean, my kids break bowls all the time just picking them up out of the dishwasher. The dog hasn't dropped one yet…*and she has no opposable thumbs*. Whom would I rather keep around? And why do we keep buying lollipops, anyway? I don't even like lollipops.)

But after thinking it through with the help of a tasty orange lollipop, I decided Todd wasn't clever at all. And I was just the one to show him why.

![Scalpelexis](http://www.wizards.com/magic/images/cardart/JUD/Scalpelexis.jpg)The next game (or so I'd like to assume…justice is swift in my ideal world), four or five of us are playing chaos format. I'm playing a new U/G/r beast deck with stuff like Contested Cliffs and Scalpelexis and Ravenous Baloth and even a Sand Squid, which is a neat solution to many things in a beast deck.

Todd is playing something annoying – I'm playing the percentages and going with Blue/Green here. He's got some beef on the table and it's causing a ruckus.

Paul is playing a mono-black and artifact control deck. He plops down an Ensnaring Bridge, thus putting an end to Todd's fun.

"Wow," I say as I look at the shiny Naturalize in my hand, "I wish I had a Naturalize for that."

"You'll draw one," Todd assures me.

"No, I don't think I will. You see, I don't have any Disenchant effects in this deck."

Todd looks up. "Really? You always play Disenchant effects."

"No, *you* always play Disenchant effects," I told him. "Don't you?"

"No."

"Huh." I paused as I caressed the Naturalize. "Then I guess we're both pretty f\*#@ing stupid, aren't we?"

And then I pinged him a bunch of times with my Scalpelexis flock, milling him out of the game. *Then* I played the Naturalize on Paul's Ensnaring Bridge. (I think I died the turn after that, to triple Subversions.)

Todd still doesn't play Disenchant effects in every deck; but I noticed the percentage go up a bit. Of course, part of that probably has to do with the fact that Naturalize is green, and he (like any sane soul) enjoys green more than white.

Do I still need to explain the point? Very well. Todd needed to take responsibility for the success of his own decks. He accounted for a critical part of the metagame – enchantments and artifacts – but not in his own assets. Some of you out there may think it's super-smart and Machiavellian to count on other players' spells to win you the game; but the problem with that logic is that for every capacity you take away from the deck, you cede more and more control of the game state to people who don't like you. (I mean, we *like* Todd. But we don't *like him* like him. Get it? No? I'll pass you a detailed note in gym class.)

If you lose a game because someone *else* didn't Disenchant a Confusion in the Ranks, or someone *else* didn't Terror a Rhox, or someone *else* didn't Stone Rain a Scrying Sheets, then stop whining. You lost a game because *your* deck didn't produce. If the card wasn't even in your deck, then you have even less case to complain: you didn't even show up prepared.

### Accounting For Your Play

One of my favorite **Magic** articles was written by this site's own Content Manger, Scott Johns. He wrote it in his pre-Wizards days, and it's called "[You Suck](https://web.archive.org/web/20060808000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://web.archive.org/web/20020222023102/magic.mindripper.com/Index.cfm?ArticleID=1585&SectionID=1&Show=All&origin=mtgcom_daily_aa240)." If you haven't read it, please do so. Too few **Magic** players have.

Scott's article is important because it speaks to the behavior many **Magic** players exhibit when they lose. Sometimes the behavior is obvious and obnoxious. Sometimes the behavior is more subtle – but it's just as destructive (possibly more so) to the player himself, since no one can see or hear what that player is telling himself.

It all boils down to accounting correctly for your mistakes, and not fobbing off every loss on "luck".

Every game, there are four forces that are pushing on you to lose. One is your opponent's skill. One is your opponent's good luck. One is your own bad luck. And the last is your unrelenting ability to screw up.

Now here's a thought experiment for you. If you play twenty games and lose ten, do you reflect upon those ten games and distribute the blame for those losses among those four factors? Do you recognize all four factors in every loss? Because believe me, they're all there. Not always in proportion, not always in clear and apparent ways. But I guarantee you: every game involves all four forces pushing upon you, begging you to lose. And if you do lose, all four forces celebrate together.

Getting your attitude right after you lose can be a hard thing. But the rewards are considerable: you'll probably lose less. In the meantime, you'll make the people who beat you a lot happier. (Don't they deserve a reward, too?)

When you win a game, which of the following reactions makes you respect your opponent more:

1.  *Firm handshake*. "Good game. You played well and took advantage of my mistakes."
2.  *Sloppy handshake and the revelation of the top ten cards of their library*. "Good game. Of course, if I had drawn my Rorix Bladewing and three Mountains all on my next turn, you were toast!"
3.  *No handshake – just lots of arm-waving and spittle*. "You got so lucky! I hate this game! Any noob can win!"

Since we all expect (or at least appreciate) (a) when we win, we should do no less than (a) when we lose.

### Accounting For Your Words

Bending away from core **Magic** mechanics, I'd like readers to think about how they grapple with **Magic** issues. Talking about what's right in **Magic** – and what may be wrong – is a passion for some. 

I love a lot of the passionate message board posts, emails, articles, and other electronic discussion out there. The stuff I like tends to be both passionate and well-reasoned. You get the sense that the writer has taken the time to read what others have written, and that she has done enough homework that her arguments are clear and supported by facts.

This sort of writing is my favorite because it means the author feels accountable to the audience. She cares what others will think of her opinion, and so she takes the time to make it as rational as possible.

What breaks that sort of accountability? Anonymity. Distance someone from their true identity – make it hard for someone to track who really said that – and some people will shout the most ridiculous things, just because they can. 

This is the reason I simply delete emails from addresses like blah@blah.com, without ever reading them. I knew from sampling (before I adopted this policy) that the people who used this anonymous service weren't always obnoxious; but the emails run about 50/50 between folks who write a readable email and those who just write junk. Compare that to those who write from traceable email addresses, where the ratio is more like 90/10 on a typical week.

There are certainly good reasons for anonymous communication – many people live in countries where there's political persecution. More commonly in America, some people just hate junk email. I can get behind that.

Where the irony strikes me as strong is when the anonymous voices pretend to care about accountability: "We're the customers and Wizards should do exactly as we say!" So many of these people who want to hold Wizards' staff feet to the fire would never dream of putting their own names, reputations, and creations out there under their real name, for other people to criticize.

Being responsible in communication can be scary. If you don't know something, if you do something wrong, if you decide you need to apologize – these are all things adults struggle with, much less teenagers. But if you pick up the skill, you'll learn quickly that the fastest, easiest way to deal with a mistake is to push through it and get it over with. Short, clear, perhaps humorous (read: self-deprecating) statements are the bread and butter of good communicators.

You want Wizards to listen to you? You want to change people's minds, about a game or anything else? You want to change a political process? Take responsibility for your own words. Do your homework. Put the arguments together right. Add value with what you say (and borrowing from two weeks ago, evaluate your ability to do so). If you goof, own up to it quickly and clearly. Sign your name at the end of everything you say, so people know whom to admire. All of these are courtesies used among people who want to forge lasting, meaningful relationships.

### Accounting For Your Actions

Once you have better control over what you say, you might want to think through how accountable you are for the things you *do* in life.

Why aren't you on the Pro Tour? Because you don't practice enough.

Why did you get a low grade in that class? Because you didn't study enough.

Why did that relationship not work out? Because you both had difficulties, not just one of you.

I used to work with a guy (years ago, not in my current job) who would come into work every day and just whine and complain about how awful it was there. "I hate this place!" "So-and-so keeps screwing things up – he's the worst boss ever!" "I never get to do anything good in my job!" And so on. I shared a double-sized cubicle with this guy – he drove me completely nuts. (I was otherwise happy with my job. These were nice people, and useful work!)

Finally, I had had enough of this fellow. While he was in the middle of his latest rant (he had just been assigned back-up reception duty, which was beneath him), I turned to him and snapped:

"Dude. If you hate this job so much, *why are you still working here?*"

This shut him up for a blissful two and a half days. Then he started acting up again, except louder. After three or four more weeks, our boss fired him.

Our boss did him a huge favor. (Me too, but we're focusing on his lessons learned.) He landed in a different job that he liked a lot more. He could have had a job like that months before, maybe years! Yet he stuck in a job he hated. Why did he do that to himself?

I'm not a psychologist, but most people torture themselves – stay in jobs they hate, drag out relationships that hurt them, pursue college majors and careers that they're not even interested in – because they don't have enough confidence in themselves to expect better.

One of the coolest things the beautiful but devious MaryJanice ever did was quit her day job, in the middle of a recession, to pursue writing full-time. I don't want this to sound too reckless – she had her first print publishing contract in hand, as well as a spouse with a job that could support two children if necessary – but she took a calculated risk because she believed in herself. (I believed in her too. I was thrilled to be proven right.)

The day MaryJanice quit – it was on her birthday, a present to herself – she took responsibility for her own happiness. She didn't count on her boss to do it for her. (He was a bit of a jerk, so she would have been waiting a while.) She didn't count on her husband or children to do it for her. She didn't even expect a publisher or agent to do it for her. She took the leap herself.

If you're under 18 and reading this, you are going to run into *thousands* of situations that will anger or worry you by the time you're 36 like me. The second eighteen years are full of them – bad bosses, erratic lovers or spouses, jobs that crash and burn, and parents who appear a bit smaller every time you visit them. And that's before you add kids!

Don't count on these people to make you happy. Sure, they have a responsibility to be civilized. But they're not in your life to carry you through it. Hold yourself accountable for your own actions. Set goals, and take the time to measure your progress every few weeks, months, or years.

The bottom line of accountability is about respect – for those around you, but most of all for yourself. Live life like you're setting up the perfect board position for your coolest **Magic** deck – expect great things from yourself. You deserve it. Right?
