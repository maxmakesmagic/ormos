### 

Baseball's All-Star Game is almost upon us. For those of you not familiar with the concept of the All-Star Game, American professional baseball's two leagues, managed respectively by the coaches of the previous year's league champion, forge teams of the finest individual players to represent the league in one exhibition game. It's a fun-filled media extravaganza featuring dream matchups between pitchers and batters that, for one game, lets baseball's finest become kids again, and reminds them of why they wanted to be baseball players in the first place.

I grew up with an extraordinary passion for baseball, and while that passion has been wanting ever since a player's strike in the mid-nineties ended a season prematurely, I still look back on the education it provided me within the realm of games as a source of inspiration for **Magic's** promotion. This week's Wise Words is about an institution that game has that ours does not.

As I watched the World Cup over the last month, I realized that one of the few things lacking in football (soccer) is a recorded history that would really help celebrate the game's tradition. Oh sure, you can look up the past winners in sports almanacs and the like and a handful of the greatest goals ever are remembered, but really, football is a game that lives in the now. That's good enough for most. Not for me though. I like having my history nice and organized, spread out before me in all its glory at my beck and call. When you leave history in the hands of oral tradition, stories inevitably change, often to the point where what you have is no longer history: It's folklore. That's why baseball's having a Hall of Fame is so important to the institution that game has become in the United States and, more recently, around the world: the game's history, its greatest players, its greatest moments and greatest feats, are all preserved in one beautiful little building in Cooperstown, New York, for the world to see through glass cases. At Pro Tour-DC in 1999, another of baseball's converts to **Magic**, Randy Buehler, put the following question to an assembled group of **Magic** historians: "Who are the best 5 players ever?" 

### The Best of the Best

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptnor01/a757.jpg)**Kai Budde** - First off, I did this alphabetically, but I guess Kai has earned the right to be first in everything. He was first in five Pro Tours: World Championships 1999, Chicago 2000, Barcelona 2001, New York 2001 and New Orleans 2001. He was first to win three, four and five Premier-level events. He was the first to win three Grand Prixs. He was first in this year's **Magic** Invitational and his National Championships, won fifteen straight matches on Sunday and became the first player to win Worlds, Limited and Constructed Pro Tours, but none of these feats are responsible for what makes Kai so impressive. Instead, it's the fact that if you put more than two players at any pro tournament together for half an hour, the conversation inevitably rotates back to the Juggernaut. When your peers can only look on in wonder, trying to pick up a thing or two from watching you play, you've risen above, and that's precisely what Budde has done. He's taken the game to the next level, playing every day, morning 'til night, creating the closest thing the world has ever seen to the perfect **Magic** player. After Pro Tour-New York, with Kai having won three of the previous six Pro Tours, Randy Buehler, was still sticking to his Finkel-guns with regards to the best player in history debate. When asked what Kai had to do to become number one, his answer was simple: "win New Orleans". When Kai did just that, the critics were silenced. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/MASTERSBAR01/695.jpg)**Jon Finkel** - Before Kai came along, the idea of anyone surpassing Finkel seemed ludicrous. Nine times, Finkel has rode his incredible talents into Pro Tour Sunday competition, a number that hasn't been approached, with the longevity argument still suggesting a case could be made for Finkel as the best ever. Regardless of how he measures up to Kai, Jon's accomplishments speak for themselves: nine Sundays, wins at Pro Tour-New York 1998 and World Championships 2000, possibly the most dominant performance ever at U.S. Nationals 2000, victory at the 2000 Invitational, multiple Grand Prix and so on and so on and so on. Jon has done it all, providing the game with its greatest poster boy and best-known son this side of Richard Garfield. Jon's true legacy? He'll forever be the first guy who had kids wanting to be him just for his Magicing skills. Kids today 'want to be Finkel' the way they wanted to be Michael Jordan, Reggie Jackson or John McEnroe. Finkel's legend has become so great that when people see his 6'3" frame, they say 'I thought he would be taller', almost disappointed that he fit human proportions. Even Jon now knows that Kai has surpassed him in some respects, but for lists like this one, his inclusion is as easy as the German's. After this is where it gets hard. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/6f/6b/6f6b6bd62ea7c2603f4f9100dea1c2aa0fd99fd8670626dad3143cbc02b02f19.jpg)**Tommi Hovi** - He has no flare. You ask him a question in an interview, you invariably get a one word answer because he can't be bothered to go into it more in English. He comes off as distant, maybe even a little xenophobic, sticking to the Finnish gamers like fleas on… well, you get the picture. Still though, the quiet exterior is what makes Tommi Hovi so mean: you don't get bashed by trash talk and you don't get animated antics: All you get is his **Magic** and that's where he beats you.

Hovi primarily makes this list for his distinction of being the first player to win more than one Pro Tour. The two Worlds Top 8s are nice, as are the multiple Invitationals, Grand Prix successes et al., but Hovi stands as a symbol of the game's metamorphosis from one of luck to one of skill in the close observer's mind's eye. Before Tommi's second victory in Rome, no one player had won more than one Premiere-level event, fueling the theory that **Magic** was primarily a game of luck and the belief that making a living from it was a ludicrous proposition. When Tommi won Rome, the face of the game changed to one of skill, giving birth to the concept of the pro player. Throw in the fact he was a pretty good player at a time when Europe was still lagging behind the U.S. by and large and you have a more than worthy inductee. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/MASTERSCHI00/906.jpg)**Mike Long** - So much to say, so little space. While Budde and Finkel are the game's greatest players, Mike Long more than anyone has introduced controversy, speculation, debate, outrage, paranoia, discussion, bewilderment and wonder to the following of the Pro Tour story. "I think I'm the Michael Jordan of **Magic**" Long said in the 1998 U.S. Nationals video, and while he never quite dominated the game the way 'His Airness' did on the court, he certainly did in the headlines.

How history will remember Long is anyone's guess. Great player? Yes. Cheat? Maybe. Villain? Absolutely. No card outside of the Invitational prizes has been as synonymous with any one player as Cadaverous Bloom with Long, and that's his legacy. Often implicated, seldom caught, Mike gave the game it earliest and greatest storylines, and on top of that, the man could play. He won Pro Tour-Paris, he made three U.S. National Teams and two other U.S. Nationals Sundays, he made numerous PT Top 8s and Top 16s and won an Invitational. Mike is as good as anyone at focusing on a new format and gaining a fast understanding of how to best exploit it, and at the same time is a master manipulator who always seems to find his way back into the spotlight. Those factors, along with an unmatched will to win, are what put him here.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/GPHEL00/104.jpg)**Olle Rade** - He looks so safe. You come to the table and this quiet, little blond kid squeaks a hello and you figure 'I can take this scrub', but the quiet demeanor is a ruse. What lies beneath is one of the largest, fiercest predators on the planet, and when that beast holds sixty cards in hand, its will turns to action and before you know it, you're dead. That's Olle Rade. In the game's history, no one player has the record to match Olle's in Constructed play, and to call him the greatest Constructed player ever is not a stretch. 

Five times Rade placed in the Top 8 of Constructed-inclusive Pro Tours, his first being at Pro Tour-Columbus, which he won. He won an Invitational, a Player of the Year crown, a Grand Prix here and there and did it all with no volume to his voice and no need for antics and hyjinx. Rade just came to play, and he did it as well as anyone. In addition to his playing and deck building accomplishments, Olle made one other important contribution to the game: along with old friend Tommi Hovi, Rade's ability to recognize a card's set of origin from the back of the card led to one of the game's biggest innovations: the institutionalization of the card sleeve. It's not what he'll be remembered for, but it's worth a mention as part of Olle's legacy.

Of these five, it feels like Hovi's place here is the most precarious, with time making multiple Pro Tour wins less important than it once was. Here are the guys who missed the above list by the narrower of margins, with future success potentially usurping the above:

**Dirk Baberowski** - The only man with more than one Pro Tour victory to not make the top five, if Dirk's second win had come in a singles tournament, he'd probably make the above list by default, but he's down here because of longevity. Yes, he's still playing while his first Pro Tour win came four years ago, but Baberowski has retired more times than Michael Jordan, and those gaps in his career leave him short of the credentials of the big five. Still, he possesses one of the keenest minds to have tackled the M:tG puzzle, has three Pro Tour Top 8 finishes and a number of strong GP showings to boot. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/USNAT01/727.jpg)**Chris Benafel** - Say what you will about the man's politics, you can't argue with the facts. Chris is in the top 5 of all time prize winnings, thanks in part to an extraordinary dedication to his craft, as evidenced by his willingness to travel farther to game than anyone not named Shvartsman. Chris may well be top five material when all is said and done, as he's only been at it for less than three years, but the lack of that one major win will shorten his measuring stick until he wins a PT.

**Randy Buehler** - No one would tell you he belongs on this list more than Randy, but when the man has a point, the man has a point. Buehler made history by winning his first Pro Tour at Chicago '98, but it's the remarkable consistency that followed that really made him worth watching. Until Shvartsman came along, Randy was the undisputed king of the Grand Prix and made it apparent to anyone watching that going pro was an option, writing articles and books, making guest appearances and arranging sponsorships the likes of which had seldom been seen before him. The lack of longevity will forever hurt his cause in debates like this one, but his ascendance to R&D should help speculators forgive.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/Worlds2001/833.jpg)**Alan Comer** - Alan comes in near the bottom of this list, but his exclusion would leave it incomplete. Alan's been around since the beginning, and his inspiration is far from that of mere mortals, caring not for the money so much as the respect and fun involved with high-level **Magic**. While his five PT Top 8s are what put him here, it's his unusual deck decisions and remarkable deck designing talents that separate him from the mercenaries of the game, with decks like Godzilla, Turbo Xerox and most notably, Miracle Grow changing environments and affecting gamers around the world.

**Rob Dougherty** - Not all of the greats start out that way. Six times, Rob Dougherty qualified for Pro Tours only to go home empty-handed before finally finishing in the Top 8 at Pro Tour-New York 3, the first of four times he'd do so. That said, while Rob has emerged as one of the best players today, his accomplishments don't measure up to those of 'The Class', but he gets included here because of the impressive communal contributions he's made, from writing internet articles to running one of the most famous gaming shops in the world: Your Move Games. Around since the beginning of time, Rob may be a better Hall candidate as builder than player, but put a sixty card deck (usually of his own design) in his hands and you have a losing situation if you're sitting across from him. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/USNAT01/880.jpg)**Scott Johns** - Five Top 8s and a team Pro Tour win are pretty good, as is his feat of making three Top 8s in a row. Scott isn't the most beloved man on the Tour, but you can't argue with the many facets of his game: he's made huge contributions to the internet community, been part of one of the most successful deck-design teams in history, bridged the gap for the past to the present and was a member of the most influential team of all time, PCL. Throw in a remarkable 2000-2001 season that saw him win a PT and make money at all of them and you have a guy who has only been limited in his career by his own decisions to take time off from the Pro Tour.

**Mark Justice** - The other PCL member on this list, Mark has to be considered that team's best player, considered by most to be the game's greatest before Finkel came along. Justice was a full-time pro before there was such a thing as a full time pro, running a store while winning U.S. Championships and making two PT finals. Mark never won the big one, a fact that will hurt history's view of his accomplishments, as will the sketchy reputation he validated by admitting to the tactics he once employed, but there were a lot of players doing pretty much the same back then, and none of them put up results as consistently as the ironically named American.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/MI00/984.jpg)**Darwin Kastle** - Like others on this list, Darwin doesn't make the big five thanks to his inability to take an individual PT title, but he's done enough to warrant serious consideration anyway. He's been to every single Pro Tour, making numerous Top 8s. He won a team PT, won GP-Manchester, won an Invitational and ranks second all time in PT points, trailing only Finkel himself. Throw in internet articles and extraordinary consistency over the years in a constantly changing game and you have one of the greats.

**Zvi Mowshowitz** - He won a Pro Tour, came second in a Junior one, made two other Top 8s and made a National team. He's possessed of one of the great deck building minds the game has seen, been a core member of two of the more important teams in the game's history and made a huge contribution to the furthering of the online and printed **Magic** communities. Opinionated, blunt, arrogant, brilliant, insightful and usually right: those and a thousand other words would be needed to fully explain Zvi.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptsd02/a893.jpg)**Steve O'Mahoney-Schwartz** - School and other facets of real life have taken their toll on Steve's Magicing of late, but that doesn't diminish what he's done throughout his career. He's shown himself to be the best Rochester Drafter in the game's history. Twice he made his way to the finals of Rochester PTs, once emerging victorious over his best friend and teammate Jon Finkel, with whom he enjoyed success on the team front. In the end though, his lack of results in Constructed leave him down here.

**Mike Pustilnik** - The truest of all gamers on the Pro Tour, Mikey P plays his own decks, shunning the technological possibilities the internet provides and has persevered despite. He won a Pro Tour. He won a Masters. He won a couple Grand Prix, made three Top 8s and has been around since PT-NY 1. Mikey has gone through bad periods where he even went so far as to fall off the gravy train, but you can't argue with the facts. The American Titan champion loves the game: fortunately, it's loved him back.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/Worlds2001/751.jpg)**Kyle Rose** - Quietly, very quietly, this monster of a man has compiled one of the greatest resumes in PT history. He won a Pro Tour (London). He made three other Top 8s. He won a U.S. National Championship, has made thousands upon thousands and may well be, with apologies to Dave Price, the best beatdown player to ever turn the creatures sideways. His dedication to the game has waned of late, but that didn't stop him from winning GP-New Jersey this past weekend and won't stop him from competing in the Teams Masters this year either. The lack of drama and communal contribution leave him wanting for the big five, but he's near the top of this list. 

**Ben Rubin** - Four Top 8s and two Masters wins. Little else needs to be said, but look at the facts. This guy made his first PT final when he was fifteen. He could have been playing in JSS. Rubin is a deck builder, a drafter, a player, a teammate and possibly the most mature kid the Pro Tour has ever seen, but in the end, it's his results that have made him one of the game's greatest. It should be interesting to see what effect university will have.

![](http://www.wizards.com/bios/images/jakub_slemr_lg.jpg)**Jakub Slemr** - The first great World Champion, Jakub will always be one of the truly good guys of the PT, managing to maintain that face and succeed in the Pro Tour wars at the same time. He won the 1997 World Championship, made two other Top 8s, succeeding in both Limited and Constructed while being a member of two teams, Legion and Mogg Squad, that helped shape the future of competitive **Magic**. Jakub doesn't really come that close to the top 5, but his accomplishments merited mention here.

One note before we end things for this week. I wrote this knowing full well that the list is dominated by Americans, but I think that as far as the history of our game goes, there is some accuracy to the notion that the USA and it citizens really have shaped this game more than any others. I hope that as you read this, this isn't a reflection on the present state of the game so much as a look back at what once was. Time will change all things, but we have to remember what was in order to appreciate change as it comes. Hope you enjoyed this and will perhaps write something of your own if you disagree with my choices. Have a good week.
