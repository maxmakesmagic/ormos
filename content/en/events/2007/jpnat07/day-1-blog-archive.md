     <a name="2"> </a>&#10;      <li>&#10;        <b>Friday, Aug 31: 10:33 a.m. - Day 1: Less Heralded Heroes</b>&#10;        <div>&#10;          <i>by Eli Kaplan</i>&#10;        </div>&#10;      </li>&#10;      <br>&#10;      <br>&#10;      <p>Make no mistake. Japan's had a quiet year, at least in comparison to the blockbuster achievements the nation's players have achieved in the last two years. The achievements of Europe's <b>Magic</b> powerhouses can't be understated. But there's a depth and breadth of experienced and technical play prevalent throughout the deep player base of Japan. If you've read any web coverage of recent events, you're certain to be familiar with the very top of the Japanese field. But the roster of less recognized yet solid talent's so deep. I've been watching top level play in the Land of the Rising Sun for years, and watched greats like Katsuhiro Mori and Masashiro Kuroda break their way out of the pack and into the limelight. Japanese Nationals is the premiere springboard for hungry players to establish their rep. Here's my four picks you ought to keep your eyes on this weekend.</p>&#10;      <p>&#10;        <b>Ryou Ogura</b>&#10;      </p>&#10;      <p>Nagoya's finest made the spotlight at Worlds last year, falling to Mihara in the finals. Ogura's made the Top Eight at two Worlds events. He's got great mental endurance, a tool necessary for excellence in long, grueling tournaments. The ability to switch gears in tournaments with multiple formats is absolutely essential to reach the top tables, and Ogura's got that talent in spades. </p>&#10;      <p>Ogura brings a nonchalant, lighthearted tone to the table, but he obsesses over the details of Constructed metagames. Japanese deckbuilders have always trended towards control, so he's almost always tuned whatever deck he brought to savage control decks. He's been one rung from the top of the ladder before. This could be the weekend he finally gets the big trophy. </p>&#10;      <p>I'm sick and tired of him beating the crap out of me regularly at Friday Night <b>Magic</b>. </p>&#10;      <p>&#10;        <b>Shingo Kurihara</b>&#10;      </p>&#10;      <p>I shouldn't have to introduce Shingo Kurihara. Kurihara's breakout performance with two Pro Tour Sunday appearances cemented his name as one of the strongest Rookies of the Year in memory. Unlike many Japanese breakout stars I've watched over the years, Kurihara's primary forte lies in Limited. </p>&#10;      <p>Kurihara's approach to the game really stands out in comparison to many American and European stars. He doesn't like to take compliments. He bemoans his imperfections and errors in his Constructed play, saying he's been practicing but doesn't feel like his work has paid off dividends yet. Does that deter him? No way. Kurihara has been putting his nose to the grindstone for the last few months, and he has every shot to reap the fruits of victory. </p>&#10;      <p>&#10;        <b>Naoki Shimizu</b>&#10;      </p>&#10;      <p>It's a little counterintuitive to call a member of last year's National team a diamond in the rough. Shimizu's been toiling away for months with a pack of talented teammates in search of the ideal control build. I'd have a hard time picturing him hammering away at opponents with giant monsters. </p>&#10;      <p>Watching Shimizu reminds me of watching old videos of Harry Houdini doing what only Houdini could do. Houdini would look at every potentially deadly hazard as a puzzle to work out. Shimizu approaches each game with the same mental focus, trying to react to his opponent's moves and nullify them so that he can take the game home in the long run. We'll see if the metagame's right for Shimizu's honed skill set.</p>&#10;      <p>I'm rooting for Shimizu to win again because I love to watch a player who genuinely loves <b>Magic</b>. Shimizu's face betrays little emotion in the game's midst, but before and after the game, he's always smiling. What beats a good game of <b>Magic</b>? Masashiro Kuroda used to be the leading public face of Japanese players until he made a serious commitment to the salaryman lifestyle, and I'd say Shimizu would be an ideal guy to step up to that role.</p>&#10;      <p>&#10;        <b>Yuuya Watanabe</b>&#10;      </p>&#10;      <p>Did someone mention Standard? Japan's prowess in that format is without peer. Hailing from the crowded card shops of Tokyo, Yuuya Watanabe broke out of anonymity at Grand Prix Kyoto this year with a vicious Izzetron remix that laid the field low. He's been playing the long Tokyo afternoon and evening Standard battles for years. Kenji Tsumura's credited him as a solid practice partner throughout lean years and prosperous years. Watanabe's a true fan of Blue, but is fond of cramming a few more threats into his sixty cards. </p>&#10;      <p>Japanese Nationals will be the testing grounds to see if he can demonstrate talent with Limited decks. Unlike Kurihara, he wasn't too talkative about his efforts to step up. He and Kenji squeaked into Day Two in <i>Time Spiral</i> Two-Headed Limited, and rallied back all the way to fifth place, one spot shy of a Pro Tour Sunday.        </tr>&#10;      </tbody></table>&#10;      <p>Neither of these players are strangers to the Top 8 of a Japanese event. The Kanagawa Prefecture's Masaya Kitayama may not be a household name to you, but he was the anchor of Team Standard Constructed Grand Prix Hamamatsu's winners, Tanii Monogatari. He's no slouch at draft either, sweeping Pod 1 3-0. Well, he almost swept it. It turns out that when there are four rounds in a draft, crazy things happen such as Kitayama facing off against Tokyo's Jun'ya Iyanaga, the 2005 Japanese National Champion. Well, Iyanaga would have been the champion if not for being in a room with video cameras, trailblazing judges, and a controversial play involving Sword of Fire and Ice. Controversy aside, Iyanaga was performing well again at Nationals and a 6-1 record would allow him to stay near the front of the pack.</p>&#10;      <p>&#10;        <b>Game 1</b>&#10;      </p>&#10;      <p>After a short stopover in Paris, Kitayama started off with a deadly series of drops, suspending <a>Ivory Giant</a> on turn one <a>Shade of Trokair</a> on turn two, and actually deigning to play a <a>Sinew Sliver</a> right then and there on turn three. Iyanagi was stuck on two forests. He tried to mount a comeback by slowing down the <a>Sinew Sliver</a> with <a>Utopia Vow</a>, but that just gave Kitayama access to the red mana he needed to play <a>Oros, the Avenger</a> on his fifth turn. </p>&#10;      <p>Iyanaga just laughed and scooped up his cards.</p>&#10;      <p>&#10;        <b>Game 2</b>&#10;      </p>&#10;      <p>With a big smile Iyanagi announced he'd keep his hand. Kitayama shrugged and kept a hand on the draw with 5 lands, Knights of Sursi, and <a>Sunlance</a>. He topdecked Trespasser il-Vec the next turn, giving him a sigh of relief, but he was in hot water. Iyanaga queued a Giant Duskwasp into the Phantom Zone with a four turn sentence, and followed up with <a>Dreamscape Artist</a>. Kitayama's Trespasser was allowed one turn of shadowy infiltration before being pressed into a <a>Utopia Vow</a>, and this time there was no Dragony consequences. </p>&#10;      <p>Kitayama kept drawing land after land after land while Iyanagi used the combination of <a>Tolarian Sentinel</a> and <a>Llanowar Empath</a> to churn out an army in a hurry - including one Empath into Empath turn.
