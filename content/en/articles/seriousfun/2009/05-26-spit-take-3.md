What would look like if you put one copy of every card in *Shards of Alara* block on a table? Ridiculous? Intimidating? Surprisingly shiny?

I recently had an opportunity to do exactly that when some folks around the office organized a Rotisserie Draft of the entire block, and it looked like this:

![](https://i.ormos.uk/bc/2d/bc2da0d38a54f49213efeb68a223fccca29f6ecd32f027e294c015d9a24fb39d.jpg) 

  

In Rotisserie Draft, you lay out a complete **Magic** set, take turns drafting the cards, and build decks out of what you draft plus basic lands (then give the cards back to whoever owns them). Sound like fun? It is! A lot of work? Yeah, it's that too. You can read about the last Rotisserie Draft I participated in, with *Lorwyn*, [here](/en/articles/feature/2007/11-26-one-delicious-draft-2).

I don't know, though .... Just one set? And six or eight people? We figured we could do better than that. As soon as my friend Dave Guskin hit upon the idea of drafting the entire *Shards of Alara* block, we realized we'd need more than eight people drafting. Once we settled on twelve as a good number, we realized how much better it would be if we drafted in teams instead of just individuals. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/8e/27/8e2774d1e5a81e2714288d9ab6e5e47c1e9e70d84978587cfbe8bcda0da34a04.jpg)

Thus was born the *Shards* / *Conflux* / *Alara Reborn* Full Block Team Rotisserie Spectacular, or SCARFBTRS for short (pronounced "Scarfbutters," which sounds like 1890s prospector slang or something). We agreed on four teams of three, reasoning that this would keep the picks moving pretty quickly and give each person a reasonable number of opponents. Mark Purvis from the Brand team generously agreed to supply the cards.

We decided initially that each team would draft one card from each set during each pass. There was a great deal of concern, however, over the possible unfairness of one team getting the best card in each set while the fourth team couldn't pick until the best nine cards on the table (more or less) were already gone. We arrived at a complicated system to solve this.

Team A, the first team to pick, would get one card from any of the three sets. Team B would get two cards, Team C would get three, and Team D would get four. Team A would get one card from each set plus a bonus card (to even up the numbers a bit), and then the drafting would settle into each team picking one card from each set. Simple, right?

Now, not everybody has access to the Brand team's giant closet o' cards. I well understand this. In bigger groups with larger card pools, you might well be able to pool your resources put together an entire set, or even block, for a Rotisserie. (I recommend sleeving all the cards and writing down who owns what to avoid arguments over damaged or disputed cards.)

For most people, though, today's format is probably out of reach barring extraordinary effort. Still, it was a very good time, and I hope you'll take the following not as mere bragging, but as a guided tour through all the most exciting cards in the block, and twelve different takes on what you might do with them.

### Ready, Set, Plan

Rather than randomize the teams, we just let them shake out. I wound up on a team with Dave and with Gregory Marques. Sadly, the three drafters named Mark (Mark Gottlieb, Mark Globus, and Mark Purvis) did not end up on a team together, although we did try to convince them.

Dave, Greg, and I quickly sat down to make a plan. Partly this is because the thought of staring down 519 cards without a plan in mind made us cringe; partly it's because we, or at least Dave and I, genuinely enjoy overthinking, overplanning, making flowcharts, etc. We wanted to have decks in mind that we'd be aiming for, with certain contingencies depending on what others picked.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/35/26/352652e90b3e026429aa9fecdae1f49106a2825b80dd72ee050efb6b2bc84c17.jpg) 

  

I'll spare you the actual flowcharts, but here was our basic plan. We figured that the first pick from the whole block was probably Martial Coup, with Lavalanche not far behind, as these are the two reliable "Wrath of God" effects in the format. Reasoning that there was only a 25% chance we'd be picking first, we built our Plan A assuming we wouldn't have Martial Coup, with a Plan B on the off-chance that we did pick first or (unthinkably, unconscionably!) the team(s) picking before us disagreed with our assessment and passed on Martial Coup. The main difference between the plans was whether we had a white-based aggro deck and a red-based control deck (Plan A), or a white-based control deck and a red-based aggro deck (Plan B).

### The Big Day

All twelve of us finally gathered on a Thursday night to do the actual drafting. We rolled randomly for the pick order, and my team became Team A, picking first. We promptly snapped up Martial Coup.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/36/8a/368ae95f3618243430b21d6e1d256a7a34f62f568c2b48683dca6bea60aa99ad.jpg) 

  

After that came Team B—Lee Sharpe, Mark Gottlieb, and Matt Tabak. They took Flameblast Dragon and Lavalanche, two cards we had not banked on getting. Team C, made up of former rules manager Paul Barclay and the web team's own Monty Ashley and Tom Jenkot, picked next, with a gorgeous spread that hadn't been on my team's radar: Sarkhan Vol; Elspeth, Knight-Errant; and Nicol Bolas, Planeswalker. Team D—Mark Purvis, Mark Globus, and Alexis Janson—rounded things out by cracking the mana fixing much earlier than my team expected, taking Savage Lands, Fiery Fall, Armillary Sphere, and Rupture Spire.

Then it was back to my team. We were now firmly in the clutches of Plan B. Anxious to grab the red cards we felt were vital to one or both of our red-based decks, we took Caldera Hellion, Slave of Bolas, Bituminous Blast, and Banefire.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/3c/69/3c69567b2f663e9f9ba5cac752cbe61b58c1dbc4113299091b0e6376df85a5fa.jpg) 

  

After that, we all settled in for three and a half total hours of drafting (yes, really!). After the small sets started to thin out while *Shards* was still full of goodies, we instituted a rule by popular vote that a team could opt to take two picks from any of the three sets rather than one pick from each, just to keep the draft moving along.

When we finally wrapped up, it was far too late to stick around and play any games—although of course several people did anyway. The rest of us played our games over the course of the next week.

### The Decks

As I played against people and saw their decks, they reminded me less of Limited decks and more of 40-card Singleton Block Constructed decks. And because of the Singleton nature of the draft, no two decks are the same. Let's take a look, including relevant sideboard cards in some cases.

Monty's deck's chief shtick is the fact that nothing ever, ever, ever stays dead. His graveyard isn't a discard pile; it's more like a waiting room. This pretty much sums up the problem I had when I played him:

Really these two decks are quite different, but I've lumped them together because they're the two decks that play more than three colors. Barclay's deck plays four colors with plenty of fixers and accelerators mostly so he can power out ridiculously powerful cards as early as possible. Whether it's Thraximundar, Blood Tyrant, Vein Drinker, or Apocalypse Hydra won't really matter that much when you're dead. Voices from the Void, Paul's only actual domain card, is a control killer.

Tom and Mark Purvis basically split Bant down the middle, and both of them are capable of some devastating plays. Tom was attacking me for 14 with the 1/3 Ethercaste Knight at one point, thanks to Elspeth, Knight-Errant and Finest Hour (Rafiq of the Many would also have worked), while Mark ran turn-one Noble Hierarch into turn-two Behemoth Sledge and pretty much had his way with me. I beat Mark in three games, but I never had a chance against Tom. Squishing the best of these two decks together seems like a great place to start a Bant deck.

 ![Glory of Warfare](http://gatherer.wizards.com/Handlers/Image.ashx?size=small&type=card&name=Glory%20of%20Warfare&options=) 

  

Gottlieb, surprise surprise, went off the beaten path a bit, to one of the rogue decks my team had identified and ultimately dismissed. The white aggro package is the same as the others in spirit, though of course different in specifics, but here it's backed up by burn and the surprisingly potent Glory of Warfare. This deck was just way too fast for me.

I've saved the best for last. As of this writing, this monster of a deck is 6-0 and counting—and that's without the Caldera Hellion and Volcanic Fallout Dave gave to Greg to shore up his defense against aggro decks.

We identified this deck as a rogue option that we'd go into if we could pick up Banefire, Hellspark Elemental, and Hell's Thunder. We talked about it as a burn deck, but really it's more of an unearth deck. Dave puts the pressure on and keeps it on, with Banefire, hasty creatures, and unearth to finish things off.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/ee/15/ee15ed3eb46b771cfe10cf838331bea7052f2e6035ae2ea060d9696388216b6f.jpg) 

  

One of my favorite plays that I saw him make was against Paul Barclay, who was at a low life total and nervous about Dave's hand of three cards. Paul opted to play Voices from the Void, but one of the cards Dave pitched was Extractor Demon. He unearthed it, smacked Paul for 5, then milled himself when it left play. That netted him Corpse Connoisseur, which he unearthed on his next turn, fetching a Shambling Remains which he could also unearth that turn to attack for the win. Discard against the unearth deck? Not so good.

### So Far, So Good

As I write this, my own record sits at a disappointing 2-4 with three matches left to play, but Dave could very well sweep our nine opponents and carry our team to second place single-handedly. All of these decks could stand to have some gaps filled if one chose to fill them out to 60 cards (although Dave's deck is close), but all of them contain interesting deck-building seeds. With a whole collection to build from, any one of these ideas could make a viable deck.
