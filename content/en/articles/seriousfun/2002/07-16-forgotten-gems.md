As we casual players get used to our shiny new *Judgment* cards, *Odyssey* block continues to reveal opportunities from its earlier expansions. Out of all the email I've received and all the articles I've read, I've seen very little experimentation with the "lower tier" rares from these sets. That's all and well and good for pro players--they're paid to be narrow-minded (and I mean that as a compliment, of course!)--but the rest of us have no excuse.

In particular, there are two cards--one from *Odyssey* and one from *Torment--*that I'm surprised I haven't heard much buzz about. Both fit into styles of decks that appeal to many casual players, and both may, in fact, be improvements on past similar cards.

Now, having said all that, if you read the words "amazing" and/or "powerful" below, you can be fairly certain I'm not entirely serious.

![Burning Sands](https://i.ormos.uk/43/e7/43e7711d998acff102de9750fedf8b9a65cb0a058e4e1649732687ba92be86c0.jpg)

### Gem #1: Burning Sands

Perhaps one of the most despised bad *Odyssey* rares, Burning Sands just feels like it mocks you, doesn't it? I actually heard one laughing at me when I busted open its pack a few weeks ago.

But looking more closely at it, I realized that it isn't just a bad Armageddon. It's an overpowered, undercosted Earthlink and should be treated with all respect due such an amazing (see note above) echo of the so-called **Magic** Golden Age. They don't make cards like they used to, you say? Well, guess again.

Burning Sands is not just a vessel for criticism. It actually can serve as the linchpin in a creatureless--or, as we'll see, near-creatureless--deck that seeks mana advantage by wiping out other players' creatures. Simply add Dingus Staff or burn for a win condition.

It's a fair question to ask what a deck like this would do against *another* creatureless deck. And so I thought of an old favorite of many a casual player: Varchild's War-Riders. Not only does this give creatures to the creatureless player, but it also greatly increases the effectiveness of the central Burning Sands - Wrath of God combo.

*Notes on selected cards:* Like most control decks, this deck is all about stall. It has targeted removal, global removal, and permanent disincentives (Seal of Fire, Powerstone Minefield) to get there. But the path can be awfully slow--attacking with the War-Riders, hoping for a good catch with Dingus Staff, or pinging with the Shivan Gorge. So I went a bit less creatureless and added two Vampiric Dragons, which will give you both beef in the air and a way to shoot more lands--er, I mean, creatures. Argivian Find is simply one of the most underrated and unfairly forgotten cards of our time. Not only would Replenish be just plain overkill, it would smack of the road too well traveled.

*Design alternatives:* Red-white often makes use of cheesy life-gain - global-damage combos to win. This would work fine in a completely creatureless model of the deck. Think Earthquake and Moonlit Wake, for starters. (Yes, they work even better with the Riders in.) Many red ![X Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/b1/6bb1152b0a3305d0a7c2e2eb725b8ebd18620607efc62f6f38b0642b97c8501f.gif) burn spells would work fine, including the common Rolling Thunder if you don't have enough Earthquakes. (Don't forget to take out the black mana; you won't need it anymore.) If you like keeping the creatures, I also toyed with Infinite Authority on the War-Riders. For card drawing, Inheritance is decent in any of the designs.

### Gem #2: Retraced Image

Very few *Torment* rares generate the disdain-tinted stares that Retraced Image does. It seems so out of place--a mediocre combo card with nothing worth combo-ing with across the entire block.

![Retraced Image](https://i.ormos.uk/82/c0/82c0f9323cfdeaef7d6d8888eb93336fa8199acf75a28b7b90461b579a59a335.jpg)

But there's a whole class of white-blue decks that would love to see this card. These decks include one or more of the following cards: Clone (and its variants), Bazaar of Wonders, and Cornered Market. Even Eye of Singularity, which appears to work against Retraced Image, can work in this style of deck if you build your creatures to wreak havoc as they die. (Think Abyssal Gatekeeper, Goblin Marshal, or even Bloodfire Colossus if you do it right.)

Retraced Image is, at its core, a potentially superior but more demanding Show and Tell--a way for you and you alone to get something big for something small. I have no idea what it's doing in blue; but now is not the time for this argument. We'll just add green, the obvious color choice, and work with a two-color deck.

While getting copies of massive creatures (Verdant Force, Polar Kraken) would certainly be fun, I wonder if we shouldn't try to play to Retraced Image's "drawback" and play a deck capable of a fairly early offense and defense. We'll settle most of the curve around the four slot. This will let us drop a Blastoderm on turn four, a Phantom Centaur on turn five, and then copy either creature that same turn; or drop a Wild Mongrel on turn two, an Elephant Guide on turn three--leaving us plenty of room on turns four and five to give ourselves more choices for a Retraced Image. The key here is running four copies of Retraced Image whenever possible.

*Notes on selected cards:* With Retraced Image, Zephid's Embrace can be placed on an otherwise untargetable creature, like Blastoderm or a creature that already has an Embrace. The Palinchrons are there as part of the theme: the ability to play two copies (or really, four) of a card in the same turn. The Iridescent Drake can scoop up any creature enchantments that get blasted away. (*Editor's note:* Be aware that putting an Iridescent Drake into play via Retraced Image will not trigger its comes-into-play ability, thanks to errata issued to curtail infinite Abduction combos. Iridescent Drake only works if you "play it from your hand," just like Hypnox, and Retraced Image does not qualify as "playing from your hand," believe it or not. I don't make the rules.)

There are going to be very few green decks from now on that do not get a great deal better with Genesis. Here, Genesis goes from excellent to superb: How much greater are your late-game chances of having the right creature in your hand if you can pull what you need from your graveyard?

*Design alternatives:* If Genesis and Palinchron are beyond your means, I wouldn't hesitate to try this deck with "call" creatures--Nesting Wurm, Howling Wolf, or even Llanowar Sentinel from *Weatherlight*. This deck is light on utility, so replacing one or more of the creatures with Man-o'-War; Woodripper; or even Aboshan, Cephalid Emperor (who's admittedly not fond of his Retraced Image) may be necessary, depending on what dominates your play group. Of course, every time I put Palinchrons in a deck, at least one reader asks if I've ever heard of the High Tide combo that finishes with Capsize or Stroke of Genius. (So to answer the question, "Yes!")

I know from experience--and the contests I run--that even the "bad rares" aren't really that bad when a casual player takes a look at them. They present opportunities for the bold and creative among us. The reason I get so many emails from professional players who have "rediscovered" casual play and love it so much is that they forgot how wide the universe looks from our planet. The questionable rares are our agricultural heartland; they feed us and sustain us. They define us. Revel in them.

That said, skip Mudhole.
