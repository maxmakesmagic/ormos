There are two kinds of people in this crazy, mixed up world of ours: Those who prefer Rochester, and those who prefer Booster. I know, you’re thinking that most of the planet’s population would have no idea what I’m talking about, but that fact is actually irrelevant in light of the reality that everyone fits into these two categories regardless of whether one knows how to add the Mt to G. The preference is coded in each person’s upbringing. As individuals, we have no say in which side of the line we fall on: we can only fight the good fight once we’ve figured out where we stand.

Germans, in general, are booster people. In North America, if you go to work looking a little ruffled and ask the co-worker how you look, they’ll usually lie to spare your feelings. When Germans go to work looking the same way and ask that co-worker, the response is usually something like “you look like crap”. You look awful and you asked, so I’m going to shoot you straight. I’m German. We’re an efficient and regimented people. That’s what being German is all about. 

See? Booster people. 

Neil Reeves is a Booster person. You can tell by the phrase he’s most often spoken of me:

*“What an idiot.”*

Blunt. Simple. To the point. It’s eloquent in its stupidity. He doesn’t try to explain WHY I’m an idiot or HOW I’m an idiot. He understands that the point of the message is to communicate a simple thought, not to explore the psychological ramifications of that thought, and the best way to do so is to avoid complication… static, as it were. To this end, he doesn’t say “Gary, when compared to a stone you are somewhat clever, but barnyard animals have the one up on you”. Instead, he comes right to the point. He’s a Booster man.

I, of course, am a Rochester enthusiast.

Not a Rochester man, mind you. No Rochester man in good standing would ever *just* say they’re a Rochester man. That would be too… Booster-ish. Instead, I feel the need to fancify my words, eloquent ‘em up, so to speak. The Rochester enthusiast enjoys the skills associated with Rochester draft: politicking, communication at a higher level, complicating things. Straightforward is never the right direction, and every choice, be it in Magic or life, must be approached from a plethora of angles. We’re a tangled bunch. This is our lot.

A couple of weeks ago, at Canadian Nationals on Saturday morning, I was feeling pretty good. I was 5-0-1 with six to go in the Swiss rounds. I was sitting comfortably in third place and about to engage in my favorite format. Not only that, but I was seated in seat four, likely the best place to be in Rochester, sitting right in front of a good friend, Elijah Pollock, who I knew I could expect cooperation from in the form of a mutually beneficial unspoken agreement that hate drafting and color clashes wouldn’t be a factor in this draft. Top 8 was a lock.

Four rounds later, of course, I was out of the tournament, and this week’s Wise Words is about exploring what is likely the primary reason that happened. I made a subtle mistake in that Rochester draft which lead to not only my having a deck weak enough to 0-3 that table, but possibly to the same fate befalling Gab Tsang. Combined, we went 0-6 at table 1 (thanks to his starting the 7th-9th rounds two points ahead of me, Swiss pairings made this possible) due to an error I think a lot of people would have made in my same position, but this fact only makes me love Rochester more, because if you screw up in Booster, it’s never subtle. When you Booster, most of your choices will come down to pure card evaluation. Or a hammer to the cranium. Subtle, Booster is not. 

Let’s set the scene:

So, the first booster is opened by Cormier who makes what I see as a somewhat surprising but by no means atrocious pick with Skirk Commando, leaving Gab to take the pack’s best card, Wirewood Savage, which falls into what, for me, is the least desirable demographic in the *Onsalught*-block Limited formats: green. *Scourge* green is pretty much awful, and as I’ve always been a believer in allowing other drafters to immerse themselves in first pack strength while looking ahead to third pack power with regards to my deck, and as a result, white and black, preferably in combination with blue and red respectively, were my colors of preference. Daoust then made what I considered a pretty poor pick in taking Battering Craghorn third, placing himself two seats behind Cormier’s red deck in a format where, for red players, the first pack is all that matters. Of course, that left me as the beneficiary, being the first player to take either a blue or white card in the form of Mistform Dreamer. My position was set. Elijah, who shares my views on green in the format, elected to take Shepherd of Rot over the obviously better Snarling Undorak, sending some unmistakably clear signals to both Nathon and I. Nathon takes the Undorak, Rider takes Piety Charm before getting Harsh Mercy back tenth, and Jordan, in seat eight, takes Wellwisher and Sage Aven.

Now at this point, I’m thinking that Elijah is obviously black all the way behind me, while Guillaume is planning on pairing red with either white or black because he took the Craghorn over the superior Undorak, suggesting he won’t be going green, and over the better-positioned Dreamer, which is likely better in red-blue anyways, meaning he’s not looking to go blue. Of course, with Elijah being a buddy and our unspoken agreement being in place, my hope was that Guillaume ended up black, leaving the white path clear for me.

Gab opens pack two, finding a weak collection of cards to his disappointment, and selects the best one, Ascending Aven, ending any possibility of Guillaume’s going into blue, and the Frenchman takes Skirk Commando, the best red card in the pack. Faced with a choice of the decidedly mediocre Peer Pressure or taking a card from outside my colors, with Elijah black behind me, I chose to take Sunfire Balm, a solid little trick that will almost definitely make my blue-white deck. Elijah surprises me with his pick, taking another Shepherd over Threaten, as I know he’s a fan of black-red, Threaten’s the better card and I’ve signaled blue-white, but he explained to me after felt it would be more prudent to leave his options open and try to cut off black as effectively as possible. Nathon takes Goblin Taskmaster, a poor pick in light of the Threaten, but it accomplishes the goal, signaling he’ll be a red-green drafter. Josh grabs the Peer Pressure, signaling to Jordan “get out”, but Christensen ignores the message, taking a second Sage Aven, risking a Tranquil Thicket which he eventually gets back when Francis wheels the Threaten (miser) and Fade From Memory, presumably a signal he’ll be going black.

Guillaume opens pack three and has something of a conundrum on his hands: Do I take the strong Shock that fits in with what I’ve drafted thus far, or the all-powerful Mistform Skyreaver, which may lead to fighting with not one, but both my neighbors. He makes the right pick this time, taking the Shock in a format where politics can be just as important as the cards themselves, passing me the 6/6 flyer. If he’d taken the Reaver, I’d have immediately ditched blue, meaning I might have to go black, alienating Elijah and possibly leading to a fight for the goods throughout the remainder of the draft. Not a pleasant thought. Pollock takes Swat, Nathon Barkhide Mauler, Josh Daunting Defender, all status quo, before Jordan takes Severed Legion, signaling inroads to black. Francis takes Goblin Taskmaster before Gab wheels—and this is important—Dive Bomber and Sage Aven, leaving a Tribal Unity that eventually gets back to Jordan.

Now, here’s what’s just happened: Gab, knowing that Christensen got that early Wellwisher and would likely be squeezed out of blue by the Peer Pressure-owning Rider, made a color switch. Despite Wirewood Savage being his best card to this point, Gab knew that he didn’t want to be green in a seat that close to what would inevitably become another green player, and at the same time, with Jordan likely green-black and Francis signaling red-black, he’d have two seats in front of him where neither player would be taking his cards if he ended up blue-white. The signal he’d made the switch came in the form of his taking Sage Aven, a decidedly mediocre card in a color combination with no Lavamancer’s Skills, over Tribal Unity, a solidly playable green card. By the end of pack three, Gab was blue-white. I was so happy to have the Skyreaver I didn’t notice.

This leads to the bad pick of the draft on my part. I opened the fourth booster to find a good card for my ‘blue-white’ deck in Glory Seeker, a crucial card in establishing tempo for a deck that needs to do so, another solid white card in Gustcloak Sentinel, no playable blue cards aside from the late-pick Backslide, and the best card in the pack, Cruel Revival.

I took Glory Seeker.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i> See, when you’ve been on the Pro Tour as long as I have, you come to understand the Rochester Draft never ends. There’s always a next time…</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

To quote the illustrious Teddy KGB, “bad judgment!” Having noticed Gab’s picks but not his signal (more to the point, I hadn’t thought through the fact he’d have to dump green in light of Josh’s Peer Pressure forcing Jordan away from blue.), I stuck to my guns for a couple of reasons. First, blue-white was definitely my color-combination of choice heading into the draft. I knew the colors would likely go underdrafted, that they formed a synergetic archetype with the very recent addition of *Scourge* and hadn’t drafted enough with the set to have learned anything further at the time. (This last situation has since been rectified) Additionally, good ole Elijah was still sitting right behind me with his pile of forced black cards, an “I © Gary Wise” t-shirt and a look of love and adoration for his synergetic “partner” that could have made a puppy vomit, and to take the black card would have been not unlike plunging a dagger into the small of his back, right in that little place that none of us can reach. He’d be cut off in his only color, and would have no choice but to retaliate, if for no other reason than to teach me a lesson whose imprint hopefully wouldn’t fade by the next time we Rochestered together. See, when you’ve been on the Pro Tour as long as I have, you come to understand the Rochester Draft never ends. There’s always a next time, and drastic choices are remembered, meaning that if you screw your neighbor, the memory is long and they’ll get you back some day. I just want to smile along with my brothers, singing kumbaya. Is that so wrong?

“What an idiot.”

Yes, it’s wrong. Politics are important, but not to the point of draft position. Draft position is more important than politics, communication, card evaluation, hymns, chocolate mousse, warm apple pie and the Queen. Well, maybe not warm apple pie (wink wink, nudge nudge). Braymore took Taskmaster over Threaten, yet managed to go 2-1 because his position in the draft ended up being absolutely ridiculous, with no red drafters in the two seats in front of him or behind him, and no green drafters for FIVE seats in front of him. Every seat that doesn’t take your colors is a seat from which you’ll be getting the equivalent of a first pick, because you’re getting the best cards in your colors as long as no one is taking them. That’s math even a Booster enthusiast can appreciate.

If I take the Revival, yes, Elijah’s going to be “displeased”, but look at what happens to my draft: I move into black-blue, getting first crack at black in *Scourge* for the boosters opened in seats two, three and four, while at the same time getting the first blue picks in *Legions* from seats six, five and four. By this logic, my position might be slightly better for black-white instead of black-blue, as with Elijah obviously cutting off the *Legions* black, I want my other color to be strong in that set, but with the Skyreaver in tow, I’m building on a bomb without a real reason to stop doing so. Instead, by taking the Glory Seeker, I ensure that both my colors are being picked by Gab in the remaining twelve boosters that are being passed clockwise at first perusal. That, in case you don’t follow, is a bad thing. If I take the Revival, Elijah is forced to find a second color (as opposed to the monoblack deck he ended up going 3-0 with), and while we’re obviously going to be forced to fight over black, he’s likely to go into white in view of the two open seats in front of him, meaning that there will be times where the strong white Legions cards will eclipse the black ones, which would be left for me.

Heavy stuff, huh?

Unfortunately, hindsight is 20/20 and my foresight wasn’t. My draws in the ensuing rounds were poor, but the deck definitely lacked a little something regardless, hardly surprising when you consider that Gab, Rider and I were all drafting blue-white soldiers, meaning we were not only fighting for colors, but also for a niche of cards within those colors. This is not the road towards successful drafting.
