### CMU

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTNOR01/902.jpg)Mike Turian has been one of the top five Limited players on the planet for almost half a decade now and in case anyone had forgotten it, he went out and won himself a Limited Grand Prix not too long ago. Mike has long gotten the credit for his success and for that of others, but it is starting to look like Mike isn't so much the teacher as part of the core.

This has never been as evident as it was this past weekend in Atlanta. Mike flew down with his CMU teammates Eugene Harvey and Andrew Johnson, but could only watch as his cronies duked it out in the finals for personal claim to team supremacy. In the end, it was Eugene beating Andrew in two quick games, but more importantly as far as team solidarity goes, a member of CMU won.

No team in **Magic** history has undergone the kind of changes CMU has. Starting out four years ago with a roster of Turian, Randy Buehler, Erik Lauer and Dan Silberman, the team eventually took on a number of other top-level players, first adding deck-building wizard Brian Schneider, then another of that ilk in Pat Chapin and finally Turian's cousin Nate Heiss. Then WotC stepped in.

With vacancies in the R&amp;D department, Wizards decided that it was time to start taking the talent from the Pro Tour needed to keep new sets interesting and creative, and Buehler, a lover of many games and holder of a PhD, was the obvious choice. Randy had been the organizational force behind CMU, and when he departed after Pro Tour-London (autumn 1999), the team seemed to fall apart. Lauer, passed over for both the R&amp;D job and that year's Invitational, seemingly went into retirement. Chapin went back to playing with Eric Taylor, Aaron Breider and other Michigan players. Heiss and Silberman both fell off the Pro Tour and Schneider quit tournament play before joining Randy in Renton. Suddenly, Turian was alone.

Fortunately for Mike, the game was still fun and the desire was still there. He kept playing with long time CMU hangers on Elliot Fung and Aaron Forsythe, while Andrew Johnson, in search of an education, decided to attend CMU. Along with Pittsburgh resident Andrew Cuneo, they continued to play, attending PTQs and like, until finally breaking out at U.S. Nationals 2000, where Forsythe, Fung and Turian all made Top 8, with Aaron making the team by beating Mike Long.

Aaron and the rest of the U.S. team (Jon Finkel, Chris Benafel, Frank Hernandez) won World Championships as was expected, with the Pittsburgher putting in a strong personal performance, but the real fireworks happened two months later at the second team Pro Tour where, just like Grand Prix-Atlanta, CMU faced CMU, with Turian's Potato Nation emerging over Car Acrobatic Team. It was around then that the new CMU of Forsythe, Turian, Johnson, Heiss, Silberman, Cuneo and a host of others became a reality.

Since 2000 PT-New York, the team has continued to put up numbers. Turian made Top 8 at Worlds this year, CAT made another team PT Sunday the next year, Harvey finished third at U.S. Nationals and was a major part of the reason the U.S. squeaked out a win at Worlds this year. Forsythe's affiliation with the team recently came to an end when he took a job at Wizards, but that seems like a renewing of the cycle. Who knows what this team will look like in four more years, but you have to think with Harvey, Johnson, Cuneo and Turian's recent success and a constantly replenishing core of players thanks to the university breeding ground, regardless of the faces, the team (not just Turian) will continue to be synonymous with drafting supremacy and strong results.

### *Odyssey* Draft Archetypes

With the completion of two major Grand Prix (speaking of which, congratulations and welcome back to Jeff Fung), it seems that some of *Odyssey* Limited's secrets are finally unfolding. Limited archetypes, while not as pronounced as their Constructed counterparts, do exist, and should each be considered when deciding colors during a draft. Here for your consideration are a few decks you may want to consider drafting next time you head to your local store, have a few friends over to game, or sit down for the Top 8 of a PTQ:

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/66/32/663287fe228543623a2a9f777014e3f75aa46e8e8f1b094d26541f8199d12b6c.jpg"><br><i>Go for the throat</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**Red-green** - This tried and true classic seems to have re-established itself in *Odyssey*. With green's fast creatures and red's mana efficient kill, the pairing seems to be a natural. When you're drafting this archetype, you really want to turn on those cavemen instincts: 'attack hard' is the deck's theme, so control cards like Chainflinger are somewhat devalued, while aggressive cards like Blazing Salvo and Reckless Charge become optimal.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/83/39/83393a469926e65d33ba1ffefc3edc51ca5d02faa8db3e3a34ee4dd3cab4bbde.jpg)**Blue-white** - Where red-green goes, blue-white will follow. Without fail, in any Limited environment where red-green becomes popular, blue-white tags along as a counter measure for those who, for whatever reason haven't gotten their hands on the green-red deck. Blue-white has traditionally been built around controlling the ground and attacking though the air, and this time around is no exception. The one problem is that *Odyssey* seems to be full of cards that eventually break though the defense, so be sure to put a limit on your defense in order to kill an opponent quickly. It's those cards like Mystic Zealot that swing both ways that really make this deck go. Of course, things go much easier if you get five or six, like Eugene Harvey did in the Top 8 in Atlanta.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/a5/32/a53291520cb17a08eb5984d8133ff6688161b4f12219660238e5b3438db863b7.jpg)**Red-black** - Billy Jensen gets the credit on this one. This control archetype seems to be designed around one card, Innocent Blood, that isn't very good in other decks. The idea here is that if you only play seven or eight hard-to-kill creatures and as many creature kill spells as you can find, you'll be able to kill all but the toughest creatures, and that's where the Blood comes in, making a ninth or tenth pick as powerful as a first. The big problem I've been having with this deck is the stopping of green decks with multiple Roar of the Wurms/Beast Attacks/Call of the Herds/Elephant Ambushes which exhaust your creature kill resources then win with the leftovers. Of course, the hope is that you'll get flashback cards of your own like Firebolt or Morbid Hunger with which you'll either regain equilibrium or dominate. Ryan Fuller drafted an aggressive red-black deck in the final draft in Hong Kong and made Top 8 with it, but he got Braids: without the rares, I doubt this deck will be as good as you hope.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/f2/97/f297e95678e39ca17445c494667d43c97ec137c462ab80c815ea384d340e78bf.jpg)**White-green** - I think I'm liking this deck more then most, but that's probably because I've drafted it quite a bit. Fat is all good in most decks, but this one is about getting your creatures down fast and then keeping the momentum going with card advantage based tricks that keep your creatures on the table while theirs go the way of the Dodo. Shelter is the MVP here, maybe even surpassing Hallowed Healer as white's best common (for this style of deck) and Sylvan Might is just as good. You must get fast creatures (Mongrel, Patrol Hound, Mystic Visionary) as establishing a momentum lead is crucial, but keep in mind that doesn't mean resorting to the play of Woodland Druid and the like: keep it solid, but draft the two-drops a little more aggressively.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/c9/9d/c99d921a561ce26f77ab3ecabba6f41845679de6f475227a3e4d20882d195dd1.jpg)**Green-blue** - Seems only natural that the two strongest colors in the set should make for a killer deck, right? This color combo revolves around green's strong threshold creatures and blue's ability to get them there. Werebear is huge here as are creature removal spells like Aether Burst and Repel, and with threshold being so important, Careful Study becomes quite playable. Cephalid Looter is your first pick here over anything short of Beast Attack, Cephalid Broker, Overrun or rares.

**White-red** - White's creature base is easily the second best in the set after green's, so if green isn't available, white can serve as a strong substitute. White won't be able to punch through on the ground quite as effectively, but makes up for it with its flyers, which can also make cards like Magma Vein or Volcanic Spray much more effective. Once again, bears are premium here, with even Mad Dog being vital, and that in turn makes a card like Ember Beast almost crucial. If you draft this combo slow, don't bother playing your matches out, but the aggressive version can hold its own.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/54/1a/541a775e7217cfe47060d45d3f1063de6ad031cec095ee7d30843eeb792a03fa.jpg)**Blue-black** - Black has a lot of powerful cards that suck the graveyard dry or sit there useless. Blue has a lot of cards that get cards to the graveyard as a mere part of their function. Seeing any synergy here? In a color combination full of powerful spells and creature recursion, Scrivener becomes a powerhouse, especially if used with multiple Aether Bursts and/or Gravediggers. Cards like Peek and Careful Study will get you to threshold quickly and allow your black cards like Painbringer, Skeletal Scrying and Zombie Assassin to reach their full potential. One thing to be careful of here: make sure your deck has some early plays, an opening hand of three land and four 4 casting cost spells is usually going to lose.

### Extended vs. Block Party

As of late, there's been a debate going on at [StarCityGames.com](https://web.archive.org/web/20050117205321/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.StarCityGames.com&origin=sideboard_ww_20011204) about the elimination of Extended in favor of Block Party, and I just wanted to weigh in with a few ideas:

*   Extended is great. I spent a couple of months playtesting this format and found myself just loving it. Andrew Cuneo pointed out how fast the games in the format seem to be decided, and in a game with a luck element, that is obviously a negative, but the fact that so many decks were competitive is just a good thing. Sometimes this game isn't about being able to break a format, it's about correctly metagaming for it and/or outplaying the opposition. That's what the guys who made Top 8 at New Orleans did, and they deserve all the credit in the world for it because with 15-20 viable archetypes and 300 of the world's best players to consider, that may take a lot more skill than finding the broken deck.
*   Block Party will get boring. Take it from one who has played with the format, it's fun to go back to those decks of old, but once that novelty wears off, it's going to get boring quick. Bring Your Own Block seems to be the far superior format, not only treading on uncharted waters, but also dealing with hundreds of potentially competitive decks that we have or have not seen before.
*   ![](https://i.ormos.uk/87/ef/87ef975da13e0863d94110c9490f8734dbdc284fda7015291eda23f93527d8fe.jpg)One twist you could throw on Block Party or Bring Your Own Block to make everyone happy is to allow everyone the use of either the basic sets or dual lands. Duals do make it so that color-screw isn't going to be much of an issue, making some archetypes a lot more viable, but more importantly, it should leave everyone contented. There are a lot of people out there who have gone through pain-staking efforts to get their set of forty (I'm not one of them, objective viewpoint), and to render those efforts moot for competitive purposes (Sorry guys, but there won't be any Type 1 PTs or GPs any time soon) seems like an awful shame. To my way of looking at it, those players who have made those investments of either money or time spent trading for the duals are the ones who have really committed themselves to being as good as they can be, and that kind of commitment deserves to be rewarded, not punished. Keeping the duals around keeps the dealers and the card owners happy, while there would definitely be archetypes around that can survive without them.

Basically, I think the idea of getting rid of Extended severs ties with the past that some of us don't want to lose, and would be a mistake as a result. That said though, there's always room for new formats, so maybe is time to add BYOB with duals to the mix.

### Trey Van Cleave

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTNOR01/822.jpg)Just before his disqualification was announced at PT-NO, Trey van Cleave and I sat down and had a talk about his sordid past. I've been pretty hard on Van Cleave, giving him more guff for his indiscretions than most, so I figured I'd give him a chance to fess up. Unfortunately, what I got instead was a series of denials on incidents I know to have happened, leaving me feeling pretty much the same about the Dane from the Ohio Valley.

When Dave Williams was suspended for one year, he stated with no doubts that he was done playing **Magic**, and now that Trey has received the same penalty for his attempted bribe of Mike Sigrist, it will be interesting if we ever see him in competitive **Magic** again. Hopefully the time off will give him time to consider his actions since entering the scene four years ago, and the fact that they taint his accomplishments.

Before I sign off this week, I want to throw some quick congratulations out to the players of the Philippines, who had their strongest performance ever in Hong Kong, placing two of their number in the Top 8, and point you all to [MiseTings.com](https://web.archive.org/web/20050117205321/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.MiseTings.com&origin=sideboard_ww_20011204), a great new site where you can read about my stalker case. Nice to know there still room for change in the M:tG landscape and pecking order. Good job guys. Have a good week.
