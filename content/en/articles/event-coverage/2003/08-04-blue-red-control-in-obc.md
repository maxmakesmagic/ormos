One of these was a red-blue control deck, which offered a wide variety of strategies and resources, some of which I tried out. My first version was:

The idea of this deck is pretty straightforward: use creature elimination to fight off early aggression, then take control with Future Sight and fatties. Against other control decks, there's Annex, which should give me a big land advantage in the early game. Sadly, there are problems with this set of theories, and this design performed poorly. One unexpected peculiarity of this format is that all of the major deck archetypes I played against had extremely robust mana bases.

For example:  
 1) Goblins - doesn't need more than three land to function, especially given the mana-reducing effects of the Goblin Warchief.  
 2) White control (including monowhite control, w-r Slide, w-r no-Slide) - most have multiple cycling effects and at least 3 Exalted Dragons. Some of the more arcane w-b control decks have a full set of Exalted Dragons and Twisted Abominations.  
 3) Zombie - all have a full set of Twisted Abominations. The blue-black version has multiple Read the Runes, which enable searching for lands.

In short, the majority of the decks seem to have both cycling lands and land-cyclers. The combination makes trivial amounts of land-disruption feel useless. So, I decided to try a small increase in land-disrupting effects - Ark of Blight. The two other major possibilities were Misguided Rage or Lay Waste. Misguided Rage is actually quite good against control decks without many non-land permanents, but it is weak against creature-based decks. Rage will always get the less-needed of either their land or creatures. Lay Waste is attractive because of the cycling component, but land destruction is much more disruptive on turn three than on turn four.

I also felt that Form of the Dragon had been underperforming - I was getting burned out a lot. People certainly play it, and claim it does well for them. The logic is that a goblin player needs to be holding three of his four Shocks to kill you. But, because my deck has so few creatures, a goblin deck will often be holding all of his burn in reserve. Very few goblin decks rely solely on Shock for direct damage; there were 3.82 Siege-Gang Commanders present in every Goblin deck which made Day 2 of Grand Prix-Detroit (see [http://www.starcitygames.com/php/news/expandnews.php?Article=5483](https://web.archive.org/web/20030804000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.starcitygames.com/php/news/expandnews.php?Article=5483&origin=sideboard_strategy_20030804a)). Also, zombie decks could use Unholy Grotto to recycle a Gempalm Polluter and do nearly-uncounterable damage. Lightning Rift could also be used to burn me out.

I cut Form of the Dragon, Quicksilver Dragon, 1 Shock and 1 Starstorm for 4 Arks of Blight. The resulting deck felt like a lot of mediocre land destruction decks do: it won a lot of games when going first; lost most games going second and didn't seem to beat the most important deck in the format, goblins. Land destruction decks often have problems dealing with quick, threatening decks filled with small creatures. My land-kill can't start working until my opponent has at least two lands – enough for a goblin deck to kill me.

In over half of my matches, I was bringing in the Rifts and Gempalm Incinerators - both of which are acceptable maindeck cards.

So, although the land kill version had been effective against zombies, and won somewhere around half its games against Exalted Dragon decks (a large improvement over the beatings I had been taking from Rift/Dragon decks previously, due to my inability to slow down Rift), the land-destruction effects showed themselves to be too slow. I decided to swap the Annexes and Arks into the board, and make it into a u-r Rift deck.

This deck has been performing very solidly for me. It has many of the targeted and sweeping removal aspects of other red-based Control decks, but also gains access to some countermagic and a great defensive creature: Wall of Deceit.

**2 Future Sight**  
 One of the key ways to substantiate my advantage in the late game. The card advantage generated by Future Sight is overwhelming against almost any opponent. One of the problems with Future Sight is that land clumps can stop your free draws – this deck can get around them by cycling a card. Classically, card-drawing effects cost a little over four mana to a get a card – Jayemdae Tome or Treasure Trove (more than four because of the initial mana investment). Cantrip cards usually cost 2 mana more than basic version of a card. This is tremendously cheaper than either type of effect.

**2 Discombobulate**  
 Despite the high cost, this is one of the few guaranteed counters in the format. This card is good enough that I've been considering Decree of Silence in the sideboard (although I would then want to run a few Chains of Vapor as well, for the “lock”).

**3 Rorix**  
 One of the more important damage-dealing cards in the deck. If you anticipate a large number of Pacifisms or opposing Rorix in your metagame, this can be switched to 2 Rorix and 1 Arcanis. Arcanis has also performed very well, but Rorix is slightly better.

**3 Starstorm**  
 Another great card against goblins, it is slightly clunky in other matchups. The cycling cost is quite high, and using to kill angels, dragons or beasts is fairly slow.

Carbonize is better than Shock because it is often used against the player. My win ratio is enormous (85% or higher) against people who actually cast Form of the Dragon while playing me.

This version has been doing quite well. I have had a winning record Against goblins, zombies and w-r control. However, part of that should be ascribed to the (currently) surprising nature of this deck.

This deck feels more more like a classic control deck than a w-r Rift-based deck. It has counterspells instead of Wrath effects, and Wall of Deceit instead the more aggressive Silver Knight. The land base is a bit more balanced, not wanting two of one color for a turn two play like Silver Knight. For card advantage, Future Sight has been excellent both at shutting the door on aggressive decks, and giving me a lasting advantage against other control decks.
