Fresh off of a Top 32 at Pro Tour-Nice, but arriving less than triumphantly from Grand Prix-Milwaukee, a despondent Zev Gurwitz slumped onto his bed and started trying to make the modifications for his potential U.S. Nationals deck. Despite having three byes, despite playing - let's be honest, himself developing - the "best deck", Zev failed to make Day 2 of GP-Milwaukee. He looked up to his roommate, noted *Sideboard* writer Toby Wachter, and said:

> *"It's just not good in Type II any more."*  
>  -Zev Gurwitz

But once upon a time, not that long ago, ZevAtog was quite good, even format-defining, maybe "best". What made this deck unique?

Coming out of the San Diego Masters finals, there were basically two schools of Psychatog decks that rose from the ranks of many independently developed styles. Ryan Fuller's deck was full of redundant bounce spells, playing both Aether Burst and Repulse, while on the opposite side of the table, David Humpherys ran multiple creatures and Upheaval.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/1a/a0/1aa06258532d7f6f71a996189d6adac7143fefba8ede14d9122c0935fb3cb849.jpg"><br><i>Once thought to be the death of Psychatog - players have adapted.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

With the inclusion of *Torment*, the Psychatog strategy was at one point called "invalidated" by well known **Magic** writer Seth Burn, due to the printing of Chainer's Edict, yet *Torment* also offered a natural compliment to the card Psychatog, itself, in Circular Logic. Zev Gurwitz's deck sought to combat Chainer's Edict by playing additional creatures, and Upheaval to seal the game before the opponent could muster the necessary ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif) (both echoing Humpherys). Like Fuller, he controlled the early game with even more Repulses, while his personal addition to the deck was Standstill.

It took a while for even the New York-based players to figure out how to run this new deck properly - especially against Brian Kibler's emerging school of Braids - but once they realized that they could play Standstill into a beatdown deck from an on-the-face bad board position, it was the beatdown players who had to figure out that they had to break the Standstill. Once New York became educated to the power of ZevAtog, it took tournament after tournament, winning Grudge Match qualifiers and other local purses consistently. As ZevAtog's legend grew, it spread across the country, qualifying players for the U.S. Nationals, and going so far as to totally dominate the Top 8s of some Regional tournaments.

For his own part, Gurwitz won a GP-Milwaukee trial, winning three valuable byes, and took his signature deck to a rare Standard Grand Prix.

The problems ZevAtog has with Counter-Trenches are many. First of all, ZevAtog has too many dead draws, with the possibility of picking up both Repulse and Aether Burst with no reasonable targets in game 1. Secondly, it cannot match Counter-Trenches's permission. ZevAtog almost always has to resolve Upheaval to win the game... and Counter-Trenches beats ZevAtog's 8 permission spells with 14 of its own - almost twice as many ways to win a counter war. Third, and most importantly, ZevAtog is outclassed in the card-drawing department. While both decks have Fact or Fiction, ZevAtog relies heavily on Standstill as well. Counter-Trenches can simply wait for ZevAtog to get to 7 cards in hand, and then break Standstill with Prophetic Bolt, forcing ZevAtog to discard before passing the turn.

These problems are complicated by the fact that Counter-Trenches can bring in as many as 11 creatures after sideboarding... but really doesn't have to if it doesn't want to. The ZevAtog player has to play a guessing game as to whether his bounce spells stay bad in game two, or if he will simply lose to Meddling Mage and Lightning Angel beatdown if he takes them out.

As Counter-Trenches has dramatically increased in popularity over the past few weeks, and with edt's recent win in Milwaukee, these are important questions for the ZevAtog player to consider... if ZevAtog is even around to consider them by Nationals.

While this quote is a blatant exaggeration, Pat's deck nevertheless went undefeated in games against Psychatog variants at GP-Milwaukee. Much as Counter-Trenches has jumped in popularity, Squirrel Nest/Opposition came out in force at GP-Milwaukee, catapulting both Chapin and former teammate Mike Turian into the Top 8, with variants chosen by a number of players. Chapin's version of Squirrel Prison, specifically, gives ZevAtog serious problems. The card Squirrel Nest by itself is enough to force an Upheaval, and while ZevAtog's heavy bounce element is not totally useless, because Squirrel Prison doesn't actually need its creature cards to win, their impact is much reduced. The presence of seven permission spells (ten after sideboarding) allows Squirrel Prison to match ZevAtog in most counter wars, and will often trump them because of its superior card advantage (Compulsion, Merfolk Looter, and Squirrel Nest in addition to Fact or Fiction). If all the Squirrel Prison has to do is stop 2-3 Upheavals, that should not be a big problem with Pat's build, while ZevAtog will have to spend its own precious instants on Opposition and other game-breaking permanents.

> *"The enemy's gate is down."*  
>  -Ender Wiggen, then Julian "Bean" Delphiki, then Brian Kibler

So the metagame has changed. Decks that ignore ZevAtog's heavy bounce element have risen to the top tier, and decks with permission enough to stop Upheaval join them. Stalling behind Standstill and bounce to a win in a single turn with Wrath/Geddon/Hatred has become and a lot less likely, and at least more difficult.

But does this mean the Psychatog strategy is altogether dead? Of course not. In Milwaukee, a little fewer than 17% of the players (107 of 651) ran Psychatog variants, but quality rose to the top, and in the playoffs, three of the Top 8 players summoned Atogs. Meanwhile, in Germany, Kai Budde took home his National Championship with another non-ZevAtog Psychatog strategy. What it does mean, though, is that ZevAtog, as a specific build of deck, is probably no longer the best mainstream deck in Standard, and that the two-strategy split of Tog v. anti-Tog may be over on Sunday afternoons at Neutral Ground.

I leave you with a modified build that took everyone's favorite DragonMaster to the Top 8 of Milwaukee. This version does not rely so much on Standsill card advantage, and meets an aggressive deck's early game with Memory Lapse for ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) rather than Aether Burst (which, as we've said, is a lot weaker against the new breed of top decks). Harkening back to the Godzilla builds from San Diego, Kibler's version runs Probe and Cephalid Coliseum in place of Standstill card advantage, and borrows the 4 Opt/2 Upheaval configuration from Matt Rubin and the rest of a successful group of Florida Regionals players.

If there is any specific disadvantage to Kibler's configuration, it is that a wise player with either Duress or permission will sometimes be able to make really unfavorable Fact or Fiction splits, hoping to keep them from choosing the pile with one of only two copies of Upheaval. Nevertheless, the presence of Probe may allow the Kibler build to cripple a different one of the opponent's resources, allowing for an uncontested Psychatog hammer blow, even if the opponent has permanents in play.
