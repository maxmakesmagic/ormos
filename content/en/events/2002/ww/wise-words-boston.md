Ladies and Gentlemen: welcome to the topsy-turvy insanity that is my world. Over the last week, my life has been turned upside down often enough that I can't remember which side was up in the first place. I went 4-7 in a Pro Tour I finished third in, found myself winning the first match of my career I couldn't say I was happy to win, and with the knowledge I'd be leaving England in mid- to late-October, found myself being returned to Boston upon my arrival in London. There'll be a lot more to come on those circumstances and my time in England in next week's column, but for the moment, know I'm writing from Toronto, my fourth locale in three weeks, my eyes would be closed and my mind unconscious if not for the adrenaline rush created by not knowing what's next, and yes, for this liberally minded follow-the-rules kid with the upper-mid upbringing, it's kind of cool being booted from a country, at least from the writer's 'experience the world and life' point of view.

Boston was a bit of a whirlwind for me, with emotional extremities likely blinding me from a few of the stories of the weekend, but I think I've pretty much caught up on what's what. 

### PHEONIX FOUNDATION

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptbos02/727.jpg)

17-0 lifetime in Team Rochester at Pro level events. Is there any doubt now that this is the greatest team of all time? Marco Blume has never cracked a Top 8 as an individual, but he's tied for third all time in PT wins, trailing only his Foundation teammates while tied with luminaries like Jon Finkel and Tommi Hovi.

The real question posed by the Foundation's remarkable run is whether this is the proof that Team Rochester is the most skill testing of all sanctioned formats. Yes, there has to be some luck involved in any streak as remarkable as this one, but think about it: Everyone agrees this is the best team in the world. They tell anyone who'll listen that they're going to keep winning and they show every time that they're more than just talk, and in doing so, they create the most responsible result one can find in any competitive endeavor: the better team wins. How can you argue with that? Sure, Kai wins more than his fair share in other formats: he's the best player and that's going to happen. But even the Juggernaut has his bad tournaments, regardless of whether he'd running the best deck, and that's got to be blamed in part on the luck factor of **Magic**.

### The Best Format Ever?

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptbos02/755.jpg)

The Germans and their 17-0 record have found the proof that in Team Rochester, where not only are there so many opportunities to outdraft your opponent, but outplay them as well (if you know every card in the opposing deck, you know what to play around), the fact that mana screw can be beaten by your teammates covering your back makes this the ultimate skill-based format in a game fighting for respectability while its own legions continually bemoan the luck of the game. Right?

Not necessarily. As you look down the standings, you'll see that there were a number of mercenary teams of pros that did poorly. Courtney's Boys seems to be the exception to that rule, but we differed from the norm by getting together for a week and a half before a PT, those flights apparently a good investment, and even then we only managed a generous 3-1-1 in the draft portion (20/20 were about to beat us when they offered the draw that clinched their Top 4 spot). 

Instead of the regular names you're accustomed to seeing that high up, there were the teams that worked hardest on the format. Each year, it seems the teams of New York/Jersey guys who make half the Pro Tours do really well. Don't get me wrong: Scott McCord and Gerard Fabiano (no offense to Jon Sonne, but I didn't really know him from Stalin before this weekend) are good players, but it was their teamwork that got them to Sunday. They knew the format better than higher profile opponents, and while I definitely see Top 8's in Fabiano's future and Scott's game face is as fierce as any in the game, this was definitely the easiest arena for them to find success in, because in a format where teamwork is so important, this team actually worked. Many of their foes didn't.

Anyone notice that the same teams keep doing well? Not right at the top well, mind you, but well none the less. Sure the Foundation keeps coming in first, but Slay-Pillage Gerrard used to be Slay-Pillage-Massacre, with Eric Zeigler playing the role of Gerard Fabiano, and they were in the Masters half a year ago. Www.starmaker.nl/lap, while getting the credit in coverage for being 'pretty good', were the number two-ranked team in the world coming in, and they finished fifth. Hobby One, in sixth place, was called Black Ops once upon a time and they've had a couple of top ten PT finishes prior to this year. Stalking Tiger, Hiddon Gibbons, who last year fell a final-round win short of Top 4 once again placed in the Top 16 after leading the pack in round 9. 11th place Team Outland 2002 is two-thirds of the Outland that finished fifth in 2000, and Plan 9 from Outland, working with them, came 16th. Folks, the point is this: whether his is the most skill intensive format in the game is still up for debate, but this is DEFINITELY the format where playtesting and hard work will pay off the most. Blood, sweat and tears: that's what wins in Teams Rochester.

### J.T. Money

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptbos02/987.jpg"><br><i>Reeves, Maher, Wise</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

Last week, I wrote about how a number of players had told me Neil Reeves is the best Limited player in the States, and after having watched him all weekend, I'm pretty much convinced. How's this for dominance? He starts the weekend playing with Bob Maher and Nick Little in the last chance qualifier, with their sole purpose in the tournament being to block for Dave Williams's team. Neil played and won all five of his matches. Then, he entered the PT with Bob and I where he went 6-0 on Day 1 and 4-0 to start day 2. 15-0 runs do some pretty sick things to your Limited rating.

If Neil weren't the character he is, though, this wouldn't be all that fascinating. Coming from outback Arkansas, Neil grew up a farmer's kid, and plays the redneck to a tee, with a cache of pranks he loves running time and again while speaking with his good ole boy southern drawl. Lumbering around at a very large 6'3", toothpick in mouth and dirty cap always adorned, it would be easy to judge this book by the cover and assume there may not actually be an IQ lingering within. 

Sorry Money, the jig is up. Well read, outspoken and always on top of his argumentative game, Reeves wears his down south costume well, using it to outthink his opponents. Oh sure, you might think this will serve as fair warning, but I sincerely doubt it. Talk to him and you'll get my meaning.

Rules of dealing with Reeves:

*   If he goes to high five you, don't fall for it
*   Take a defensive posture. He's always attacking.
*   If he calls you 'Frenchie', it means you aren't from the States, Mexico or Canada
*   Never, EVER mention Farid Meraghni in his presence.
*   Don't ever stand between him and a draft. The man actually complained that we were playing on Sunday because it meant he couldn't game as late on Saturday night.
*   Don't ever expect him to trust you to carry him. Our final round win over www.starmaker.nl/lap was the first of his career where he'd lost and his team won. Ever.
*   As a result, don't expect much faith from him. After he'd lost to Victor Van den Broek in that match, with Bob and I still playing, he went back to the hotel room and got some sleeves for a draft. Needless to say, when he found out we'd each won, he wasn't pleased with the fact the sleeves would be useless.

While this paints a rather comical picture, there are two things about Neil you have to take seriously: First, never, EVER screw around with his hat. He'll get the shotgun. Second, if you sit across from him, be very afraid. In Limited, this may be the next best thing to Kai himself.

### 20/20

Who???

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptbos02/728.jpg)

Okay, okay, we all know their names, but really, this may be the story of the tournament. 20/20, a.k.a. Elijah Pollock, Dave Rood and Steve Wolfman, came out of nowhere to finish second in Boston, toppling what I think is a pretty solid team in the semis, much the same way they did in the Swiss until they offered us a draw while sitting on victory's doorstep with the realization the draw put them into Sunday.

Both times we played, Steve Wolfman handed me my rear. The semifinal result feels like a bit of an aberration, but the Swiss beating was followed by a number of fun game beatings. In fact, Wolfman beat me in last year's tournament too as Potato Nation put up a less than spectacular showing. Yes, this man owns me. Funny thing… I've been getting paired up against Steve Wolfman for years.

Steve Wolfman is that kid. You know the one, every community has one. He's a little awkward, has a brain for the game, and could probably kick most PTQ-type posterior if not for self-confidence issues. Wolfman had been to a handful of PTs but never finished higher than the mid-forties, and while he'd had a couple of near misses at Nationals, as he made his way to University, it started looking like he'd never find the brass ring he so desperately wanted.

Now, everything changes for Mr. Wolfman (My PT record against him forces me to refer to him thusly). The dream that was Boston will bring him the status and respect he's likely wanted, not to mention some degree of Pro Tour security, with a mere appearance in Houston guaranteeing him a gravy train slot until the end of next year's Team PT. I can't help but think that the money paying for the remainder of his school days will be pretty good, and if that's not enough, Tokyo beware: he'll be playing the Masters there too. Now that he's got this result under his belt, the confidence should no longer be an issue. I can't wait to see how it affects him.

Those who know this team would likely tell you that Steve, who went 11-1 on the weekend, was the weak link here, but Rood and Pollack are better than you might think. Elijah, coming out of Toronto, has been playing on par with the likes of Gab Tsang for years, with the only thing standing between him and PT success being an intense frustration with the game's luck factor, or more precisely, the idea of paying airfare and hotel only to go face to face with that luck factor. A math whiz, Pollock has been dominating the Toronto PTQ scene for years, and half a decade ago was one of the top juniors on the planet, making two Junior Pro Tour Top 8s before that circuit was disbanded in favor of the JSS. Now he's back on the Tour and on the gravy train for the first time. It'll be interesting to see if he decides to pursue it now that he has $10,000 in his pocket and more to come.

Last but not least of the trio is Rood, the 18 year old French Canadian who met his teammates while journeying to Toronto in the hopes of stealing some Anglophone PT slots. If you've been watching the results coming in from Grand Prix over the last year, you won't be as surprised to see his name in the lights. Top 8 in Montreal, 11th in Cleveland, 18th in Las Vegas… GP New Jersey is the only event he didn't make money in this year, and he carries with him a swagger reminiscent of Mike Long in his heyday. You may not know who these guys are, but they definitely know how to attack for two.

The point? This year, these guys equated to Cinderella. A year ago it was Les Plus Class, and now Gabriel Nassif and Amiel Tenenbaum are widely considered among the best in the game. That could be 20/20 a year from now, and if that happens, next year's Cinderella could be some random kids from Malaysia or three old women from Uruguay or, hey, why not, it could even be you. If the numbers prove anything, this is the format where it can happen. It's just up to you to know it better than anyone else. Try practicing while you dream of the stars.

Have a good week.
