Trying to qualify? If you are interested in playing a difficult strategy that is far more powerful than the decks other people are playing at PTQs, you can have an enormous advantage over all of your opponents. The trick is to utilize the most broken cards in the format. Before going any further, let me just slip in a little disclaimer:

*"Using the most powerful cards or using a truly broken strategy does not assure one of victory. This is especially true when a large number of very important choices are demanded of a player during each match. It is not recommended that one pick up and run this deck cold."* 

That being said, OBC has an absurdly powerful, near unbeatable (it is actually beatable, but not greater than 49% of the time by anything else in the format) strategy not currently being used.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>If a degenerate deck exists, and no one plays it, is the format broken?</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

It would appear to an outside viewer that OBC is but a collection of blue-green and monoblack decks, with the occasional w-g or w-u deck (and I suppose b-u) for the most part. This is true.... for the most part I will say one thing about this format, and that is that it has prompted me to ask a bit of a philosophical question. "If a degenerate deck exists, and no one plays it, is the format broken?"

The situation that exists in OBC right now reminds me, a great deal, of the way Type 1 often becomes, with broken decks existing and not enough people playing them to change anything quickly. If you can win turn 1 or turn 2 with Force of Will backup, but almost no one else is doing what you are doing, is the format unplayable? The answer I have come to is to play the strategy that you know to be broken over and over again until people realize something needs to be done about it. For the time being, not enough people have been exposed to, or have been playing the OBC version of this to bring about a demand for change. This means, of course, that you have an opportunity to be one of those people that plays Academy before people knew it was too good (which, in that case, happened to be about three minutes). It is not easy to play, by any means, but when well piloted, favorable against the archetypes people have been playing at the PTQs. Some matchups are 60% win and are some 90% win, but all appear to be favorable. Now, I realize that I am risking sounding like one of those guys that thinks a deck beats everything and holds an unrealistic view of the format, or of how games will go, but I am willing to risk that. I feel very strongly on this subject.

It is important to note at this point that, at the time I'm writing this, Zombie Infestation-Upheaval decks and monored decks are not an actual part of the metagame. If they become more prominent, this changes a few things, as both of these decks can give the following deck a hard time. Black-red land destruction could also pose a problem, I suppose, but u-g, Braids, monoblack control, g-w, and Solitary Confinement are not problems. The deck in question is Burning Wish/Mirari's Wake/Time Stretch. Academy decks, Raven-Aluren decks, and a number of other powerful decks evolved to the next level by making the sacrifice of splashing another color for the best tutor effect available. When you have a card as broken as Mirari's Wake, it is worth considering adjusting the mana to make the best use of the cards that work with it. In this case, I believe that the issue of the fourth color injuring your mana base can be overcome by playing just as much blue, green, and white mana as you would if you didn't play the red, and cutting business cards for the mountains. The red adds so much that it is worth the sacrifice. The theory is that if you play Wake and untap, you win, so you just need to make sure that you are able to put yourself in that position. I believe that having access to Kirtar's Wrath and Quiet Speculation for Moment's Peace is the best way to not die. The red allows you to play with a much larger count of Wraths and Quiet Speculations than anyone else, as the Burning Wishes can be either of these as well as Stretches or your win condition, not to mention other options like Cleansing Meditation and Firebolt (the ultimate Braids answer). I wish I could play more Krosan Verges. It is mana acceleration, uncounterable card drawing, color fixing - everything you want. Burning Wish fetches Time Stretch in general. It only takes 6 lands to win with Firecat Blitz plus Mirari's Wake. Six lands makes twelve mana, which makes ten 2/2 Firecats, which is 20 damage. You can Wish for Deep Analysis after flashing it back.

The sideboard works as follows. Board in one Firebolt to Quiet Speculation into your graveyard, leave one to Wish for against Braids. Bearscape comes in against all black decks, Envelop versus control black decks. Spirit tokens are also a fine way to win against creature-based decks.

The maindeck might need slightly more blue mana and possibly slightly less white mana. I'll leave this to you in your testing.

I tried Mirari, but found if I could Mirari something, I would have been able to win without Mirari. The same was true for Nostalgic Dreams.

The idea is to get Mirari's Wake in play and then untap. No one ever really loses when this happens. Not real people, in real life. Between Quiet Speculation for Moment's Peace, maindeck Kirtar's Wrath and Burning Wish for another Wrath or Speculation, there are plenty of ways to shut down creature beats, Speculation for Analysis/Insight, Compulsion, and such helps get whatever is needed. Logic is to counter opposing Envelops, other Logics, Mind Sludge and other problem cards. Remember, you don't need to go infinite with the Stretches - you just need to untap with Wake in play.

The reason that the deck has such an apparent surplus of land is that this whole Wake and win strategy is so powerful that mana screw is the most likely stumbling block (as one might expect from a 4-color deck). It is worth sacrificing some power for added mana consistency. Believe me; this deck has power to spare.
