I’m quite fond of <i>Arabian Nights</i>. From the recent sets, I’d say <i>Urza’s Saga</i> had the most impact on every Constructed format and <i>Apocalypse</i> had the opposed-color painlands and some very neat gold cards.</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;                 <b>January 28, 2003</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/d5/77/d577174465a6216c4498875eafb3da2b3211ea99f3f114f05b0965ac4275f693.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<p><b>Question</b>: What do you think is the biggest mistake a player can make when deckbuilding?</p>&#10;<p><b>Answers:</b></p>&#10;<p><b>Nicholas Labarre</b>: Not sticking to the 60 cards rule. It's definitely tempting to add this last tutor target, and I even played against a 62-cart Rector deck at PT Houston, but I can't think of a worse idea in constructed. <br><b>Kai Budde</b>: Hmm, tough question. The one that is most common by far is not playing enough lands though.<br><b>Eric Taylor</b>: Too much ego invested into a deck you have created.<br><b>Eric Froehlich</b>: Not spending enough time on the sideboard is the number one mistake, especially since you will be playing more games sideboarded than not. Other than that, having bad mana can lead to some unfortunate draws and quite a bit of whining for the weekend.<br><b>Olivier Ruel</b>: Some players try to play their best rare at any cost. Some others never play three colors in limited. And I really think that is the worst, not being open minded.</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;                 <b>January 27, 2003</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/24/ed/24ed5be5578a04d4bd33e38ab1834ea7e497fbcee8f490f096ac18a1ccfd9f54.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<p><b>Question</b>: Where do you get the names for decks like PT Junk, Trix, and The Rock?</p>&#10;<p><b>Answers:</b></p>&#10;<p><b>Zvi Mowshowitz</b>: PT Junk is called PT Junk because its designer believed in truth in advertising. Trix got its name because Extended combo decks always have to be named after breakfast cereals and any deck that plays with <a>Illusions of Grandeur</a> is definitely for kids. The Rock got its name from because everyone knows it loses to paper. At one time, I did my best to give my decks funky names, often with multiple meanings. Zero Effect, for example, came from the movie and because the deck’s plan is to make cycling cost zero, play free spells, eventually go through cycles for zero and then leave you with zero cards in your library. But lately, deck names are as uncreative as possible.<br><b>Victor Van Den Broek</b>: The name for Angry Hermit Part II was thought of by Frank Karsten and Noah Boeken. Frank played Angry Hermit Part I in every format he could, so Noah mentioned this deck also had a Hermit and it was very angry.<br><b>Kai Budde</b>: Most names are rather random in my opinion, but I use them anyway because people recognize them. <br><b>Dirk Baberowski</b>: I'm European. We like to put our efforts into playing rather than naming our decks.<br><b>Brian Kibler</b>: Deck names are an interesting phenomenon. Often, decks are named by their<br> creators (like my Red Zone deck, which I named after the Feature Match playmats), but sometimes they're simply named as a fluke, most famously the Sligh deck, which got its name because Paul Sligh was the first player to post a high finish with the deck at a Pro Tour Qualifier, despite the fact that the deck was designed by Jay Schneider. </p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

  

<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;                 <b>January 24, 2003</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/f0/9f/f09f4d2a172231ed02bcfadb60e0264389ebd0a5d2bbaaf40ad6a927154a8f0e.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<p><b>Question</b>: How do you determine which decks are rogue and which aren’t?</p>&#10;<p><b>Answers:</b></p>&#10;<p><b>Zvi Mowshowitz</b>: Look around the room. If no one else is playing it, then it’s rogue.<br><b>Eric Taylor</b>: A statistical analysis of the metagame.<br><b>Kai Budde</b>: I actually don't really care. Any good rogue deck is a net deck a week later. All this talk that “I played a rogue deck today” seems rather pointless to me in the age of the internet.<br><b>Gary Wise</b>: If Bram Snepvangers plays it, it’s rogue.<br><b>Adrian Sullivan</b>: There is a lot of misconception about what &quot;rogue&quot; means. Rogue isn't &quot;wacky&quot; or &quot;strange&quot;. Rogue is simply far outside the metagame. For example, a red burn deck (not Red Deck Wins) is clearly rogue. It isn't an expected archetype and it's not one that you're likely to have playtested against. At the same time, playing against it is likely to be very different than playing against other red decks. A blue-green Oath deck is not rogue because it plays quite similarly to another deck (Gary Oath).</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

The best topdeck was drawing my fourth <a>Illusions of Grandeur</a> (three were gone thanks to <a>Crosis, the Purger</a>) and a <a>Donate</a> on the next turn, while Darwin Kaslte was killing me with his Dragon.
