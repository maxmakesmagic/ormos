I awoke early before Day 2, well rested and ready to game. My nerves were somewhat on edge, seeing as I was in first place in the standings. Being undefeated after Day 1 of an event is a double-edged sword – on one hand, you're in great shape for the following day, needing only a few wins to secure a place in the elimination rounds. On the other hand, however, that simple fact means that if you don't make Top 8, you'll never hear the end of it. Sure, if you're 5-1 and you miss, that's perfectly reasonable – no one recounts the tales of the 5-1's who didn't make it in years past. What we hear about are the extremes, both the falls from grace and the remarkable runs – Kamiel Cornelissen's palindrome 7-0, 0-7 performance in San Diego, Sol Malka and Mike Turian both missing the brass ring from undefeated records in 2001, or Ryan Fuller's 14-0 in Toyko and Mike Long's 1998 undefeated streak before a Cadaverous Bloom found its way into his lap. If my performance was to be remembered, I did not want it to be for a fall.

My second draft table was not much more impressive by way of names than my first, despite it being the vaunted table one. Mike McGee and James Cross joined me as the other 6-0s, along with Dave Humpherys at 5-0-1, with Adam Horvath, Phil Freneau, James Chung, and Paul Rietzl filling out the 5-1's. I found myself seated between Freneau and McGee, with Humphreys across the table. This would be important, as Dave and Paul and I had discussed our preferences before the draft, and it became quite clear that Dave and I wanted to draft very similar decks, with some combination of white, black, and blue being the goal. My bias toward these colors and particularly away from red is quite extreme, but I believe it is warranted. I've gone so far as to pick Glory Seeker over Sparksmith, and I regularly take the 2/2 over Shock. This is especially important in Rochester Draft, as color positioning is both crucial and often overvalued by other players who are afraid to fight for a color. I'll fight for white or black any day, so long as the overall number of players drafting the color are within reason, and I'm more than willing to draft blue with another blue drafter to my right, since *Legions* is where you really want to be blue anyway.

In any case, this draft is also [covered by the Sideboard](/en/events/2003/natus03/draft-2-brian-kibler-paul-rietzl-and-david-humpherys). Freneau opened up with Infest in seat one, so I took a white card in seat two. When I opened Gluttonous Zombie and nothing in white, I decided that I was willing to brawl with Phil if need be, especially since I prefer my white decks to be nearly monowhite with a splash for removal. Things solidified color-wise pretty quickly, with the only really frustrating point in the draft coming when Rietzl took an Aven Brigadier that clearly would've ended up in my deck. Paul defended his choice by saying it wasn't clear what his second color was yet, but his pick both deprived me of a bomb and Humpherys of a pick from the pack entirely, when he would have otherwise gotten an incredible wheel. When Paul opened Eternal Dragon in the last pack of the draft, he just looked at me and grinned, pretending he was going to take it, and then sent it along. My deck ended up very powerful, with a solid creature base backed up by bombs in the form of Decree of Pain and Eternal Dragon. I felt that I could quite readily run the table.

**Round 7 – Mike McGee**

McGee was the talk of the tournament at the end of day one as the JSS kid who'd sworn to drop at 5-1 or worse and then posted a 6-0 record. I hadn't seen him play prior to this round, so I didn't know what to expect going in other than that he'd drafted quite well and that he's a constant Magic Online player, so chances were he'd be no pushover. His deck was u-r, once the top dog of *Odyssey* block draft, now merely passable due to the overall weakening of red in the latter two sets and the loss of two packs of Lavamancer's Skills and Sparksmiths, the cards that truly made the archetype one to fear. As it stands, u-r now ends up generally being a collection of mediocre cards that work pretty well together, but not exceptionally so. Without the ability to take much advantage of tribal effects or the best creatures on any front – white has the best fliers and green has the best ground-pounders – the color combination is just not nearly the powerhouse it once was.

Game 1 was a clear example of how underpowered u-r has become, as I dropped a turn four 4/4 Daru Stinger and McGee had to run his Zombie Cutthroat into it before pinging it off the board with one of red's copious unexciting one damage removal spells. I had backup and it was all better than what he could bring to bear and soon enough the game was mine. Apparently he was holding Blatant Thievery at the time he killed my Stinger, and his friends behind him claimed that if he'd just held off on killing it and instead taken control of it he would have won the game, but getting bashed in the face for three turns by a 4/4 while I play other creatures hardly seems like a particularly effective strategy to me. Game 2 went similarly, as he played underpowered creatures and I played good ones. At one point I make a read on a morph as Zombie Cutthroat, so rather than cycling Decree of Pain to try to clear his board, I hold back and take some damage to play out creatures of my own for a couple of turns, and when he flips another morph to come over top with Mistform Seaswift, I'm able to block his Cutthroat and cycle to kill his entire team after damage when he flips it, leaving me with a 3/3 flier in play and more to come from my hand. Between the card advantage, board advantage, and life advantage the play gives me, I'm able to overwhelm McGee before he can mount an effective defense.

I kept talking to Mike throughout the rest of the day, and he seemed like a genuinely nice kid, which is good to see in a player of his age. Many top JSS players seem to be really arrogant and abrasive, which can be expected, I suppose. When you're fifteen years old and the best among those you regularly play with, it's easy to get cocky, but I think such off-putting attitudes – especially among younger players – can't be anything but bad for the game. Good natured competitive spirit is one thing, but elitist behavior is another altogether.

On a somewhat related point, Mike's father asked me after our match if I felt that his son's decision to stay in Nationals rather than play in the JSS had been unwise, and I didn't really have a good answer for him. It's easy to say from outside the situation that he should've dropped and gone for the easier payoff in the JSS, but regardless of how much harder the competition might be in Nationals, it's hard to bring yourself to drop when you're 6-0. If I were to make the decision for him, I'd say to drop, but if it were me in the same situation, I'm positive I'd want to stay in. If it were all about the money, I don't think any of us would be playing Magic – anyone who's intelligent enough to be a successful pro Magic player could certainly make more money in another field. It's hard to find the glory of a National Championship or the thrill of victory when you're an accountant, though.

Matches 7-0 Games 14-3 

**Round 8 – Dave Humpherys – Feature Match**

I knew going into the round that I'd be playing Dave, as he'd dispatched James Cross, the other undefeated player, in round seven. Since we were teammates and working with the same scouting information, we agreed to just show each other our decks before the round. I imagine Dave probably gleaned more benefit from this than I did, as my memory seems to be considerably better, but I was able to learn a few important things like exactly how many cyclers he had, which gave me a better idea of just how dangerous his Stoic Champion was. Interesting enough, I found that Dave knowing that I only had three morphs and exactly what they were could potentially be used to my advantage, as it could give my bluffs more weight when the probability of an individual morph being a Sanctifier, for instance, was much higher.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/natus03/a821.jpg)This match is [covered on the Sideboard](/en/events/2003/natus03/round-8-brian-kibler-vs-david-humpherys), so I won't go into a tremendous amount of detail. Game 1 I was just able to overwhelm Dave, as his deck didn't really come online fast enough to deal with my start. Game 2 was extremely frustrating, as I stalled on three lands and then four for forever, with Dave casting multiple spells a turn while I was struggling to play more than a morph. I managed to stay alive for a long time through judicious blocking and managing what few resources I had on the table, and set up what would have been a nearly board-clearing cycled Decree of Pain on, what was essentially, my last possible turn to draw a land to survive, but I remained stuck on four and Dave even had the Slice and Dice to cycle to take out what was left of my team when I blocked the following turn, leaving me with effectively no outs.

Game 3 was quite stressful, as I had a relatively slow start and Dave played a 5/5 Aven Warhawk on his fifth turn. I was punching through some damage with Deftblade Elite distracting his huge flier, but that damage didn't seem to be adding up fast enough with the threat of Dave destroying my entire team with Slice and Dice at any moment. One turn, I misevaluate my chances of winning if he does have Slice and choose to hold back a Dive Bomber to leave mana open for Astral Steel, but I realize afterwards that even if I manage to save a creature with Astral Steel, there's no way I can come back against his Warhawk and Grassland Crusader, so I just go all-in the next turn and throw everything on the table. It turns out Dave does not have the Slice, and I'm able to squeeze in enough damage to kill him before he can draw it, leaving me as the sole player without a loss in the tournament.

Matches 8-0 Games 16-4

**Round 9 – Paul Rietzl**

Paul is a bit more confident coming into this round than he was prior to our last match, which is reasonable seeing as here he has an incredibly powerful r-u deck complete with Kilnmouth Dragon, Riptide Shapeshifter, Lavamancer's Skill, and Rushes of Knowledge full of Skirk Marauders, not to mention the fact that there's much less chance in this match that I'll be attacking him for thirteen on turn four. Game 1 is like something out of *Onslaught* Block Constructed, with huge effects swinging the game back and forth. Paul's Kilnmouth Dragon has an amusing staredown with my Dragonstalker, but draws him seven cards off a Rush of Knowledge in the meantime. Not to be outdone, I wipe the table with Decree of Pain and load up my own grip. Paul's Rush seems much less impressive as he has only a morph on the following turn, which turns out to be Skirk Marauder once I start playing out my own hand. A second Rush of Knowledge digs for two cards and sets Paul far behind in tempo. I dump my hand on the table and there's nothing he can do.

Game 2 is very close, as I cycle Eternal Dragon – prompting an exasperated sigh from Paul – and play a face down Cutthroat. Paul quickly flips up an Ascending Aven and starts attacking, and I realize pretty quickly that I'm not going to be able to afford to flip my Cutthroat if I'm going to win this race. I never have the opportunity to return my Eternal Dragon as my mana is too imminently crucial to work toward a longer game. Fortunately, this means Paul doesn't have the chance to take advantage of his much more powerful card drawing effects, and – much like against Mike in round seven – his creature quality can't compete with mine. I'm able to take down one of his fliers at a crucial point so he can't just race me in the air, and suddenly I'm a lock for Top 8.

Matches 9-0 Games 18-4

Now, from the end of Day 1 through round nine, Mark Rosewater had been periodically making comments about how he'd have to find someone else to do commentary for the Top 8. Unwilling to jinx myself, I'd been repeatedly noncommittal, making offhanded recommendations about who might be good, but always mentioning that I wasn't in the Top 8 just yet. With my spot all but mathematically guaranteed – even if I were to somehow 0-3, my tiebreakers at this point were absurd – I was finally willing to admit that I wouldn't be available for commentary. We tossed around a few names, like Ed Fear, EFro, Matt Ranks, and Tom Guevin, but Mark didn't seem to want to commit to anyone just yet. He joked that he'd just get me DQ'd in the last three rounds, but I pointed out that I felt I was probably a bit more valuable in the Top 8 than I was doing commentary for a few hours on Sunday and he conceded to finding a replacement.

I went out to a sushi lunch once again and gave my mother a call to tell her the good news. I joked with EFro about just not going back to the site – or just not even bringing my Standard deck – since I could theoretically make Top 8 without even playing another match. We discussed how I'd handle potential draws and concessions over the next few rounds – basically, if I felt someone was a good matchup for me in the Top 8, I'd either draw or even concede to them to get them in. If I felt it was a bad matchup, I'd play to try to knock them out so I wouldn't have to face them in single elimination format. My hope was for a Top 8 filled with both good players and good matchups, so I'd not only make the team but have a competent squad to take on the world in Berlin.

The next two rounds went according to plan, as I found myself paired against Neil Reeves and Jon Finkel, both good friends, good players, and fine matchups to boot. Things got messier when I was paired against Mike Turian in the last round, playing g-r, but I looked at the standings and decided that drawing with him would ensure that he wasn't in my first round bracket, so at worst I'd play him in the semifinals. Rather than wait for the rest of the Top 8 to be determined, Finkel, Justin Gary – who'd won his 11th round match and managed to draw in as well – EFro, and I went out for a celebratory – what else? – sushi dinner. After several kamikaze and lemon drop shots toasting to our hopefully continued success and returning a call from my mother to explain that I had not, in fact, played a single game since I'd last talked to her, I managed to once again lose the credit card game with Jon and Justin – putting me down something like $300 on the weekend in meals alone – before heading back to the site to see how things had shaped up.

"How things had shaped up" could be summed up in a single word – badly. Both Jordan Berkowitz and Josh Wagener had managed to win their last rounds, while Chris DeMaci had lost, which meant that in all likelihood two more red-green decks would be filling out the previously unknown spots in the Top 8. Sure enough, Tom Guevin missed on tiebreakers, which meant that Neil Reeves and I would be facing Jordan Berkowitz and Josh Wagener – and, more important, Compost – in the quarterfinals. Jon had what looked to be a good matchup against Justin Gary, but the winner of that match would face the winner of mine, so if things played out as they looked on paper, our National Team would end up including none of the big names from what looked to be the best U.S. Nationals Top 8 of all time.

Now, generally I don't write stream of consciousness asides in any of my articles, but I feel this particular instance is deserving of one. As I was writing this, my computer froze, and when I restarted it, apparently the Word autosave function somehow hadn't saved my work for the past four hours. So I was almost done with this already and now I have to relive missing the National Team all over again. As if it weren't painful enough the first time...

I head out that night to playtest my Top 8 matchup with Finkel, EFro, and Tony Tsai while YMG heads to their hotel to work on Justin's matchup. I figure Finkel and I can figure out the matchup against Wagener's deck together, since he's Jon's potential semifinal opponent – plus the YMG hotel is quite the hike and I wanted to get to sleep early. Game 1 is clearly quite bad, with Phantom Centaur essentially unkillable, but after sideboarding – despite Compost – things actually look up somewhat. With a full complement of Duresses and Cabal Therapies, an early Compost isn't nearly as likely, and without that engine running in the early game, the overload of creature removal actually works. Graveborn Muse gives you a Necropotence-esque drawing engine to try to race to Corrupts before you get burned out. Not the best, but entirely winnable – the matchup seemed around 50% after board in the games we played. Still not entirely confident, but feeling better about my odds, I went to sleep.

I awake early the next morning, either due to excitement, nerves, or Rubin and Hegstad coming in at 6 AM after staying out drafting all night – regardless of why I'm up, I can't get back to sleep. I shower and put on my one non-t-shirt I have with me – intended to be worn to go out Saturday night after missing Top 8 or in the Top 8 itself – and head over to the site. No one but the hardcore all night gamers are there, so I wander around listening to my headphones – "Rapture" off Paul van Dyk's Politics of Dancing Disc 1 was the track for this tournament. I've found my tournament music steadily progressing to all electronic music these days, both because I listen to more and more of it in general and because I've found that tracks that aren't lyrically based are more conducive to concentration. I actually wrote a term paper for a contemplative spirituality class which examined the implications of music on meditative forms of awareness, which was inspired at least in part by this shift.

Anyway, away from music and back to Magic. I run into Rosewater before the others arrive and talk to him a bit about how the Top 8 could go. Mark has a habit of always predicting the better player to win any given match regardless of the decks involved, an optimistic tendency of his which has by his estimation made him more accurate than not. I sometimes wish I could share that outlook with him, but unfortunately things in Magic don't always go that way.

Soon enough the rest of the Top 8 shows up, our pictures are taken, and we get our do's and don'ts of being on camera from Mark. I've heard the speech more than anyone, I'm sure, because of the doing commentary for the past couple years, but this time is particularly amusing as Mark pointedly looks in Neil Reeves' direction while informing us to refrain from inappropriate language on stage. I'm informed, however, that my Quarterfinals match will be taking place off camera – along with the Turian/Walls match – in order to fit the JSS Quarterfinals on stage as well. Freed from any responsibility to avoid screaming obscenities every time Compost came into play, I put on my headphones and made my way over to the table for my match.

**Quarterfinals – Josh Wagener**

My U.S.A. Basketball Dream Team jersey over my shoulder, I sat down in an attempt to keep the dream alive. As [the coverage shows](/en/events/2003/natus03/quarterfinals-brian-kibler-vs-joshua-wagener), sadly, that dream was not to be. Land light hands in the first two games kept me from really being in either game, with a fourth land in Game 1 almost definitely winning me the game, while Game 2 was likely a lost cause due to Compost showing up early on. Game 3 was closer than I like most double mulligans to be, again due to stalling on three lands for several turns, but eventually Corrupt was able to swing it. Game 4 was enormously frustrating to lose, as I ended the game with a pair of Corrupts in hand and five lands in play. Granted, I did skimp on land in deck construction, and this is the matchup where making every land drop is the most important because of the reliance on Corrupt, but missing turn four land drops three games straight was quite frustrating, to be sure.

I don't want to commit the classic gamer mistake of selective memory here, however, and make some sort of claim that I was unlucky. Sure, it was unfortunate that I had both a bad matchup and bad draws in the Top 8, but I certainly had my share of good fortune on my way to 9-0. It's like Matt Damon says in Rounders – "Few players recall big pots they have won. But every player can recall with remarkable accuracy the outstanding tough beats of his career." It's always ridiculous when our opponents play Sparksmith or Timberwatch Elf or Visara and beat us with it, and we regale our friends and teammates with our horrible stories of misfortune, but when we do the same, it's business as usual. If you were to listen to all the tales of woe told at any given Magic tournament over the course of the weekend, you'd get the impression that Magic players as a whole are a pretty unlucky bunch.

I spend the rest of the day appreciating what good fortune I do have – being that I'm of legal drinking age and now three thousand dollars richer – and take the edge off my loss with some good old fashioned liquid refreshment. I'm quite... refreshed by the time I meet up later that evening with the Your Move Games crew – and special guest Mark Rosewater – for a celebratory dinner at the Hark Rock Cafe. Justin Gary expresses his appreciation for having me on board if for no other reason than that it gives him someone to drink with on such occasions, and we knock back a few in toast to our finishes. While the others head off to sleep, Justin, Dave, Mark, Zvi and I walk back in the direction of the site, and Mark spots an ice cream shop he wants to stop at – conveniently located next to a tequila bar, which makes the stop more than agreeable to me.

I don't know if I would have ever really envisioned myself sitting on the patio of a tequila bar in San Diego taking shots with Justin Gary and Dave Humpherys while Zvi Mowshowitz and Mark Rosewater looked on with amusement, but there I was. The somewhat surreal scene didn't last very long, however, as early flights abounded among the motley crew, and soon it was off to another hotel to hang out with the usual suspects and then back to mine to get some sleep. After once again hitting up the sushi joint for lunch on Monday, I catch my flight back to Atlanta, and suddenly 2003 Nationals was over.

I had a great tournament, despite my 9-0 start ending in a train wreck in the Quarterfinals. I'd joked that I was going to name my report "from 9-0 to the nut low", both as a reference to how frustrating it is to miss the Team after not losing an actual match in the Swiss and to the eventual U.S. Team compared to the potential one. Interestingly enough, Justin Gary has been on both U.S. Teams with ridiculous bottom halves of the Top 8, with Finkel, McCarrel, Nate Clarke, and Mario Robaina being edged out this year by Neil Reeves, Turian myself, and Finkel once again for "best bottom four ever". I would've preferred a different distinction, myself, but at least I'm in good company.

So that's that. Nationals in a nutshell – a ten thousand word nutshell, but a nutshell nonetheless. I hope you enjoyed my little look back at my time in San Diego.
