### 

It’s a tough life, this Pro Tour thing. You get invited to all the tournaments most people fight and claw and maneuver for entry to, you see the world, meet fascinating people and, once in a while, are asked to go somewhere across the planet for free. I should have been an accountant. 

Seattle was my fourth Invitational and after more than five years of Pro Tour travel, it almost didn’t come about. Even before I found out I would be rejected at the English border, the notion of traveling 10,000 miles for a no-profit tournament wasn’t the most exciting one. Think about it: I travel every three weeks or so. Gone are the days where I cling to the window watching take off. Those days have been replaced by the reading of whatever book I happen to be perusing until an off-handed appraisal of the cabin suggests we’re off the ground. Traveling is no longer a vacation for me. Vacation is when I’m at home. Woe is me.

In addition to the rigors of travel, there were other negatives. My constant Invitational travel companion Kai Budde wouldn’t be attending, for one. Kai and I had made a habit of heading to the more exotic Invitational locations about five days ahead of time every year for a little practice, a little gaming and a lot of good times. Throw in the fact that having my picture on a card means a lot less to me than it does to most of my competitors and you had a trip, now shortened to the length of the Invitational festivities, that wasn’t looking too exciting.

Admitted to the Invitational by way of internet ballot for the third time, I decided that if people were going to bother to vote for me, the least I could do is show up. If the Invitational had been in Buenos Aires as originally planned, the ballot may not have provided enough incentive, but I’d only been to Seattle once, for 1998 Worlds, and while there didn’t get around to seeing WotC corporate. That was the clincher. We booked a ticket from Heathrow and when I suddenly found myself living in Canada, WotC was able to get that departure switched to Pearson International out of Toronto.

While Kai wasn’t in attendance, a number of other people joined him, most of who had been invited to Invitationals past, in addition to being suspended at some time or another. That left an Invitational full of good peeps (as all the homies say) and no antagonism. I’m a very antagonize-able kind of guy, so that lack of stress was a good thing. 

I arrived in Seattle early and traversed the 300 some odd meters from my hotel to Wizards, which was pretty much as I’d pictured: a low-ceilinged cubical-filled casual-dress workplace decorated in all manner of gaming memorabilia. With lots of candy. The candy wouldn’t be worth mentioning, except every desk seemed to have five or six varieties. This was handy come tour time. I’m starting to understand the expansion of certain WotC waist lines.

Slowly, I started bumping into assorted staff, going out to the famous mana pool for a game of catch with Brian Schneider, Mike Donais and … who was that guy? Neither of them seemed to know, but he just randomly joined in and soon I was throwing far wonkier passes than my counterparts, doing my all to get the pigskin both wet and dirty. Good times. I never could throw a football properly. Eventually, I headed back to the hotel and bumped into Carlos Romao and Diego Ostrovich and before we knew it, we were chatting away like old friends. I’d met both of them briefly, Carlos at 2002 Worlds and Diego at 2001 Worlds where I SMASHED him despite his first-turn Hidden Gibbons against my blue-heavy deck, but I’d never really gotten the chance to know these gents. One thing about the Invitational, by the end of it, you know everyone pretty well. I was glad for the chance to get to know these two.

Everyone else but Tomi Walamies showed up over the next few hours and drafts were plentiful, especially when WotC started supplying the product, courtesy of Mark Rosewater. The days and nights turned mostly into a drafting blur but, needless to say, I learned a lot from drafting with fifteen of the world’s finest. At some point, we took a break for WotC-funded meals, where this non-drinker wasn’t hindered by either the two drink maximum or any angst against those trying to stretch it, thanks to the grandiose performance put on by Matt Vienneau in 2001. By the way, I’m partly responsible for Matt’s showing, as he, Rosewater (who also doesn’t drink), Dave Price and I shared tables for both of last year’s wine tastings, with Mark and I feeding our ‘dates’. Needless to say, Dave is a far more tranquil lush than my Canadian counterpart.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/mi02/980.jpg)Eventually, my roommate Eivind Nitter showed up and joined the drafting festivities. Eivind is one of those lovable, somewhat spacey guys that are always good for a few laughs and a few more good times and he didn’t disappoint. During our first draft, he leaned over, showed me a Boneknitter, covers the “Bonek” with his thumb and chortles “heh… heh… Nitter… hehe”. To this day, I can’t look at the card without having to stifle a laugh. 

On one of the days, the WotC staff got sick of us and ditched us in the middle of Seattle with the hopes we’d get lost in the big city. They called it a tour. Olivier Ruel, Jens Thoren, Eivind, Gab ‘yellow hat’ Nassif and I start looking for food, finally finding this run-down little diner where everyone gets something good except Olivier, who ordered a bowl of gray. No missing words there. Through the meal, France’s clown prince tried to tempt us with some of his gray, even going so far as to offer an exchange of goods with each of us. His efforts went unrewarded. We headed out and did the cultural thing, using our few hours in the city to go see a movie, White Oleander. Eivind and I loved it, while Olivier and Nassif seemed impartial. Often wordy Jens was having fun balancing five kinds of food and his drink on his tummy as he slumped down in his chair. Every time I’d look at him, he’d just pat his stomach, saying ‘plate’ with a smile. Good times.

After the movie, we headed to this very cool arcade. Now, I’m not much of a video game man, but I had to take part in he live-action fighting game where you actually made the motions for your character. In the end, Olivier beat me up. Literally. Then, it was on to Dance Dance Revolution, and no, I did not play, but I did manage to win $10 when Olivier bet me that Jens would beat Nitter. Who bets against Nitter? Gotta’ love the French. Be it Magic or otherwise, they’re usually good for a couple of bills.

Thursday night, after the newly non-alcoholic tour (for some of us. Dave Price’s report does no justice to the level of intoxication those deviants reached), a lot of drafting and some good eating had been completed, Eivind and I made our way to the room (which was huge; good job WotC) and got into our respective beds.

Me: Hey, do you have a deck for tomorrow (Online Extended)?  
 Eivind: No, do you?  
 Me: Not a one.

WotC had given us access to a special **Magic Online** server that would allow us to practice the formats, but for some reason I couldn’t get on, and at the same time Eivind didn’t have a computer, so we were both up a certain creek without a paddle. Additionally, with the mess my life had been over the proceeding weeks, I really hadn’t had much time to think about decks, especially with Houston on the horizon. Eivind suggested blue-green threshold could be really solid with a lot of *Onslaught* fetch lands, so I grabbed the hotel stationary and started writing down every card we could think of until we’d reached sixty. Not a single card was scratched off the list, with the endeavor taking around 90 seconds. Pro Tour deck building, ladies and gents.

Morning came, and with it came the tournament. First up was Two-Headed Giant, the high-fun-low-skill portion of the tournament. Basically, the way Two-Headed Giant works is that you have a lot of cards from which to build two decks with a guy who doesn’t speak much English, then you wait until one of your foursome is mana screwed, at which point their opponents are declared the winners. Mark announced that roommates would team up for the first round, meaning Eivind and I would face Diego and Carlos and then we’d mix it up. I remember Eivind having the insane draw while worrying about his misclicking en route to our winning round one, Carlos and I taking advantage of Eivind and Diego’s troubles in round two, and then fighting the brave and valiant fight as only Diego’s intentional ministrations on his roommate’s behalf prevented my otherwise inevitable victory (translation: our cards were crap and I think one of us double mulliganed and they were SOOO lucky). The best part of these rounds was meeting Carlos at the donut counter after each game.

So, next up was Online Extended with mine and Eivind’s finely tuned machine in hand… or disk… whatever. The whole **Magic Online** aspect of this tournament was weird and cool and a little daunting all at once. At past Invitationals, repeat visitors like Chris Pikula, Dave Price and I had grown accustomed to playing in front of crowds of, say, six, maybe seven people on Days 1 and 2, so seeing hundreds upon hundreds in the chat rooms waiting on the results was exhilarating. Finally, we had an exhibition tournament with an actual audience. Granted, there were few schmucks out there craving a little attention and making statements reflecting as much but, for the most part, the community was great about the whole thing. At the same time, having those games preserved for all time means that any misplay or misclick is recorded forever in the annals. Fortunately, I was able to overcome all fears about such things and screw up time and again at a record pace, at the same time allowing myself a certain amount of leisure in the process (translation: I didn’t pay all that much attention, so if there are mistakes in those games, don’t bother mentioning them to me; it will take too long to catalogue them all and I just clicked as fast as possible without thinking too much).

My first Extended opponent was Itaru Ishida, which was definitely a bad thing as the Japanese always come with finely tuned, unpredictable Constructed decks and beat me down with them (for reference, see my matches against Ishii – Rage, Obliterate, Kavu Chameleon and Keldon Necropolis against my 17-counter Humility deck and Fujita – the first ever Rice Snack… I think I cast one creature before dying, in 2000 and 2001 respectively). Given this psychological disadvantage, I decided I needed to go further in the hole, so after a good start, I misclicked when Itaru cast Fact or Fiction, giving him piles of five and zero. The fool took the larger pile, meaning it would take him far longer to use up all those spells. This would prove a real hindrance were he to play Ensnaring Bridge or Cursed Scroll, but he just started taking control of the game. However, I am a smart man: with Aquamoeba and Centaur Garden facing down five thousand, three hundred and forty-six bear tokens, I make the decision to draw my last Garden with one card in hand, and with 

Wonder

in the graveyard, I was able to pump the ‘Moeba and fly over for the win. PT Skills (translation: lucky S.O.B.).

Second round, I face off with Yellow Hat Nassif, his amazing losing streak and his more amazing 4-color deck, which proved very good when drawing all four colors in the opening hand. I could have won this one except for two things: 1) I felt very badly about Nassif’s poor start to his first Invitational and decided to let him win and 2) His deck outdrew, outperformed and outdid mine, smashing my face in two games straight. This would be the only match Eivind and I would lose in the format. Call us Ormerod-Ronaldson, Buehler-Lauer or Mowshowitz-Johns. I’m not sure which of us is Zvi though. In the last round of Online-X, I played Brian Kibler who came with red-green-black beats. Unfortunately for him, red-green-black doesn’t beat the Nimble Mongoose-Worship combo. Skills again, skills again.

So, I was 4-2 and flying high. After a meal of some sort, I took it upon myself to sleep and awaken to play more **Magic**. The rigors of the Pro Tour life. Next up was Auction of the People. I’m a big fan of the auctions, going back to my baseball fantasy draft days. This year, I didn’t do too much research, playing a few games a few hours beforehand. I was pretty pleased with the deal I got on “L”, a potentially strong deck whose mana base wouldn’t be hurt as much as some others do to the ability to loot into needed lands.

My first opponent in this format was Walamies and his “G” deck, a white weenie of sorts with a splash of red. Starting at identical hand sizes and life counts, we agreed I had a slight edge right up until he’d smashed my face in two straight with some amazing topdeck skills. I got him back though: a few conversations later, I’d arranged for his less-than ceremonial fall from gravy train status.

Next up was Dave Humpherys and the “F” deck, the consensus number two in the pool and a steal at the price Dave paid. Unfortunately, all of the speculation about the deck was true and despite the fact I just needed one moderate-math topdeck to win, I felt like I lost one I deserved to lose here. Finally, I was paired with Grand Prix king Alex Shvartsman, of whose “Q” deck I made fast work. There were donuts to be had upon completion.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/92/79/9279b221a3f1b483796b2aa1f25032a33b14bd120c6df28d5099e489b30ba1db.jpg)The next format up for grabs was easily my strongest and favorite as a gamer: Rochester draft. I love the Roch. With the maneuvering, communication and other strategies, Rochester has always been a biggie for me (bring on Chicago!). This was back when the format was still fresh for everyone and no one wanted blue, so when, in seat seven, the blue cards in pack one went undrafted, I remedied that. Now here’s a little lesson for you aspiring pro players out there: if you go blue, make sure Mistform Skyreaver and two 

Future Sight

s get opened and passed to you. This is crucial for success, as I found out the hard-but-not-so-bad way. In short, my deck was nuts, regardless of the misclick that left a Smother in my sideboard. I deemed it good enough to board in on occasion.

My first Rochester match was against my good buddy and fellow blue mage Olivier. Olivier made a crucial error in the draft, receiving only one Skyreaver and no Future Sights from his neighbors. Idiot. Throw in a couple of poor draws on Frenchie’s part and it was a fairly easy win. Next up was Jens Thoren who, to the annoyance of most, wouldn’t shut his mouth all weekend. Jens put up a solid but ultimately futile effort as his red-green deck, shockingly, wasn’t able to handle a 6/6 flying beast… or alligator… or angel. By the way, in case you were fooled, Jens is not a natural blond. Last, but not least, was Brian Davis’s strong black-green deck, helped, in part, by cooperative drafting from his left, hurt in part by pugilistic drafting from his right. Brian and I played a good close match, going the full three before I squeaked it out. Sad to say, despite my 3-0, I can’t say I won the table because Jon Finkel, sitting behind me, went 3-0 with his white-red deck. Can’t win ‘em all I guess, even when you do.

Last was the new Standard. Eivind and I concentrated out efforts on doing as little work as humanly possible, taking our Extended deck, removing the four Yavamaya Coasts that would have warranted disqualification and replacing them with other lands. Brilliant! It made sense, after all, we’d gone a combined 5-1 in Extended, so if it worked once, it had to work again. Right? Apparently this was bizarro **Magic** world and we were dead wrong, going a combined 1-5. I knew the Coast was a good card and all, but until that result, I never fully comprehended why those things were selling for 18 tickets on **Magic Online**. Lesson learned.

My first match was against Chris Pikula, whose own blue-green deck looked pretty bad to me. After all, Hampton Court Palace figured out that Counterspell was pretty much crap in blue-green by the time Euros rolled around and City of Brass, gem that it is, is no Yavimaya Coast in the two-color deck but hey, what’s it matter? Chris outdrew and probably outplayed me, spanking me like a kid in a candy st… huh? Whatever, I went down hard.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/95/f4/95f4d9734e18f7212f545fa54f6e2d94ffffedc77978ad7aa9c21e9f554b6f82.jpg)Round fourteen found me paired up against Dave Price, who I was shocked to see was playing Sligh, as he’d only done so in every major tournament since the Spanish Inquisition (he played Stompy there). Goblin Piledriver was pretty good, but Worship was better and, fortunately for me, when Mystic Enforcer was my last man, Dave couldn’t find a then-tech Threaten to end me once and for all. That meant a final round pairing against mighty Jon Finkel would either put me in the Top 2 or send me to golf season.

Finkel who, despite his protestations to the contrary, is a gamer’s gamer, played Battle of Wits in every format possible, including the Auction. Good stuff. Jon told me his chances of winning this match, in which a win would put him 2nd or 3rd, were slim and none, which he proved by leaving deep indentations in my face before dumping my carcass on the roads of Renton. Brutal. The feature match was so lopsided that they stopped covering it halfway through. It was ridiculous. I felt like Germany playing a World Cup final or The Netherlands watching the first round. Jon played well, opting to draw three cards when I activated his Standstill in response to him activating his Standstill (I was trying to deck him) and beat me. I mean, come on. He’s JON FREAKING FINKEL! What’d you expect?

The rest of the weekend went off without a hitch, as I subjected myself to Chris Pikula’s merciless wit while enjoying some pizza at Rosewater’s house (good job taking those pre-Pokemon stock options Mark!). It was a good end to a good weekend and perhaps the end of an era, with Dave Price admitting he was pretty much done and Chris possibly soon to follow. Time erodes all things eventually but hey, I’m sure sometime soon, I’ll write an article looking back at the past and reminisce on how those guys were very good in the 90’s.

Thank you so much for sending me to the Invitational, folks. I guess hobnobbing with some of the game’s darker characters in the past dampened my outlook on the event, but this year’s experience, with WotC headquarters, the WotC staff and fifteen really good guys to compete against was a great time and a memorable one. If I’m fortunate enough to get the opportunity, I won’t be so jaded as to not realize the honor you bestowed upon me next time around, though I’ll still refuse to pander for votes before they’re tallied… it sort of feels outside the spirit of the event to me. Thanks guys, it means a lot. Vote Turian and Dougherty!

Have a good one.
