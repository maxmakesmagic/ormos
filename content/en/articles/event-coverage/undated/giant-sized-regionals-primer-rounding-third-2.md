### 

Having already gone over [u-g madness](/en/articles/event-coverage/2003/04-23-giant-sized-regionals-primer-blue-green-madness) and [Psychatog](/en/articles/event-coverage/2003/04-25-giant-sized-regionals-primer-psychatog), we are left with possibly the most popular deck you will see at this year’s Regional Championships: r-g beatdown.

Despite representing a green guys-and-bolts strategy that realistically predates either of its two main contemporaries, this year’s incarnation of r-g feels to me the youngest of the Big Three archetypes. U-g madness is grandfathered from *Odyssey* Block and Psychatog has been ruling Standard since the San Diego Masters. Despite the fact that the r-g color combination was the dominant deck of the Tokyo Block Constructed tournament, despite the fact that it has a rich history that predates Flametongue Kavu, predates Kird Apes, and has been winning major tournaments since players realized you could super glue a Channel to a Disintegrate, the current incarnation of r-g seems firmly rooted in the current metagame, a baby beater rediscovered as a child of the *Onslaught*, and especially, *Odyssey* blocks, after taking the summer off.

Though Jeff Cunningham’s Elvish Archers deck took his Provincial Championships months before the Chicago Masters, the version of r-g upon which everyone seems to be basing their builds is the post-*Legions* version posted by Kai Budde.

This list is eclectic and because you are probably used to looking at it by now, you may have forgotten how odd it probably seemed the first time you saw it. This deck isn’t the 187 machine that Ryan Fuller rode to his perfect record in Tokyo. It isn’t a jumbo-sized Fires of Yavimaya deck where even pre-Wild Mongrel “best bear ever” River Boa might be considered too small and ineffectual to play. Despite the fact that Reckless Charge is remains available, it isn’t built like the blisteringly quick Frog in a Blender; despite the fact that Repulse is gone but Beast Attack remains, it isn’t Fat Crank either. Kai’s is sort of a realistically built “good stuff” deck, aggressive, but fairly middle of the road. It has some fast beats, it has some solid removal, but at the end of the day, it doesn’t top off in either direction.

Specifically, Kai’s list seems to settle the question that various players have tried to answer with everything from Ironshell Beetle to Nantuko Tracer. Skirk Marauder has grown into the two-drop slot not for any real excellence, but because it does the humble job it is asked to do a little better than the other available creatures; as David Price said in his [preview](/en/articles/event-coverage/2003/01-08-legions-card-preview-skirk-marauder), red decks have often settled for cards like Firebrand Ranger and Rage Weaver to fill the two-drop spot. R-g just wants a body to play on turn two if Wild Mongrel hasn’t shown up… the fact that Skirk Marauder can do anything else is just gravy.

That being said, with the solidification of the r-g list with *Legions*, there is very little room for variance away from Kai’s. Gone for the most part are the Fiery Tempers that we may have seen in Brian Kibler’s list, or the Blistering Firecats from Jeff Cunningham’s. Part of the reason that r-g will be so popular is that its core cards are so centrally focused that it is difficult to build incorrectly and the strategy is so intuitive that the cards fall into place in such a way that wide variation doesn’t make a lot of sense. Aaron Forsythe, editor of MagicTheGathering.com and once a successful r-g Regionals player in his own right, commented:

> *“The deck is relatively cheap to build, quite well-tuned and fun to play. Granted, there's not a lot of room for "personalizing" the deck—every slot is just about set in stone at this point—but if you want a good chance for success and quick rounds, this is probably the deck for you.”*

If there is variance in a r-g list, it will generally be within about two cards of those two Skirk Marauders. My longtime friend Jonathan Becker told me that he thought Skirk Marauder was underpowered; when I asked him what two-drop he would prefer, Elvish Warrior or one of the many 2/1 creatures for ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif), he said he’d rather play Phantom Centaur in that slot. I pointed out that the r-g deck needs a redundant two-drop to back up the Mongrel and especially as an Elephant Guide target, pointing out that none other than Zvi Mowshowitz had cut two burn spells to *add* more Skirk Marauders; Jon responded that Markus Koenigstein’s list, which was the r-g to win the Last Chance Qualifier in Venice, made precisely this change.

But none of this tells you why a player would want to run r-g beatdown to begin with. Why would an aggressively-minded player choose this deck – which looks suspiciously like an *Odyssey* Block deck that nobody played in the actual *Odyssey* Block because they could play one of three different u-g archetypes – go with r-g beatdown over u-g madness? Back when r-g had access to far better two drops and u-g had access to nothing more of consequence than Yavimaya Coast, former PT Champ Mike Long said “This deck just seems to be outclassed by u-g beats. It only has the Mongrel to discard and its other two drop\[s\], Yavimaya Barbarian and Blurred Mongoose \[were\] last seen beating up Merfolk last year during the big Static Opposition kick. This year, it's no match for big Wurms and Mongrels.” 

So, now without Flametongue Kavu, with the vastly inferior Skirk Marauder as its ancillary two-drop, the question remains… why run r-g?

While it, too, has a lot of non-basic lands, including nearly as many painful ones as Witt’s hybrid deck, Kai’s has the kinds of lands that make the appropriate color of mana and smooth out early game development. The power of r-g in Standard, the upgrade that makes it Tier One here when it was virtually unplayed in *Odyssey* Block, is the superiority of the mana base over the u-g deck.

Where Alexander Witt was forced to play four copies of probably the most dangerous land of all time, where Ken Ho had to stoop to playing with Tarnished Citadel to smooth out his mana base in Osaka, Kai can consistently play with early drops like turn-1 Llanowar Elves and turn-2 Wild Mongrel, support the double-![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif) Phantom Centaur as early as turn 3 and effortlessly cast ![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif) cards like Violent Eruption come the midgame.

It may be true that u-g can outclass r-g in terms of being able to pump out 6/6 creatures on turn 4 or even turn 3, draw lots of extra cards and fly over the opponent’s best defenses, it has to draw the right lands to do so. Like I said in [Single Forest, Double Island](/en/articles/event-coverage/2003/02-21-single-forest-double-island), the enemy of the u-g deck is oftentimes the u-g deck itself. If you draw the right lands, you will consistently smash your opponents. If you draw a Windswept Heath, forest, and island, you might lose a game to triple token creature while gripping Turbulent Dreams. Probably the most persuasive reason to run r-g over u-g is that over the course of the eight or nine Swiss rounds of Regionals, you will be more likely to have the freedom and agency of losing games to your own mistakes than be handicapped by the erratic nature of your ostensibly two-color mana base.

The other incentive to r-g is the excellence of the burn spells. Violent Eruption is a golden card on four mana, doing slightly more than Arc Lightning (once a ubiquitous spell in red decks), for slightly more mana. In the r-g beatdown deck, it will often perform as a miniature Giant Growth while clearing the opponent’s blockers, doing an additional point or two to the head, all on just three lands. Grim Lavamancer, too, is extremely annoying. Even when another creature deck seems to lock down the ground, holding off conventional attacks, Grim Lavamancer can pull out those last couple of points you need to win a game. His ability can take out opposing small creatures with little or no real restriction and serves as a recurring Shock that can’t be checked by permission.

Volcanic Hammer may be the most overlooked advantage that the r-g beatdown deck brings to the table. Snubbed as everything from the bad Incinerate to the worse Lightning Bolt, Volcanic Hammer doesn’t seem to do what we have come to expect from a red spell at its mana cost. That being said, it is surprising how difficult the decision making process for another Wild Mongrel player can be when you direct Volcanic Hammer at the opponent’s best bear ever on turn two. Generally functioning as a Gerrard’s Verdict at least, you will find the opponent doing everything from pitching a second Wild Mongrel to the first in addition to another card, to setting up solid, recurring card advantage with your Grim Lavamancer. Volcanic Hammer is a subtle and incredible weapon against the cards some of your opponents will lean on to win sideboarded games and is an efficient packet of damage when simply sent to the opponent’s head.

### U-G Madness

You have probably seen a lot of disparate information about this matchup around the internet. With noted players advocating both sides, it is probably hard to determine which deck wins.

In my experience, over the course of many games, u-g has come out on top in this matchup. That being said, which deck has the advantage has a lot to do with the individual templating of the opposing u-g deck.

When I tested r-g vs. my u-g threshold deck, the key cards were early Wild Mongrel as well as Roar of the Wurm. If the u-g deck got Roar of the Wurm, it would typically overwhelm the r-g deck. Each Roar tended to be a “free” card, while itself asking multiple cards from the r-g deck in order to be stopped. If the r-g deck got Wild Mongrel on the second turn and the u-g deck didn’t, it would typically be overwhelmed by the speed of cards like Elephant Guide and Violent Eruption.

Elephant Guide itself is one of the most interesting cards in this matchup, being alternately unstoppable or unplayable. A few weeks ago, when u-g decks played no bounce, I would say that it was a breaker for r-g. On a Wild Mongrel, Elephant Guide allowed the r-g player to go toe-to-toe with even a Roar of the Wurm token and could put or keep the u-g player on the defensive as early as the third turn. These days, many (but not all) u-g decks seem to be playing Unsummon. Jeff Cunningham recently added 3 Unsummons to his u-g deck, which goes a long way in the r-g vs. u-g matchup, especially in terms of neutralizing r-g’s best spell. Elephant Guide is nowhere near as strong against u-g in the presence of Unsummon, but on the other hand, Cunningham’s list, in particular, removes one Roar of the Wum in order to make this change, which can be good for the r-g player.

Brainburst has released this information to “free” status, and you can read Cunningham’s commentary [there](https://web.archive.org/web/20050505202722/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.brainburst.com/db/article.asp?ID=2933&origin=sideboard_strategy_20030429a).

Game one victories for r-g will generally revolve around early damage, probably on the back of Wild Mongrel, while the u-g player sets up his defenses. The u-g player’s creatures are bigger than yours and their Wild Mongrels get to be a lot more effective after the first few turns, but the u-g player will generally be playing defense until he gets Roar of the Wurm online. The challenge for the r-g deck is to pick up an early lead, and then penetrate the u-g defenses or deal lethal damage via burn before he is overwhelmed by flyers.

Sideboarded games tend to be a lot better for r-g. The reason is that r-g can bring in Ensnaring Bridge. Even one Ensnaring Bridge can buy several turns from a u-g player, while the r-g player should once again try to exploit an early lead with Violent Eruption and Grim Lavamancer. Today, there are few u-g lists out there packing even a single Naturalize, meaning that the u-g players with 2 Turbulent Dreams are playing to set up a single Wonder turn where they can lift the Ensnaring Bridge and attack with lethal damage in the sky before the r-g player can replay the Bridge.

The key card most u-g players bring in against r-g will be Equilibrium. For this reason, r-g should generally not keep Elephant Guide in, even if the opponent did not display main deck Unsummon. Equilibrium can also fill the r-g player’s hand with creatures, lessening the efficacy of Ensnaring Bridge.

Besides the –4 Elephant Guide for +4 Ensnaring Bridge swap, I would also tend to take out Skirk Marauder for Phantom Centaur. With no Elephant Guides in the deck, the need for a 2-power two-drop is greatly reduced. At the same time, Phantom Centaur can come out as early as turn 3 and is a real threat that u-g has to deal with. It is outclassed by only one type of creature, Roar of the Wurm, mowing down Arrogant Wurm and Wild Mongrel while living to tell the tale.

### The Mirror

Most mirror matches come down to card advantage over tempo. Dave Price, onetime King of Beatdown, was notorious for drawing first in the red-on-red matchup, saying that the one extra card he got more than made up for his opponent’s ability to play the first Jackal Pup… he was going to Shock it anyway.

In this vein, the r-g player will want to maximize the amount of card advantage he can generate, while minimizing the effects of his opponent’s card advantage. With solid early game plays like Firebolt and Volcanic Hammer available, I think it is safe to remove Llanowar Elves, though I wish there were some way to add land. Llanowar Elves can help the best draws, but when you are trying to minimize the opponent’s long-game card advantage, they may just end up targets for Skirk Marauders, Grim Lavamancers, and Firebolts, without offering any card advantage themselves; the last thing you will want to do is give the opponent an additional card when he draws his best burn spell (Violent Eruption). Phantom Centaur should definitely come in and in the absence of any instant removal besides Violent Eruption (and you should generally be able to see when that might go off), Elephant Guide becomes a real breaker card.

There is almost no way to stop a Phantom Centaur enchanted with Elephant Guide. It will tear apart anything put in front of it and keep swinging. You can theoretically stop it with Ensnaring Bridge, but I don’t think that symmetrical cards in a 56-card mirror match are very good. Think about it like this: your opponent is going to have 4 business cards where you have 4 cards that affect both players equally. If you want to hedge your bet against an opposing Ensnaring Bridge, Naturalize is fine for the other two Llanowar Elves and can be a great surprise play when the opponent goes for the Elephant Guide. That being said, I don’t think that I would play Naturalize in the second game if I had won the first, instead choosing to play 2-3 copies of Caller of the Claw. This elf can help reduce the impact of an opposing Violent Eruption and can play surprise trick at the end of the opponent’s turn, setting up a lethal alpha strike that he may not have seen.

### Psychatog

This matchup should be yours in game one. For conventional Psychatog decks, turn-2 Wild Mongrel, turn-3 Elephant Guide is going to leave a mark. The combination of the r-g deck’s quick clocks and heavy burn component, as well as the nature of the Psychatog deck’s response cards, sets up a favorable scenario for the beatdown deck.

Psychatog decks tend to have no large-scale answer card other than Upheaval, so there is no reason to not play out every threat that you draw. The Psychatog deck has a lot of mana-efficient response cards, but if you can keep the pressure on, it can be difficult for the opponent to find an opening to cast Concentrate or Deep Analysis to refill. Unlike in the u-g-on-Psychatog matchup, r-g can lay an Elephant Guide when the opponent taps out, or finish him with burn even when it looks like the board is stabilized.

The Psychatog decks you will see this weekend have been modified since Kai Budde lost to his own r-g deck in Stockholm. Rather than being purely anti-control, they now pack everything from main deck Ghastly Demise to possibly the more problematic Aether Burst, which can counter Elephant Guide with no consequence.

The Psychatog player will sometimes be put in a position where he taps out for Dr. Teeth on the third turn. In the wrong situation, this can be catastrophic; the opponent can just block repeatedly while keeping his Psychatog huge with Concentrate and Deep Analysis. If you have Wild Mongrel and Elephant Guide, though, the opponent will not generally be able to block, or will be able to kill the Wild Mongrel only at great cost.

Conventional wisdom says that Compost is the lead card for r-g against Psychatog. Psychatog decks in 2003 have Innocent Blood, Chainer’s Edict, Smother, and Ghastly Demise as potential 1-for-1 plays, not to mention the incidental black cards pitched to

Once you start sending fire at Doctor Teeth, the opponent will have a hard time keeping his Atog alive against most of the 3- and 4-damage burn spells to begin with and if the creature itself dies, you can draw additional cards. At the same time, resources spent keeping the Psychatog alive will reduce the opponent’s ability to set up the nine mana necessary for the Upheaval kill.

That being said, Psychatog decks are primarily playing for time. If they can assemble the right hand, they will be willing to use their black removal spells as “bad bounce.” Think about it like this: we consider Aether Burst and Unsummon good creature sanction spells despite the fact that they do not actually kill creatures. Unsummon a Wild Mongrel and it moves from the board, where it is dangerous, to the hand, where it is not (yet). Smother a Wild Mongrel with Compost in play, and essentially the same exchange occurs. Scary Wild Mongrel leaves play and something, probably something not as scary as a Wild Mongrel, jumps into the r-g player’s hand. While the Psychatog player refills with Concentrate and Deep Analysis, or worse yet, sets up Standstill, he may be able to keep pace with Compost long enough to resolve Upheaval. Once that happens, the board is reset and all the r-g player’s card advantage work can be undone. It doesn’t happen very often, but there are definitely r-g players out there telling sob stories about the games they lost with Compost going.

Additionally, Alter Reality has become a staple sideboard card for the Psychatog player. A single Alter Reality will counter multiple Composts. This card will make your alleged sideboard trump look silly.

I think the scarier card for Psychatog is Phantom Centaur. If it hits the board, there is nothing much a Psychatog player can do to hold it in check over the long term. If he has Aether Burst, that is a reasonable response and certainly Persuasion can be a problem, but Phantom Centaur dodges Innocent Blood and Ghastly Demise and makes Psychatog a poor blocker. Even though Phantom Centaur can technically be the target of Alter Reality, the situations where that response will win the game are few.

### Astral Slide

This is a dismal matchup. In game one, you will have to win quickly or not at all. Every card in the Slide deck trumps every card on your team. Multiple creatures? Meet Wrath of God. Meet Starstorm. Meet Slice and Dice. It is entirely possible the opponent will not even have to spend actual spells while mowing down your small creatures with Lightning Rift. Burn spells? Teroh’s Faithful, Renewed Faith, and Exalted Angel are all present to frustrate those efforts. 

You will have to accept that the lives of your creatures will be short and that you have to eke out every bit of effectiveness in order to make them matter. Elephant Guide is poor against Astral Slide, but if you can get a hit or two in before Astral Slide comes online, you can buy some precious life points, and potentially have a creature left when the opponent busts a Wrath of God.

In sideboard games, you should clearly bring in Naturalize and Phantom Centaur. Naturalize can go a long way, but you only have two; Phantom Centaur is tough to kill, and will reset in size if the opponent ever aims Astral Slide at it. Caller of the Claw can give you a lift after a Wrath of God or the equivalent. None of your main deck cards are bad, per se, but many of them are small. Being small in size marginalizes your 1- toughness creatures, especially against Lightning Rift and Slice and Dice, but you have to balance the effectiveness of your sideboard cards against the speed you need to win. I would cut Basking Rootwalla first. Cutting the Rootwalla is like cutting Llanowar Elves in the mirror match, but in this case, the speed Llanowar Elves can give you in armoring up with Elephant Guide, playing Call of the Herd on turn two, or even representing the extra body that can fuel Caller of the Claw, far outweighs the ability to pump Basking Rootwalla, which is not going to ever happen in the early turns of any game you can expect to win. I’d deal with the balance by pulling some burn spells, probably two Firebolts and one Violent Eruption. Firebolt is objectively a powerful spell for its mana cost, but with no early creatures in the Slide deck, it is just a card that will do 2 points of damage once; most of your sideboard cards offer a better deal. Violent Eruption is a tough cut, but it is also the most purely difficult cast in the r-g deck. Burn for the most part is going to be unimpressive against an opponent with access to almost no upper limit of life gain, much of which occurs while drawing extra cards.

Astral Slide is traditionally considered a weak deck against Psychatog and some of the other methodical board control decks. If you choose r-g at Regionals, hope that that these opponents keep the PT Champion deck out of your bracket.

### Beasts

Gary Wise’s metagame archetype at the Chicago Masters is, for the purposes of the r-g mage, exactly the same deck as Astral Slide. It shares two, if not three, of the same colors, has many of the same lands and wins much the same way.

Beasts, like Astral Slide, will dominate the r-g mage on the ground. Rather than actually removing creatures, it will just play bigger stuff than the r-g player, making attacking unattractive. Much like Astral Slide, the beasts deck can gain enormous life, this time via Ravenous Baloth, making racing difficult. Once Contested Cliffs comes online, it can also start picking off the smaller r-g threats.

In game one, beasts may actually be a more difficult opponent than Astral Slide. When creature decks clash, the one with the bigger blockers often takes the win. Add the same endgame threat (Exalted Angel), put it in a more aggressive deck and you will have much the same result for r-g, except packaged in a shorter game one. Much as in the matchup against Slide, Elephant Guide will be r-g’s most likely route to victory. Beasts doesn’t have Astral Slide or any real removal outside of Contested Cliffs, so a strategic Elephant Guide can be extremely hard to stop. I’ve heard of games where a r-g variant with Birds of Paradise armored up that 0/1 flyer on turn 2 and rolled over a PT player two games in a row. Any victories for r-g against beasts will come on the heels of similarly unlikely developments.

As opposed to the Slide matchup, r-g has a powerful weapon in Ensnaring Bridge in games two and three. Winning a conventional game will usually only occur when the beasts deck has a poor draw, so r-g’s best plan may be to sit behind Ensnaring Bridge and go for the burn plan. Improbable as it may seem, it is also theoretically possible to deck a beasts deck with only 1-2 Nantuko Vigilantes given your 4 Ensnaring Bridges. One thing you should try to avoid is killing your opponent’s Nantuko Vigilante once it has already taken out an Ensnaring Bridge. The last thing you want is to allow the opponent to use Genesis to take out later Ensnaring Bridges or Elephant Guides.

Firebolt is your friend in this matchup. It takes out turn-1 Birds of Paradise and Llanowar Elves and stops Exalted Angel before it is able to morph. Besides Elephant Guide and the sideboarded Ensnaring Bridge, Firebolt will be among the r-g deck’s best cards in staving off the beasts deck’s dominance over the board, at least in the early turns.

I wouldn’t be too worried about beasts, were I an aspiring r-g mage. Beasts may be great against r-g, but the other two members of the Big Three give this archetype fits. Beasts has answers to Wonder and Roar of the Wurm only in its sideboard and may be invalidated entirely by the environment’s migration towards Unsummon. Psychatog decks with lots of removal can slow down the beasts deck’s more methodical fatty-based and burn-free offense, setting up the Upheaval + Psychatog finish with no fear of Basking Rootwalla.

### Monoblack Control

Monoblack control fears r-g. Game one is up in the air and is not a blowout in either direction, despite monoblack’s seemingly inexhaustible palette of spot removal. We have found that r-g wins a lot of games by just aiming Volcanic Hammer to the opponent’s head in the early game… monoblack will typically not have a lot of other targets.

If this opponent’s other target, Visara the Dreadful (of which he will typically have only one), does come out, r-g is in the unique position among creature decks to laugh her off. A Grim Lavamancer and almost anything else will take out the monoblack deck’s entire basket of goods. If the opponent is running the Shvartsman/YMG version from the Chicago Masters with no maindeck Smothers, he will also have essentially no answer to Elephant Guide other than Duressing it from your hand.

The real reason monoblack fears r-g is in the sideboard. Compost wins the game. Period. Because of this, many players are predicting that monoblack control will be a no show, with no good way to beat Compost once it hits the board. At the Boston Open, many players simply didn’t run Compost in their sideboards because they thought that the fear of Compost was enough to keep black decks out of the tournament and were for the most part right. Especially given the evolution of Psychatog sideboards towards blue answers, this may be a reasonable direction.

Earlier, I mentioned that Volcanic Hammer would perform against opposing sideboard cards. Monoblack is a matchup where the opponent will try to play answers that don’t set off Compost. Eastern Paladin will be highly valued for its defensive strength, as well as the fact that unlike the spot removal spells, it doesn’t allow a Compost player to draw extra cards while killing creatures. In the face of Volcanic Hammer, Eastern Paladin is nothing more than an expensive target… that will nevertheless give up one of those extra cards.
