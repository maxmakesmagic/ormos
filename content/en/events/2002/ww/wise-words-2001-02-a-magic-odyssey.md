Now is the time for reflection.

World Championships is over, most of its participants returned home from Sydney (we miss you John O!) and with the completion of the tournament to end all tournaments comes also its annual twin, the end of the Pro Tour season.

2001-02 will be remembered. It will be known as the season where Kai hit triple digits in Pro Tour points. It will be remembered as the season in which Latin America emerged, much in the way France did. It saw Godzilla destroyed, YMG reborn, two Colonies brought forth to the world and even Australia got a Pro Tour. 

This season gave birth to more new names: Farid, Amiel, Cole, Nassif, Efro, Blume... ok, so they aren't all new. Still, they're part of what makes the game keep going, the characters that go beyond the black and white print of the computer page, and they're worth remembering for their successes, their failures and their love for **Magic**. Here then, ladies and gentlemen, is the Wise eye view of the 2001-02 season.

### In the beginning

![](https://i.ormos.uk/2c/41/2c4153462c0c1df20291d12d90c45aa7dce81d8eca8efb3aa575ad902e2425df.jpg)

As has become tradition of late, the new season got started with the Team Pro Tour. Defending champions Potato Nation were back, as were old favorites YMG and Antarctica, but the old school fell by the wayside, allowing for some new powerhouses to make themselves known. Kai Budde, fresh off a season where he'd won both constructed and limited Pro Tours and only just starting to be mentioned in the same sentences as Jon Finkel, recruited two old friends, PT Chicago '98 winner Dirk Baberowski and the larger than life Marco Blume to come sit with him in New York. Apparently, he made the right decision.

Phoenix Foundation, named for a McGuyver reference, stormed the field leaving all manner of gamer corpses in their wake. In the finals, the German team played out an age old rivalry, defeating French newcomers Gabriel Nassif, Nicolas Olivieri and Amiel Tenenbaum, a.k.a. Les Plus Class, just the beginnings of what would prove to be France's finest season to date on the Pro Tour. Tenenbaum's PT points vaulted him to the front of the Rookie of the Year race, while the Germans shared a three-way tie for Player of the Year. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/GPMOS01/907.jpg)

Missing from New York was Dave Williams. Riding a streak of play that had seen the Texan place better than ever in recent months, Williams's run came to an end in the quarterfinals of World Championships, where he was ejected and eventually suspended for having marked cards in his deck. The suspension, to which Williams originally stated he'd never play again, was for one year. Now, with Williams due back before Pro Tour-Boston, he's said to be ready to get back to slinging the spells. 

Riding the high of winning his fourth Pro Tour in total, not to mention having won eleven consecutive Sunday matches, Kai's next trip was to the **Magic: the Gathering** Invitational in Cape Town, South Africa. The first Premiere-level event to take place on African soil, this one didn't disappoint, with five formats - Duplicate Sealed, Auction of the People, Standard, Rotisserie Draft and Five Color - leaving just Budde and Dan Clegg standing. In the final, it was Kai once again, casting Tinker on turn two of the final game to go get Jewelled Bird, saving himself from what would be a turn three loss at the hands of Clegg's Hatred, winning the Invitational and the right to design his own card.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptnor01/a869.jpg)

Upon the completion of Kai's third Pro Tour victory format in less than a year, the Kai vs. Jon rumblings started getting stronger. Asked what the German Juggernaut had to do to leapfrog Jonny Magic, noted PT historian Randy Buehler replied 'win New Orleans'. As if the Juggernaut heard this, Kai steamrolled the competition in one of the most memorable Top 8 runs in recent memory. Playing blue-red Trix, Kai made a number of memorable topdecks against Darwin Kastle and Tomi Walamies, defeating the latter in the finals three games to two. Walamies's emergence, coupled with teammate Anton Jonsson's Top 8 appearance signalled the beginnings of a new PT cadre, trumpeting the arrival of Team Punisher. Also of note was the disqualification of Trey Van Cleave, whose attempt at bribery bought him a year on the DCI's suspended list.

Meanwhile, in the New Orleans Masters, Mike Pustilnik and Chris Benafel were making history. Facing one another in the final of a triple-Odyssey booster draft tournament, Pustilnik and his four Wild Mongrels ran Benafel over, making the little man from New York the first player in the game's history to win a Pro Tour, a Masters and a Grand Prix. With his loss in the final, as Chris pointed out, Benafel became the first player to lose in the finals of a Pro Tour, Masters and Grand Prix. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptsd02/a465.jpg)

The new year came and went, and the denizens of the Pro Tour made their was to sunny San Diego, a nice reprieve in the middle of winter, especially for one first-time pro from France. Playing more sanctioned matches in the previous year than anyone else in the game, Farid Meraghni may have even surprised himself a little with his victory, defeating Jens Thoren 3-2 in the triple-Odyssey final. The most memorable moment of the tournament came in Farid's Semifinal match against long-time bridesmaid Donnie Gallitz. Long considered to be among the best players without a Sunday appearance, Gallitz declared San Diego would be his last Pro Tour and finally broke through with the Top 8 he'd dreamed of over the years. Gallitz, never one to be accused of being shy, made the most of his time under the lights, even taking a lighter to Farid's Spellbane Centaur, the instrument of the American's demise. It was a great exit for one of the game's great characters.

In the Masters, another character was walking away with the big check. In the final of what would turn out to be the first of many tournaments dominated by the Psychatog archetype, Ryan Fuller defeated Dave Humpherys in the mirror match, walking away with $25,000 before being asked to leave the game for a year for multiple infractions that led to suspension. Joining Fuller on the suspended list was Bob Maher, whose part in a four-year-old ratings fix earned him six months of down time.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images%5CGPkyo00%5C863.jpg)

After San Diego came Osaka, the Pro Tour's third trip to Japan. A year earlier in Tokyo, Tsuyoshi Fujita had broken Japan's Top 8 drought, leaving most eyes on Japan's most famous ambassador of the game, and he almost duplicated his performance. Fujita rode the top tables for most of tournament, as did countryman Tsuyoshi Ikeda, but in the end, both players fell just short of the dream, coming fourteenth and thirteenth respectively. With Japan's hunt for more Top 8 glory ended, all eyes turned on Sunday to the foreign usurpers battling among themselves for victory. In the end, it was Ken Ho defeating Olivier Ruel in the final, effectively ending the Invitational curse. For three years running, the player who'd come last in the Invitational went on to win a Pro Tour in the following season, and with Olivier coming last in Cape Town, it looked like the streak might be extending itself when he took a 2-0 lead in the final at Osaka. Ho managed to fight his way back though, taking a Top 8 that had four Frenchmen in it.

Meanwhile, in the Masters, a familiar story was replaying itself for the Japanese masses. For the second year in a row, Japanese upstarts Panzer Hunters (Itaru Ishida, Reiji Ando, Kazuyuki Momose) found themselves in the finals and on the brink of victory only to see their western opponents stage a comeback. Even more familiar was Kai Budde leading the charge. Pro Tour winners Phoenix Foundation (Kai Budde, Dirk Baberowski, Marco Blume) became the first team to take both a Pro Tour and a Masters, let alone in the same season, despite being down one match and one game in each of the two other matches at one point. The win helped solidify Baberowski's status as one of the game's greats, and Budde's status as the greatest player the game has seen to date.

The last Pro Tour of the season took place in Nice, a fitting location in sight of the French success throughout the year. With the Pro Tour's finest gathered to booster draft Odyssey-Odyssey-Torment, it was defending European Champion Eivind Nitter who walked away with the gold, drafting red-green in a sea of black decks and defeating Bram Snapvengers in the final. While Bram didn't get the Pro Tour win, he did achieve a victory for the ages, becoming the first man to defeat Kai Budde in a Pro Tour Top 8, ending the German's 'Sunday streak' at fourteen matches.

While Goliath was being toppled in the main event, David was topping his assailants in the Masters. Qualifying through the gateway tournament, Dutchman Alex Witt defeated Alan Comer, Gerard Fabiano, Jens Thoren, Kai Budde and finally Justin Gary to win the event. Back to Kai once more, the Juggernaut finally ended his Masters drought in singles competition by reaching the Semifinals. He'd never advanced beyond the second round prior to Nice.

The end of the season's final Pro Tour signalled the beginning of the **Magic** year's most exciting time. The National Championship season had arrived, and the big names were thriving. Some of the more notable National Champions this year included Kai, Walamies, Anton Jonsson and a 12 year old kid from New Zealand named Cole Swannack, but the tournament to watch was U.S. Nationals, where last year's upstart Eugene Harvey became this year's superstar, defeating unknown Eric Franz in the final. Frightening for some and exciting for others was the re-emergence of Mike Long at what has historically been his best tournament. Long's results of late had fallen on hard times, but the Virginian isn't one of the game's greats for nothing, and he showed it by surviving until the Quarterfinals before being felled by Alex Borteh.

With the cancellation of both the APAC and Latin American Championships, Jin Okamoto and Scott Richards had both become eternal continental champions by default, but European bragging rights were still up for grabs. With the continent's stars converging on party town Amsterdam, it was little known David Brucker, who previously had only found success in native Germany, who emerged from the crowd victorious, playing a blue-black Psychatog deck without the staple Nightscape Familiars. Brucker defeated French National Team member Christophe Haim 3-1. The victory had to be bittersweet for the German though, as he could only watch between Sunday rounds as Germany lost the World Cup final to Brazil. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/worlds02/516.jpg)

With Euros completed, there was only one major tournament left uncontested on the season: World Championships. The Pro Tour made its way to a new continent, Australia, as Sydney played host to the tourney to end all tourneys. When the dust had settled on twenty-one rounds of **Magic**, Brazil's Carlos Romao, had made history, along with Diego Ostrovich becoming the first Latin American players to make a Pro Tour Sunday, and Romao took it a step further, defeating his Team Bandana team mate in the Semifinal en route to claiming the World Champion title for himself. In the final, Romao won the mirror match against German Mark Ziegner 3-2, but the loss was a bittersweet one for the German. Teamed with Felix Schneiders and Kai Budde, the latter had a sub-par tournament, forcing the superlative effort from Ziegner to get his country to the team competition finals, where they faced perennial favorite America. The Germans tried to end the American run in the final round of the Swiss, where they would have conceded if paired against Denmark if only to keep the U.S.A. out of the final, but the States completed a perfect day, beating the Germans and then winning the playoff against Denmark to advance to Sunday. Sunday, though, is Kai Budde's day and the Juggernaut did not disappoint, defeating U.S. champ Eugene Harvey to take the crown home to Germany, in the process making Justin Gary and the recently returned Bob Maher happy men. They, along with Kyle Bigos and Jeff Butz, were on the last U.S. team, circa 1997, to not win the teams competition.

While Kai had long before clinched his third Player of the Year crown, coming into Worlds, the Rookie of the Year race was still up for grabs. With Farid opting out of the trip to Sydney, there was one competitor, Amil Tenenbaum, who could still catch him without making it to the finals. Amiel needed to make Top 8 to tie his countryman, and when he defeated Raphael Levy in the final round of the Swiss, eighth place looked to be in reach, but it wasn't to be, with the comedic Frenchman edged out by Ken Krouner and Ziegner on tiebreakers. That left Farid Meraghni as your Rookie of the Year.

That, in a nutshell, was 2001-02. Latin America emerged, France thrived, Kai dominated, we met Farid, Amiel and Cole, were re-introduced to Marco and Dirk, said goodbye to Donnie and through it all, we kept on **Magic**-ing. May 2002-03 bring more of the same.

Have a good week.
