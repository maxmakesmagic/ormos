I feel like telling you “Welcome back.” If you caught my last [flavor article on *Guildpact*](/en/articles/feature/2006/02-13-the-italicized-world-of-guildpact), you're already my pal. If you didn't, no worries - there's no test at the end, and we can be pals anyway. I was one of the few, the brave, the flavor text writers for *Dissension,* the rest being:

*   Garrett Baumgartner - Formerly a temporary employee here at Wizards, Garrett cut his teeth in his first flavor text gig on *Dissension*.
*   John Carter - High-level judge, your Saturday School instructor and part-time wordsmith, John is no stranger to flavor.
*   Elye Alexander - A pal of Kamigawa Block flavor powerhouse Alex Smith, Elye picked up where Alex left off in *Guildpact*.
*   Jay Moldenhauer-Salazar - You may have heard of former Building on a Budget columnist, former Into the Aether columnist, former House of Cards columnist, and previous flavor text writer JMS.
*   Rei Nakazawa - Flavor mainstay Rei Nakazawa may have written more flavor text than all the other humans.
*   Jake Theis - As assistant brand manager for **Magic**, Jake strides the line between love for the game and knowledge of its business realities.
*   Matt Cavotta - Our commander-in-chief, the man behind the flavor curtain, Matt of course filled the gaps in our collective creativity with his own powers of flava. He was also responsible for revising our pieces, shaping them into sharp objects capable of wedging in the mind in the space of one glance.

Now that you know the crew, I want to let you in on some of the behind-the-scenes thought processes that went through one writer's head as he worked on this action-packed third chapter of the Ravnica Block.

### Colorful Personalities

I've got to get one thing off my chest first. The Ravnica Block made it easy on us, the flavor text writers. Flavor dripped off of every set from the get-go. Here's why, and it'll sound obvious:

[![Art from Ravnica, Guildpact and Dissension style guides](https://i.ormos.uk/b1/43/b1432f15c268fe81056abba0f27f59cf99db048f14532007b9fc7398d056a314.jpg "Art from Ravnica, Guildpact and Dissension style guides")](http://www.wizards.com/magic/images/mtgcom/fcpics/features/332_guildportrait_big.jpg)

Guilds.

Associating cultural groups with color pairs was one of the simplest, yet one of the most brilliant (if you ask me, and you *are* asking me because this is my article), ideas ever to grace the creative team. It was like the exact opposite of one of those embarrassing bad-dream moments where you're up on stage and you're holding a microphone and you're afraid to look down for fear that you forgot to wear pants and the spotlights swing around to center on you — and you had never learned the words to the song. I'm saying it was *not* like that. Instead, the creative team gets a stroke of inspiration, belts out a perfect rendition of a timeless classic, and electrifies the crowd.

What I'm saying is this: when it comes to flavor, the Ravnica Block is most assuredly wearing pants.

See, **Magic's** five colors are already champing at the bit to combine with one another. You're ten years old and you see Craw Wurm and you think, “Good, but better with Flight on him.” There's nothing better than harvesting those ripe fruits from their respective colors, blending them together and getting a refreshing deck smoothie out of it. Even during **Magic's** leaner mana-fixing times, tournaments and kitchen tables alike were full of awkward pairings of Capulets and Montagues—I'm looking at you, Wild Mongrel with Wonder in the grave. Then Ravnica Block came along and shed light on those awkward pairings. It gave them legitimacy. It gave them names.

And it gave them *personalities*.

I was in a meeting a long, long time ago—they called it 2004, if you can imagine a number so small—in which a bunch of people sat in a back room of the R&D games library in the old Wizards building, and talked about colors. **Magic** colors. We brainstormed what an organization based on each color combination would be like. Brady Dommermuth wrote down words and phrases he heard shouted out on an enormous whiteboard using a complex system of colored dry-erase markers, guiding (my fingers really wanted to type “guilding”) the discussion through each guild in turn. The personalities of these color-combination guilds felt surprisingly self-evident; it was as if these guilds had already plied their trades for thousands of years, and we were just discovering them. Even early on, it became natural to talk in terms of “Simic” or “Orzhov” rather than “Blue/Green” or “Black/White.” And now—two years later—here we are talking about the third set of the block.

I feel like a proud parent for my role in the Azorius-Rakdos-Simic chapter of the illustrious Ravnica Block. Let's crack open the leatherbound tome of memory and look at some of the individual cards I worked on. Along the way I'll let you in on more of those details that go into making **Magic** cards from my perspective. The first time the flavor text writer sees the cards, they're designed, concepted, and mostly developed, but still not-quite-congealed into a unique element of the Ravnica setting - and it's our job to take it the rest of the way.

### Dread Slag

Case in point. Toward the end of a **Magic** card's life cycle, a lot of crucial steps overlap. The flavor text writers are making their submissions at the same time as the artist is painting the final art, the development team is tweaking costs and balancing the card in its various environments, and editing is finalizing templates. There are times when you have to respect that the cards are potentially still in flux, and anticipate changes. Here was the art description for the card that became Dread Slag:

> *Show a chaotic, spreading mass that's an amalgam of phobias -- disembodied limbs, evil porcelain clowns, spiders, creepy old dolls, vipers, mouths agape in screams, you name it. This mass is spreading out like a giant, horrifying amoeba, like a conglomeration of all this world's nightmares.*

When the art description is this wide open, you've got a dilemma as a flavor text writer. What you're writing won't be able to key off of details in the art or card concept if there *are* no details. Anthony Waters's sketch was in, but we've seen how often an initial sketch can change on its journey to its final art, especially when the description is so open. Therefore I knew I wouldn't be referring to this monster's handily-specified “curling horns” or “dripping fangs” or “particularly jauntily-angled top hat” (which is good, because its top hat was removed in the final art).

So I went back to the art description. If I couldn't describe the creature for fear of stepping on the toes of some last-minute switcheroo, I could describe the concept, "an amalgam of phobias." What's the origin of such a creature? What role does it play in the Cult of Rakdos?

>  *A thousand phobias sopped from the city and wrung from Rix Maadi as one.*

I didn't come up with the name for this card, but I love the word “slag” here. “Slag” got used a few times in the *Mirrodin* block for its more literal meaning of the waste impurities derived from smelting iron. Its value here is metaphorical—this is the waste product of melting down the stuff of nightmare. Everyone's got phobias in Ravnica—ghosts, mindleeches, solifuges—and this guy has something for everyone. Imagine the pall of fear hovering over a typical Ravnican district—now imagine it sinking through the street, traveling via some dark osmosis to the lair of all fears, Rix Maadi. Finally imagine the Demon culling the essence of this heavy fog into a single creature, and then unleashing it back onto the streets above. *That's* some dread slag.

### Flame-Kin War Scout

>  *"The forward flame-kin are popping. We have incoming!"  
>  -Svar, Boros signaler*

![Flame-Kin War Scout](https://i.ormos.uk/d0/64/d064fff5bf4ab192f1879be1728b0a160e4de9d77b176fda601485ec8b764653.jpg "Flame-Kin War Scout")

The Izzet aren't the only guild who use elementals in their ranks. There's also the flame-kin, a humanoid species of fire elementals employed by the Boros to fight under Razia's banner of righteousness. There are several varieties of flame-kin, but this one, the War Scout, was mechanically sort of like a Seal of Flametongue Kavu. I imagined an impassioned-but-structured battalion of Boros troops with a perimeter of these Flame-Kin War Scouts. What would happen when the army met enemy resistance? The War Scouts would throw themselves into the enemy's ranks, charring them (you know, four damage?) but also blasting themselves apart, resolving into a disorganized pop of heat and light.

That, I thought, would make a great signal to the rest of the army. Imagine being a typical human Boros soldier trying to see the front lines from the middle ranks. You've got an Oathsworn Giant marching just to your right and a couple of Ordruun Minotaurs ahead of you. You can't see the Golgari heathens, but you can definitely hear the Flame-Kin War Scouts erupting in sudden zeal at the heathens' approach. Thank Razia for those crazy flame-kin (as long as they stay way out there, on the front lines)!

### Grand Arbiter Augustin IV

It's always exciting to write for a card with both storyline import and powerful abilities; Augustin IV is the Azorius guild leader, and while I hadn't played the card before, the drama and scope of its abilities were obvious. Happily, those abilities also dripped with flavor—as any proper Azorius leader should, Augustin sets down harsh rules amendments as soon as he shows up. He doesn't just come into play, he *takes office*.

To my mind, his flavor text had to express not just how cool he was, but how *Azorius* he was. This being represents the pinnacle of order under the governance of wisdom. What does that do to a man's personality, to his mind? In some ways, I thought, personality was the *last* thing he could afford to have. Some judges rule half by gut feeling, letting their personal values motivate and inform their rulings. These are colorful characters, but they're not Azorius characters. Augustin would not abide any personal bias sneaking into his judgments—and neither would other high-ranking senators around him. In a guild driven solely by law and order, the only way he could keep his job would be to dissolve away almost everything that makes him human. He's more like a window than a person—he's a transparent conduit connecting the perfect justice of the law-books to the living world.

>  *The Arbiter is a conduit of justice, a will so disciplined that it dispenses justice without ego or remorse.*

Of course, in the timeline of *Dissension*, the living world of Ravnica gets so messy that our perfect conduit of justice goes a little bonkers. It happens.

### Haazda Exonerator

Haazda Exonerator is a functional reprint of Devout Harpist, except with modern templating and Ravnica Block flavor. Thinking about what this card did made me think about what it's like to be a creature sacrificed for the purposes of removing, say, a Pillory of the Sleepless aura. How would you like to be told by your planeswalker master to sacrifice your life just to Disenchant something? Even in Ravnica, where spirits often linger after death, that'd be a high price to pay, and this creature does it willingly. Furthermore, creatures like Nikko-Onna or Nantuko Vigilante can destroy an aura without giving their lives. Why would the process be lethal in some cases?

Yes, I really spent this much time thinking about the daily life of Devout Harpist.

I got inspired when I thought about how powerful Ravnica Block's auras are. These auras create saprolings, drain away life or electrocute someone when they enchant something. The answer is clear—aura magic is just more potent in Ravnica! Think what it must be like to have Faith's Fetters clamp down on you: this is no pleasant, pacifistic feeling; it's a full-scale shutdown of your ability to think and move. Faith-inspired or no, this is enchantment magic that sinks its roots deep in you. So it's not surprising it takes nerves of steel—and perhaps the willingness to risk death—for heroes like this simple, guildless cleric to wrench it free.

>  *"This aura threatens the sanctity of your soul. Wrenching it free won't be easy on either of us. If you're ready, we'll begin."*

### Riot Spikes

![Riot Spikes](https://i.ormos.uk/1a/2e/1a2ee1ee2ec7272b7a16810451cb6b4d2ec24556d5111d1f2b4f7e3b0ba4bc34.jpg "Riot Spikes")

Speaking of auras, all the guilds have aura magic, but each one develops aura spells differently. Riot Spikes is a Rakdos spell, so this aura had to support a destructive game-plan (+2/+0, check) while reveling in the pain caused in the process (+0/-1, check). Really, this spell was Rakdos-flavored from the get-go, even before creative text was added!

The spell was already concepted as magical spurs or spikes jutting out of the enchanted creature, giving it the ability to do additional damage at the cost of a little pain. So my job was to explore that idea further, and if possible, to point out the dual nature of the spell—either to boost up your 2-toughness flyer to end the game in a hurry, or to pick off wussy Centaur Safeguards or Gelectrodes with the toughness-reducing effect. I liked thinking about the latter scenario in flavor terms. Rakdos auramancers know this macho pump spell that hurts almost as much as it augments its target. So they sometimes cast it on their enemies, laughing in derision, knowing the weaklings won't be able to enjoy the benefits of their newfound spikes.

The detail that Rakdos himself was the one who provided the spell's skin-ripping vector was just for fun—flavor gravy.

>  *Most auramancers would have let the spikes hover just above the skin. Having the spikes rip through the skin from beneath was a touch added by Rakdos himself.*

### Valor Made Real

>  *“As my father taught, 'Training will raise your shield to the blow, but courage fills the gaps the shield leaves open.'"*

Frankly I have no idea where I got this one. I must have channeled some Azorius soldier reminiscing about his father's propensity for pithy sayings about blocking multiple creatures. It's like when you read a tournament report, and the author is like, “I don't remember at all what happened in this round, but my deck must have come through for me, and I won 2-0.” Sometimes you're “in the zone” and everything works out. I guess I was an egoless conduit of *flava* that day.

### Vesper Ghoul

![Vesper Ghoul](https://i.ormos.uk/e0/aa/e0aa156ded2bd0c37b9b74a66ea1cf482d1b9efb01e35d9306b89a993b1f4063.jpg "Vesper Ghoul")

Sometimes the weirdest, most out-of-flavor cards give you the most creative ammunition. When a card is a conundrum, it means you have a mission, a problem to solve. Vesper Ghoul is certainly a head-scratcher—why does this zombie have a mana ability? And “so that black could have more color fixers for Limited” is not a legal answer here!

Well, a mana ability is definitely an anomaly in the undead world, and the card would feel incomplete without the flavor text at least mentioning—and at best explaining—that ability. At first I thought of quoting some up-in-arms green character, a shaman or something, who called the Ghoul an abomination. It's unnatural, this shaman would think, for something dead to be in touch with the natural mana energy of the land.

Then I thought, wouldn't someone truly in tune with nature's teachings see the Ghoul as evidence *for* the glory of Nature? Nature is full of bizarre creatures, each of whom has adapted to the peculiar circumstances of its environment—perhaps the Ghoul was just another finch with a beak adapted to the seeds it ate, just another being with abilities it needed to cope in its environment. In the extreme afterlife circumstances of Ravnica, the undead do weirder things than tap for ![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif)….

>  *"Nature's exceptions remind us not to think in absolutes. Just as the orchid blossoms on blackened stone, so does the gift of mana occasionally manifest in the undead."  
>  -Bougrat, druid of the Cult of Yore*

It was the fabulous Matt Cavotta, flavor text overseer extraordinaire, who attributed my quote to a druid of the Cult of Yore. That cult was a preexisting group of druids dedicated to happenings in Ravnica's natural world (see Earth Surge), and so it was a great match.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/12/95/129592c6552447095856a22cdc2cca4e5b42d7753009ed789956fcdbaa85fb1d.jpg)

### Proud Parents

All of us who worked on the Ravnica Block were like proud home-schooling parents, knowing this kid of ours was going to grow up to be a great success, but having to contain our excitement and keep the kid out of the public eye until it was of age. Now all the cards have released, and I'm happy as a pig in slop. I might tell you to take a moment to go read those italicized words hanging out at the bottom of the text box, but I don't think I need to. I think you're already inspired to read a bit more about Ravnica without my telling you. You look at a green-blue spell and you wonder what that old Momir Vig was thinking that day when he cooked up such a thing. Or maybe you do something as simple as put Flight on Craw Wurm, and think, “Heh heh, flying Craw Wurm.” That's good—that enjoyment pulls you into the world of **Magic**, pulls you into the same pride its creators feel, and makes you a little bit of Ravnica's proud parent, too.
