The tradition of big creatures with drawbacks goes back a long way in **Magic**. Perhaps the earliest examples are from *Arabian Nights*, where the Djinns and Efreets were first introduced. **Magic** players were given Serendib Efreet and Juzam Djinn, both of which are quite large, one being a 3/4 flier for ![2 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) and the other being a 5/5 ground creature for ![2 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif). Their drawbacks were minor ones: they each deal a point of damage to the controllers during upkeep. Sure, sometimes a player would die to their own Juzam Djinn, but pound for pound, these guys were worth it. Along with the Serendib Efreet came the Ernham Djinn, a popular character who is still around today. At 4/5 for ![3 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/7f/5d/7f5d2f1e55eaa9d19a9a23027b63bfbb5b39dbf8642160dda1a477a03df23a6a.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif), he was once considered quite a big creature, his only drawback being that he would give one of the opponent’s creatures forestwalk until the next upkeep. Now, though, a 4/5 for ![3 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/7f/5d/7f5d2f1e55eaa9d19a9a23027b63bfbb5b39dbf8642160dda1a477a03df23a6a.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif) isn’t quite what it used to be; green regularly sees 3/3s for three and 4/4s for four, with special abilities on top of it.

At first, there weren’t many red creatures that were made in the image of the Djinns and Efreets. They attempted to make a good one in *Arabian Nights*, but the Ydwen Efreet paled in comparison to the others. Still, the Ydwen Efreet wasn’t a bad attempt. He was a 3/6 for ![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif) that could attack just fine, but when he wanted to block, you had to flip a coin and half the time he just couldn’t do it. His biggest drawback was that because of his casting cost, he could really only be used in monored decks. Even then, Ydwen Efreet just wasn’t that big and he didn’t have evasion like the Serendib Efreet. For the time being, anyway, the overpowered creatures were reserved for blue, black, and green. That is, until *Fallen Empires* rolled around. As much as people complain about the quality of the cards in *Fallen Empires*, the set contains one of my favorite creatures: Orgg. For ![3 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/7f/5d/7f5d2f1e55eaa9d19a9a23027b63bfbb5b39dbf8642160dda1a477a03df23a6a.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif), Orgg is a 6/6 creature who doesn’t realize quite how big he is. His drawback is that he can’t attack if the defending player controls an untapped creature of power greater than 2 and he can’t block a creature with power greater than 2. Still, in a deck with a lot of removal or against a deck without many creatures, Orgg is a great value. Orgg is even better if you can find a way to get him out quicker than turn five; people would often use Mana Vault in decks with Orgg to allow for a turn two 6/6 monster and also to help pump up Fireballs and other X spells later in the game.

What does all this have to do with *Legions*? If you thought the Orgg was good, wait until you see the Goblin Goon, a new creature from *Legions*.

Take a look at these u-g madness and threshold decks and look at the hoops that they jump through just to get a 6/6 creature for four mana. They have to discard Roar of the Wurm to Wild Mongrel or Merfolk Looter or Careful Study to get it in the graveyard and then cast it from there. Sometimes though, when they don’t draw enough spells that allow them to discard, they get stuck with this card in their hand that is useless until they reach seven mana.

Now think about the Goblin Goon.

No jumping through hoops. He is just a 6/6 for four mana. Not bad, when you think about it. Certainly, Goblin Goon has a drawback. He needs a lot of buddies around. But if you put Goblin Goon in the right deck, packed with other aggressive creatures and removal, the drawback shouldn’t be too much of a problem.

It’s not clear quite where Goblin Goon will fit into the Standard tournament environment. Is the Goblin Goon better than Blistering Firecat in monored? Can you afford to run two expensive creatures in the deck? Perhaps he is better suited to an aggressive red-green deck with Llanowar Elves and Birds of Paradise, with those quick mana producing creatures making it more likely that the deck will have more creatures than the opponent and also make it so the Goblin Goon can be cast one turn earlier. It is hard to complain about a turn three 6/6 creature.

Where Goblin Goon is going to really shine, however, is in Extended. Mana Vault isn’t in the format, sure, but there is another way to get a turn two Goblin Goon: Goblin Lackey. There are already competitive red beatdown decks that use the Goblin Lackey, to get an extra Goblin Piledriver or Mogg Flunky into play on that critical second turn. The deck is full of goblins, Jackal Pups, and burn spells, so the Goblin Goon will fit right in. Sure, when you don’t draw a Goblin Lackey, you are going to have to hard cast that Goblin Goon, but once again, paying ![3 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/7f/5d/7f5d2f1e55eaa9d19a9a23027b63bfbb5b39dbf8642160dda1a477a03df23a6a.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif) for a 6/6 goblin isn’t so bad. Goblin Goon will allow the monored goblin deck to have insanely explosive draws. Just imagine, first turn mountain and Goblin Lackey. Second turn, play another mountain, attack with the Goblin Lackey for one, putting a Goblin Goon into play, and then tapping the two mountains to cast something, say a Mogg Flunky. What deck out there can handle ten power worth of creatures staring them down on the second turn? Not many, I’d bet.

I don’t know about you, but I’m going to dust off my Goblin Lackeys and give them another try as soon as I can get my hands on some Goblin Goons. I hope that you all enjoy beating down with the Goblin Goon as much as I will.
