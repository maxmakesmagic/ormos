That title says it all, doesn't it? Wherever we are, whatever we do, **Magic's** calendar seems to affect our lives about as much as, well, our lives do. Last week I mentioned that my time in England had ended abruptly, with my return from Boston being met at the English customs with the news that I'd be sent back to the States. This week's column is an attempt at saying goodbye to those I haven't had a chance to do so with, and the start of our cumulative attempts to figure out *Onslaught* Limited.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>Sorry folks, I am not the drug runner you are looking for.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

England was good to me, from the day I arrived there (September 20th, 2001, eight days postponed from my original scheduled arrival date) to the day I got turned away at the border (September 27th, 2002). For the record, because I was planning on leaving in November, I had abandoned my attempts to get a visa, and that as much as anything led to the government's fears that I might try to live in country indefinitely without permission to do so. Sorry folks, I am not the drug runner you are looking for.

In my year in England, as I observed a new and completely different Magic community from the one I'd grown up with in Toronto, I found there was a lot of good to learn from. Leading the good was the county's knack for finding just the right combination of sixty cards to outdo just about everyone else in the world. 

For years, John Ormerod and Ben Ronaldson, with help from Warren Marsh, Richard Edbury, Dan Paskins and the like, have been building and breaking decks in all manner of sixty card environments. From Trinity Green to RDW2K to Coco Pebbles to Deep Dog to Pirates! to the Wake deck built and played by Sam Gomersoll in London, decks originally built by these fine gents have been making their way to the top of Constructed fields for years, and 373 days in England gave me a glimpse into the techniques that have carried that group so far. Here's the thing, fair reader: while a certain amount of talent must be required in order to build great decks, the techniques these guys employ can be successfully utilized by anyone.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>It all starts with brainstorming…</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

It all starts with brainstorming, with Ormerod sitting at his work terminal or Ronaldson at his desk at Royal Tennis Court reception, utilizing the time on their hands by constantly turning ideas over in their heads. Having watched Ben so closely, I can honestly say that for him, I think the attempt to break a format is a lot more interesting than actually playing it out afterwards. Solving the puzzle is the game to him.

Once those brainstorms have formatted themselves into decklists, they're shared with the collective, oft-times being rejected or ignored after brief surveying, but occasionally, people take notice and make suggestions that the originator may not have thought of in the first place. This kind of banter seems crucial to me; one mind is capable of a lot, but it's very easy for one person to overlook the same crucial piece of information continually.

Once everyone's ideas have been assimilated, it's time to test. And test. And test. These guys are relentless, playing game after game, regardless of whether there's an opponent to be found. I find playing against myself to be very difficult, with personal biases interfering with results, but that's probably because I tried doing it so late in the game. For the English though, it seems almost effortless, with each game showing them new things and bringing with the result an understanding of what needs to be changed in accounting for the environment. This is especially important when addressing mirror matches, an English specialty. These were the only guys with Defender en-Vec in their 2000 PT-NY Rebel deck sideboards. It showed in the results.

In addition to the deck building, there's a certain familiarity within the community that North Americans could learn from as far as gamesmanship goes. In North America, we tend to be combative and secretive, regarding those who aren't with us as being against us. In England though, decks and ideas are shared, people ask and are answered openly about their deck choices for upcoming PTQs, and there seems to be a genuine feeling shared by all that England must be well-represented on the world stage. 

The English unfortunately haven't quite come to grips with the fact that in competitive **Magic** there are formats with less than sixty cards (I only played in one draft that didn't take place at the palace, and including transportation costs, that one cost me $25), and the lack of a community base in London has left the competitive players in the role of nomads instead of community leaders, so not everything is perfect, but there are countries, let alone players, where dreams of deck-building fame go unfulfilled. The English are almost bored of building the newest and greatest. For the rest of us, here's hoping they stick to sixty cards a little longer.

### *Onslaught* Thoughts

I had the opportunity to draft a little with *Onslaught* during our gathering in Boston and a little more this past weekend. Here are a few findings thus far:

### The Importance of Being Tribal

Heading into Boston, the white portion of my *Onslaught* Limited review was all but completed when Randy Buehler told me it would be more suitable to do an eight-article series instead of the usual six- or seven-. Having drafted the set a couple of times now, I've gone back and restructured the whole thing. The first installment should be up this week.

The reason for Randy's advice is pretty simple: tribal dominates. At first, I must admit I was a little worried about the power level of some of the bombs in the set, but I've come to understand that in part, the reason they're so strong is because they have to be: Tribal effects have raised the power bar to a certain level. As a result, regular old bombs would hardly make a dent in tribal hides, and that means the megaton variety was called for.

Yes, the bombs introduce a somewhat random element into *Onslaught* Limited, but what **Magic** format has ever been without that luck factor? The best way to combat those bombs is knowledge of the environment: knowing the cards and knowing how best to draft a deck to be as consistently strong as possible. That way, by the time the bomb hits the table, perhaps you'll have enough control of the table that the bomb won't matter.

The elf deck can get into the four digit area in life total. Good luck with your Rorix. The goblin and zombie decks are both capable of killing on turn five or six. Hope your 8cc creature did a lot in your hand. I'm not saying that bombs won't cause you problems as long as you stick to tribal lines, but doing so will definitely make them easier to cope with.

The extent to which you should be going tribal is almost ludicrous. To quote Mike Donais "You won't be drafting red-white, you'll be drafting goblin-soldier", and done properly, he's absolutely right. I'm not saying I won't be picking Exalted Angel for my soldier deck, I've preached before about how there are exceptions to every rule when it comes to limited play, but more often than not, actually, almost every time, the tribal pick will be the right one.

### Go Hard or Go Home

I still remember the first deck I ever built. It was a red-white monster, with twenty lands and forty creatures, each of which cost four five or more mana, and each of which compensated the cost with power and toughness to spare. The deck was unbeatable. The deck was unstoppable. The deck was all-powerful.

All that and more. Until I played my first game with it.

I remember being challenged by this girl in my residence named Danielle, who promptly played a Forest, cast Thallid (I've been playing this game for a long time) and passed the turn. I had no chance. The lesson I learned that day was that for a deck to prove viable, in any format, it must be able to do something in the early game: if it doesn't, your opponent's early-game-capable deck will run you over. Get ready to learn that all again for the next year.

*Onslaught* offers you all manner of temptation. Huge power, ridiculous toughness, insane creature abilities… all at the low, low cost of a lot of color specific mana and high casting costs all around. First you take your Visara… then your get your Butcher Orgg. That forces you to pass the other high costs bombs in blue-green and white, but then you find yourself looking at more and more six and seven casting cost cards. Then your opponent plays first turn elf, second turn elf, elf. Thanks for the game. See my point?

The key here is to fight temptation and learn your tribes. Know what makes each deck tick, which tribes work well together and which sacrifices to make for your mana curve. It's more important than ever, in an environment where people will be playing too many high cost cards, that you optimize your mana.

Mana optimization is a term that been thrown around by pros for a long time but which really hasn't gotten its due as far as Limited analysis goes. If you've been reading this column for a while, you're probably familiar with the mana curve, but it's possible you don't know what makes it important. Mana Optimization. The idea is that your deck will prove the better functioning of the two in your game if you use more mana than your opponent. The best way to do this is by drafting a mana curve, that is, creatures to fill each casting cost slot. Generally, the ideal draft deck, assuming fifteen creature cards, has always looked like this:

That's why more than ever, you have to fight the urge to draft the big creatures.

There is a way around this. Draft those fatties who come with morph. Morph, which some pros are already calling the best creature ability ever, brings with it mankind's greatest fear: the unknown. Racism, ageism… any ism you can find, more often than not can be traced to the fear of the unknown, and that's embodied in these obnoxious flipped over creature cards that could say '1/1 protection from Goblins' or '4/5, flying, Gain life whenever this absolutely insane card deals damage'.

Morph allows you to play the fatties while maintaining some semblance of a mana curve, offering you a 2/2 creature whose unknown qualities will more often than not make it unblockable, leaving you the option of flipping that card over when the time is right, that time being much, much later. When you draft your Treespring Lorians, your Venomspout Brackuses and your Towering Baloths, be sure to count them as threes in your mana curve, and play them that way. Holding your breath for lands number six, seven and eight will leave you wondering what could have, and probably should have been.

### A Few Favorites

Here's a short list of the cards I've found surprisingly good thus far:

**Oblation** - I just love this card for all the tricky little things it does: return your best card to your deck when your opponent's about to remove it - or - Your opponent has that one blocker standing between you and victory; let them draw, they'll be dead. The stack tricks you can do here are great, and in times of desperation, you can use it as a pure card drawer. Not a first pick, but a wonderful utility.
