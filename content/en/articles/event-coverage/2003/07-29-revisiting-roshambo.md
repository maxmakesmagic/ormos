### It Wasn't Easy Being Green

About three years ago, the Masques Block Constructed Pro Tour-New York boasted quite a diverse field. There were rebel beatdown decks with all the two-drops, rebel control decks with Blinding Angels and other flying creatures, rebel combination decks that played a long game of Distorting Lens in concert with Lightbringer, g-w rebel decks with Blastoderm and Saproling Burst, and even b-w rebel decks that "played the metagame", somehow predicting that they would hit other decks with plains in play in order to break the mana cost on Massacre. There was also a very small minority of blue decks with Rising Waters, but those were few and far between in the main draw, and ended up making up only about 25% of the 2000 PT-New York Top 8.

Now despite the many different decks that showed up at that year's PT-New York, some players, particularly a roguish group radiating out of Madison, Wisconsin, decided that they would run and gun for the expected plains and small creatures. These individuals, including Pro Tour Champion Bob Maher and Grand Prix Champion Sol Malka, took this monstrosity to the big game:

As was his custom with off-center green decks in those days, the best finish for Roshambo was a 9th place for Squee-Bind innovator Mike Hron.

Roshambo was chosen by these players as an aggressive metagame decision for a specific field. It contained several cards that were, in and of themselves, quite strong against small white creatures: Thrashing Wumpus, Ascendent Evincar, 

Forced March

, and especially Massacre could win the game all by themselves when played against decks whose plans revolved around Ramosian Sergeant and Steadfast Guard.

> *"The deck never lost to rebels. How many rebel decks did I play against at PT - Rebels? That's right, none. If I had just played a rebel deck in round one, I would have been in the winner's bracket, playing against rebel decks every round, and it would have been me in 9th place instead."* -John Shuler

Of course, even more than the singularly powerful black cards, Roshambo contained a powerful proactive combination that would be of use against non-rebel decks as well: With Death Pit Offering in play, Natural Affinity + any of the sweeper spells would essentially Jokulhaups the opponent's board while leaving the Roshambo player's lands intact. While pulling this off was especially easy against a rebel deck with no permission (remember Massacre was free), the theory was that the Armageddon effect would be able to overwhelm thusly any opponent.

That was the theory anyway.

> *"The reason Mike did so well was that he was the only one who figured out to sideboard out his artifacts and enchantments in games two and three. The rebel decks could bring in Disenchant and Seal of Cleansing to fight cards like Death Pit Offering, and then our deck wouldn't necessarily beat them anymore."* -Bob Maher

Though Roshambo did include five parts of Ramos for artifact-based mana acceleration, which gave it some play against a resolved Rising Waters, the deck's almost total lack of early game disruption and expensive threat base made it a poor choice against Counterspell decks like those that Sigurd Eskeland and Mike Bregoli brought to the Top 8. As blue became increasingly popular in the PTQ season, with free counters and aggressive flyers both readily available, Roshambo became less and less a viable choice, eventually giving way to Joel Priest's Snuff-O-Derm descendent.

It is quite possible, therefore, that you have never heard of it.

Which would mean, of course, that you might not notice that Roshambo's unique elements, Death Pit Offering and 

Natural Affinity

, are both returning to Standard play in *Eighth Edition*.

### Roshambo Returns?

While modern Standard is not going to be quite as generous in terms of synergistic threats (there is today no Saproling Burst, nor is there about to be a 

Chimeric Idol

, as there were in Masques Block Constructed), the environmental elements that made Roshambo a powerful choice in the first place seem to be reappearing, while those that drove it away all seem to be leaving the Standard environment.

Much ado has been made of the return of Savannah Lions in *Eighth Edition*. Along with Onslaught Block creatures like Deftblade Elite and Silver Knight, Savannah Lions may help return white weenie return to its days of bygone glory when wielded by such Magicians as Randy Buehler, Matt Linde, Brian Hacker, and Kyle Rose. That being said, this new incarnation of White Weenie will have no access to Disenchant, and as good as its components are, not one of these creatures measures up to Ramosian Sergeant in the dangerous department.

With the drastic changes being made to the Core Set, tribal weenie decks like goblins should receive similar increases in popularity. While good old goblins will probably have the same scariest matchup as a prospective Roshambo deck in post-*Eighth Edition* Standard play (the r-g deck packing Wild Mongrel, Elephant Guide, and Phantom Centaur), these small creatures will nevertheless be greatly incentivized by both Onslaught Block and the removal of Engineered Plague.

I am aware that Roshambo is essentially a combination deck, and that Diabolic Tutor is available both in *Odyssey* and *Eighth Edition*. But because I expect a fast format with Eighth Edition, cycling into appropriate response cards seems like a better plan than tapping four mana at sorcery speed when I might need to at least bluff a Smother. At the same time, the natural synergies between cycling creatures, Skeletal Scrying, and Unholy Grotto - while still plowing through the deck to assemble the key elements of the combination - should be obvious.

An even bigger gaping hole in the four mana black sorcery department should be Mutilate. That card is not present for two reasons. First of all, this deck has only 10 swamps, rather than the 22-24 that you might see in a typical deck with Mutilate. Secondly, Mutilate's high end is too difficult to control. In a typical deck, that might mean being forced into killing one's own Visara the Dreadful. In this deck, it can mean blowing up all of your lands by accident. At the same time, Decree of Pain is available. It makes quite a good Infest at five mana, meaning that it can play natural combination piece at eight. In a long game, it can counter a Decree of Justice, or draw a ton of cards. While a little unwieldy, Decree of Pain serves here as a supplement to the rest of the deck's array of creature elimination.

The core combination, therefore, is Infest + Natural Affinity, ideally with a 

Death Pit Offering

in play. With all the creature removal there to buy you time, this three card combination is surprisingly not difficult to assemble. Infest is both better and worse than Massacre. While it is clearly weaker against decks with plains, Infest is less expensive in all other situations. The combination can be made even cheaper by adding red to the deck for Pyroclasm. In that version, I would probably say goodbye to Decree of Pain and totally rework the mana base into something only Brian Kibler could love.

### The Second Dumbest Mirror Match Ever

Next to mana screw, mana flood, and could have/should have/would have, some of the most hackneyed complaints made by **Magic** players are those about matchups. Bad matchups come first, but seemingly random mirror matches are up there as well. After all, who hasn't scowled as the opponent drew Wonder and Wild Mongrel while sitting on forest, forest, Centaur Garden? While the previous worst mirror match ever probably has to go to five-color green circa 1997 when one of two sideboarded Simoons could wipe out the opponent's entire team and mana base both for an economical ![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif)![Red Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/86/2f/862ffc3d21299d18f0315a3f77721a10737fb394e147b0987204ef0496ce1060.gif), the runner up has to be this archetype. Especially with no main deck disruption of any sort, watch out when playing Death Pit Offering. The other guy might just tap three mana for a Natural Affinity.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>Can the build be improved?</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Is Roshambo going to be a tier one deck? Maybe not. For all its natural strength against weenie hordes, it is going to have to play a sly game against the popular r-g deck; it has little natural resistance to Phantom Centaur and no life-gain main deck. But that doesn't mean that it can't be a solid tier two deck, the right choice for a specific field, and a tournament winner at that. This deck has a lot of play against a large wedge of the expected post-*Eighth Edition* opposition, and a devastating combination built in. It can play one-for-one in the early game, and then use its graveyard to recoup; it can quickly switch into beatdown mode and attack with a lethal horde of 4/4 or even 6/6 lands. It can play bad black control against a u-g opponent, and if there's one thing I've learned, it's that even bad black control looks pretty good with a Gorgon Legend in play.

Perhaps a different build of the same core engine is the right one. The boys from Pro Tour-Rebels had Divining Witch to call up a missing combination piece while busy blocking a Blastoderm. Later builds had more threats that could weather a Death Pit Offering (especially Chimeric Idol). This deck may be too centered on conventional black creature removal without even playing its best of breed. As good as it should be against small creatures, the deck probably will have some issues with the zombie tribe. As good as cycling is in theory, it's not nearly as attractive when the other guy has a Lightning Rift in play. Can the build be improved? You bet. After all, the format is so young, it's yet to be born. Just don't ignore the little combination you've never heard of from Masques Block Constructed before even realizing it's there.
