<table>&#10;  <tbody><tr>&#10;    <td>&#10;      <table>&#10;        <tbody><tr>&#10;          <td>&#10;                 <b>August 22, 2008 – Magic Rules Corner</b></td>&#10;          <td> </td>&#10;          <td>&#10;            <img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c7/c1/c7c1fe5cf49f58117fbd931561f1b60bd3183ad6276cf758a8fd3c7d6702fe27.jpg">&#10;          </td>&#10;        </tr>&#10;        <tr>&#10;          <td colspan="3">&#10;            <table>&#10;              <tbody><tr>&#10;                <td>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>Q: </b>My friends and I are wondering, when <a>Faceless Butcher</a> comes into play and targets an <a>Avatar of Woe</a>, can the Avatar's ability be activated in response and destroy the <a>Butcher</a>?<br> –Oscar, Guatemala City, Guatemala</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>A: </b>From the <b>Magic</b> Rules Corner:</p>&#10;                  <p>Yes, it can... but it isn't going to do what you probably want.</p>&#10;                  <p>Let's say Player A controls an unsuspecting <a>Avatar of Woe</a> (and it's not tapped, summoning sick, <a>Arrest</a>ed, <a>Snakeform</a>ed, etc.).</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    </p><div>&#10;                      <div>&#10;                        <a>&#10;                          <img alt="Avatar of Woe" src="https://i.ormos.uk/3b/14/3b1474bb780cd45e89f32f206f41ddfbf7cf53e22d9a99acf5a89bdf87398cf6.jpg">&#10;                        </a>&#10;                      </div>&#10;                    </div>&#10;                  <p></p>&#10;                  <p>Player B plays <a>Faceless Butcher</a>, and nobody has any responses, so <a>Faceless Butcher</a> resolves (as a creature resolves, it's put into play). Its controller puts its &quot;comes into play&quot; ability on the stack, choosing <a>Avatar of Woe</a> as the target.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    </p><div>&#10;                      <div>&#10;                        <a>&#10;                          <img alt="Faceless Butcher" src="https://i.ormos.uk/87/ff/87ff642542a46397f4181248b7dee298f0e26fad3ea7445ab3c5275559fa3b9a.jpg">&#10;                        </a>&#10;                      </div>&#10;                    </div>&#10;                  <p></p>&#10;                  <p>Now, each player has a chance to respond before the ability resolves. Player A decides to tap <a>Avatar of Woe</a> to play its activated ability, choosing <a>Faceless Butcher</a> as the target. That ability goes on the stack on top of <a>Faceless Butcher</a>'s first triggered ability, so it will resolve first.</p>&#10;                  <p>Everyone passes, so the top item of the stack—<a>Avatar of Woe</a>'s activated ability—resolves, destroying <a>Faceless Butcher</a>. This triggers the <a>Faceless Butcher</a>'s second, &quot;leaves play&quot; triggered ability, so its controller puts that ability on the stack.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    </p><div>&#10;                      <div>&#10;                        <a>&#10;                          <img alt="Living Wish" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c8/5d/c85d92de3f50e5d775e89ebb03ba1d63b87b6d2c5219406bf9a8ad9f79d4a5fb.jpg">&#10;                        </a>&#10;                      </div>&#10;                    </div>Abilities on the stack exist independently of their sources, so even though <a>Faceless Butcher</a> has been destroyed, its first triggered ability is still on the stack, now with its second triggered ability on top of the first.<p></p>&#10;                  <p>Everyone passes again, so the second triggered ability resolves and tries to &quot;return the removed card to play.&quot; But with the first triggered ability still on the stack, there isn't any removed card, so the ability does as much as it can—which is nothing—and finishes resolving.</p>&#10;                  <p>The first triggered ability is <i>still</i> on the stack (seems like it's been there forever, doesn't it?), but it's finally at the front of the line. Everyone passes again, so that first trigger resolves and removes <a>Avatar of Woe</a> from the game.</p>&#10;                  <p>Here's the thing, though. Because <a>Faceless Butcher</a>'s &quot;leaves play&quot; ability has already resolved, that Avatar isn't coming back from the removed-from-the-game zone unless a card like <a>Living Wish</a> or <a>Riftsweeper</a> says so.</p>&#10;                  <p>Some decks pair <a>Faceless Butcher</a> with sacrifice outlets to pull this trick, and there's a similar combo involving <a>Oblivion Ring</a> and <a>Vedalken Mastermind</a>.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <i>The <b>Magic</b> Rules Corner is a weekly feature dedicated to answering your rules questions. For more help with <b>Magic</b> rules, check out the <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20090225235831/http://www.wizards.com/Magic/TCG/Article.aspx?x=magic/rules">rules page</a> and the <a href="http://forums.gleemax.com/forumdisplay.php?s=&amp;amp;forumid=154">Rules Q&amp;amp;A Forum</a>.</i>&#10;                  </p>&#10;                </td>&#10;              </tr>&#10;            </tbody></table>&#10;          </td>&#10;        </tr>&#10;      </tbody></table>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

That's the short answer. :)</p>&#10;                  <p>Longer answer:</p>&#10;                  <p>I start mentally putting a &quot;stable&quot; of illustrators together as soon as the setting and world design starts to congeal. Just as we look at the card file to see what the setting &quot;'wants to be,&quot; I look at the setting to see what the stable &quot;wants to be.&quot; It's kind of a &quot;Player's Guide View&quot; of the set. What group of talent will make the trip through the card set as interesting and distinctive as possible and best sell what we're going for in terms of the tone, vibe and look of the world. Look at how wildly different <i>Future Sight</i> is from <i>Lorwyn</i>. That's not me arbitrarily mixing things up for the sake of rotating artists, it's completely in response to what the set &quot;wants to be.&quot;</p>&#10;                  <p>Then I literally write out a list of who I want to use, based on set needs and how much work is available, and then contact those people to try to make sure they are willing and available.</p>&#10;                  <p>From there, I go one single card at a time and try to match each art description with the single artist who I think will be most interesting and shine brightest on that particular card (based on my perception of that artist's skills, approach, aesthetic and 'voice'). The goal is to have the best possible venues for the artists AND the best art for that card's needs within the spectrum of what we're going for with the setting and world design.</p>&#10;                  <p>JJ</p>&#10;                </td>&#10;              </tr>&#10;            </tbody></table>&#10;          </td>&#10;        </tr>&#10;      </tbody></table>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

I was wondering though what would happen if in a multiplayer game more than one person owns a <a>Necroskitter</a> and a third person's creature dies with a -1/-1 on it?<br> –John, Sydney, Australia</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>A: </b>From the <b>Magic</b> Rules Corner:</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    </p><div>&#10;                      <div>&#10;                        <a>&#10;                          <img alt="Necroskitter" src="https://i.ormos.uk/72/3e/723e178cd526fc90134f10896ba67dddf2e80f826288f1aaaff60e3c6e305762.jpg">&#10;                        </a>&#10;                      </div>&#10;                    </div>What you have here are two triggered abilities that trigger at the same time because of the same event. In what order do they resolve?<p></p>&#10;                  <p>The answer actually depends on the order in which you're sitting and whose turn it is. To find out why, let's go to the Comprehensive Rules. Rule 410.3 reads, in part:</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    </p><blockquote>If multiple abilities have triggered since the last time a player received priority, each player, in APNAP order, puts triggered abilities he or she controls on the stack in any order he or she chooses. (See rule 103.4.)</blockquote>&#10;                  <p></p>&#10;                  <p>Ah, APNAP order! ...What was that, again?</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    </p><blockquote>103.4. If multiple players would make choices and/or take actions at the same time, the active player (the player whose turn it is) makes any choices required, then the next player in turn order (usually the player seated to the active player's left) makes any choices required, followed by the remaining nonactive players in turn order. Then the actions happen simultaneously. This rule is often referred to as the &quot;Active Player, Nonactive Player (APNAP) order&quot; rule.</blockquote>&#10;                  <p></p>&#10;                  <p>That means that to figure out whose <a>Necroskitter</a> trigger goes on the stack first, you start with the player whose turn it is and go around the table in turn order until you find a <a>Necroskitter</a>. That <a>Necroskitter</a>'s trigger goes on the stack first. Go all around the table this way, putting all the <a>Necroskitter</a> triggers on the stack.</p>&#10;                  <p>Because the stack resolves from top to bottom, the trigger controlled by the last player in turn order will resolve first. <a>Necroskitter</a>'s ability includes the word &quot;may,&quot; so it's optional. The player whose trigger resolves first has the option of returning the creature to play. If he or she does, any other <a>Necroskitter</a> triggers won't do anything. (This is true even if the creature dies again with the trigger still on the stack, because the game treats it as a different object when it changes zones.)</p>&#10;                  <p>If everyone who controls a <a>Necroskitter</a> declines to return the creature this way, it stays where it is—not a bad option when the creature is, say, <a>Phage the Untouchable</a>.</p>&#10;                </td>&#10;              </tr>&#10;            </tbody></table>&#10;          </td>&#10;        </tr>&#10;      </tbody></table>&#10;    </td>&#10;  </tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Any statistical significance?<br> –John, USA</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>A: </b>From <b>Randy Buehler</b>, Director of <b>Magic</b> R&amp;amp;D:</p>&#10;                  <p>R&amp;amp;D was interested in studying how important it is to go first, especially in the current Extended constructed format, so we collected data at both Grand Prix - New Orleans and Grand Prix - Hiroshima. We had players mark an asterisk on their scoresheets to indicate which player won the die roll and then we added up the winning percentages of those players. We know that almost everyone who wins the die roll chooses to go first, but we tracked winning the die roll (not going first) because winning the die roll is the thing we <i>actually</i> care about -- it would be bad if that random event was really important. </p>&#10;                  <p>The results were fascinating: in New Orleans 47% of players who won the die roll went on to win the match. 47%! That means winning the die roll was a <i>bad</i> thing. The only explanation that makes any sense is that going second was actually correct some of the time, but no one knew when to choose 'draw.' In Hiroshima the results were a little less crazy: 53% of players who won the die roll went on to win the match. </p>&#10;                  <p>R&amp;amp;D started out a bit nervous after the format rotated -- after watching Pro Tour - Houston it looked like the first few turns of the game were crucially important in Extended and that led us to fear that the die roll was too important and the format might require a massive round of bannings. However, this data completely allayed our concerns. Extended is faster than Standard, but it's not so fast that the die roll reigns supreme. </p>&#10;                  <p>In addition, this data further confirms our belief that the 'play/draw' rule does a good job in general of making the game fair no matter who goes first. The last time R&amp;amp;D collected a massive amount of data about the winning percentages of players who go first was back when going first was strictly better than going second (because you also got to draw a card). At that time the data showed that winning the opening die roll gave you a winning percentage well over 60%, which is why the play/draw rule was introduced in the first place.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <i>This question first appeared on <a href="/en/articles/askwizards/2003/04-01-ask-wizards-april-2003">April 18, 2003</a> (when Randy was still Director of <b>Magic</b> R&amp;amp;D; he's now Vice President of Digital Gaming). Some of us think that if the first strike damage from a double strike creature kills the creature blocking it, the normal damage will go through and damage the opponent. Others think all of the damage from the double striker is absorbed by the blocking creature. Would you please shed some light on this matter?<br> –Jason, Cheyenne, WY, USA</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <b>A: </b>From the <b>Magic</b> Rules Corner:</p>&#10;                  <p>Once an attacking creature becomes blocked, it won't get to deal combat damage to the defending player unless it has trample or some other ability that specifically lets it do so. This is true even if the blocking creature somehow leaves play in between when it blocks and when combat damage is assigned.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    </p><table>&#10;                      <tbody><tr>&#10;                        <td>&#10;                          <div>&#10;                            <div>&#10;                              <a>&#10;                                <img alt="Hill Giant" src="https://i.ormos.uk/e7/db/e7dbde8a30d10c4e4f9c5195994c5b703edf922171ac0535c54c006c10137da0.jpg">&#10;                              </a>&#10;                            </div>&#10;                          </div>&#10;                        </td>&#10;                        <td>&#10;                          <div>&#10;                            <div>&#10;                              <a>&#10;                                <img alt="Runes of the Deus" src="https://i.ormos.uk/1e/32/1e32e889604245da5bfe64c46b955aeaeb26cdddb824979de83f781284087983.jpg">&#10;                              </a>&#10;                            </div>&#10;                          </div>&#10;                        </td>&#10;                      </tr>&#10;                    </tbody></table>&#10;                  <p></p>&#10;                  <p>So let's say a <a>Hill Giant</a> enchanted with <a>Runes of the Deus</a>—a 4/4 with double strike—is blocked by a <a>Grizzly Bears</a>. In the first-strike combat damage step, the <a>Hill Giant</a>'s controller assigns 4 damage to the <a>Grizzly Bears</a>. After first-strike damage resolves, the <a>Grizzly Bears</a> is destroyed. </p>&#10;                  <p>When we get to the normal combat damage step, nothing happens. The <a>Grizzly Bears</a> is too dead to assign its combat damage. The <a>Hill Giant</a> is still blocked. It doesn't matter that the blocking creature is gone—once a creature becomes blocked during combat, it stays blocked for that entire combat. The <a>Hill Giant</a>'s only option is to assign its combat damage to the creatures blocking it, but since there aren't any left, it assigns nothing.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    </p><table>&#10;                      <tbody><tr>&#10;                        <td>&#10;                          <div>&#10;                            <div>&#10;                              <a>&#10;                                <img alt="Tattermunge Maniac" src="https://i.ormos.uk/9d/15/9d15ec03a845d68611da2db9e9c5d148843de6025ca4272e13d67b108a1865a1.jpg">&#10;                              </a>&#10;                            </div>&#10;                          </div>&#10;                        </td>&#10;                        <td>&#10;                          <div>&#10;                            <div>&#10;                              <a>&#10;                                <img alt="Runes of the Deus" src="https://i.ormos.uk/1e/32/1e32e889604245da5bfe64c46b955aeaeb26cdddb824979de83f781284087983.jpg">&#10;                              </a>&#10;                            </div>&#10;                          </div>&#10;                        </td>&#10;                      </tr>&#10;                    </tbody></table>&#10;                  <p></p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <a>Runes of the Deus</a>, however, brings us to that big exception: trample. Let's say that instead of a <a>Hill Giant</a>, the attacking creature is a <a>Tattermunge Maniac</a>, now a 4/3 with double strike and trample. During the first-strike combat damage step, the <a>Tattermunge Maniac</a>'s controller assigns 2 damage to the <a>Grizzly Bears</a> and 2 damage to the Bears' controller (keeping in mind that he or she could assign more of that damage to the <a>Grizzly Bears</a>, of course). This is lethal to the <a>Grizzly Bears</a>, so it heads to the graveyard.</p>&#10;                  <p>When we get to the normal combat damage step, the <a>Grizzly Bears</a> is too dead to assign its combat damage, as above, but the <a>Tattermunge Maniac</a> has trample. An attacking creature with trample can have its excess combat damage assigned to the defending player it's attacking (or the planeswalker it's attacking) as long as each creature blocking it is assigned lethal damage first. (&quot;Lethal damage&quot; is the amount of damage that would theoretically cause the blocking creature to be destroyed. This is equal to the creature's toughness, minus any amount of damage that's already been assigned to it or is simultaneously being assigned to it. Ignore things like protection or other damage prevention when calculating this number.) If there aren't any creatures blocking the attacking creature, &quot;lethal damage&quot; is considered to be 0, so all of the attacking creature's combat damage—in this case, 4—can be assigned to the defending player.</p>&#10;                  <p>&#10;                    <i>The <b>Magic</b> Rules Corner is a weekly feature dedicated to answering your rules questions.
