With Euros nearly upon us, I've been doing a lot of drafting lately in the hopes that my theories about this tournament will be correct. See, Euros is coming right at the end of a Standard format that's been getting a little stale (red-green's surprising turnout at Canadian Nats not withstanding) with Psychatog this and Psychatog that, and while we can't use *Judgment's* environment-altering cards like Anurid Brushhopper and Seedtime to throw a little spice in there, we can use them in Limited while they're still fresh to get a leg up on the opposition.

See, in a couple of months, by the time Worlds comes around, everyone's going to have figured out as much as they're going to how to use *Judgment* cards in Limited play, but we aren't there yet. Right now we're in that nether-period of time where a third of all drafters are exercising their need to choose by picking as if we were still in *Odyssey-Odyssey-Torment*, another third are, even worse, reading each *Judgment* card as they're cast against them and the last third are prepared to take advantage of the other 67% and their inability to get off their collective posteriors in beginning the learning curve that should serve them for the next four months.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/5a/48/5a487069d9fc2c7a1ecece659d5f148fe7e62be20971e10ca589d784c729e925.jpg)

This is especially true this year for two reasons: First, *Judgment* probably makes *Odyssey* block the most complex draft environment ever devised by the Machiavellian minds in R&D, thanks largely to the importance of graveyard manipulation and control, and second, **Magic Online's** beta test has finally come to an end. For months, internet-friendly gamers around the globe have relied on this, the single greatest teaching tool in the game's history to do its thing, providing a 24-hour learning apparatus in the comfort of one's own home, and all for the low, low price of nothing. Zero. Nada.

**Magic Online's** beta test finally came to an end on June 16th, meeting with a mixed reaction composed mostly of 'Too bad, it was so great to have' to 'I guess it's a good thing, I haven't slept for three months', but the cruel irony of the timing here is that it comes just days after the introduction of *Judgment*. Players may have gotten a draft or two in here and there, but by the time they'd learned the pictures, let alone card names and functions, **Magic Online** was gone, only to return on the 24th, by which time it will be too late for it to teach an environment to Europe's finest as they'll be heading to Amsterdam on the 26th or 27th. Now, we're all scrambling to get prepared in an exercise that feels akin to rowing without a boat, but perhaps the remainder of this column will provide a few useful observations about drafting in *Judgment*.

### Black

The center of the *Odyssey* block universe seems to be black, but it's more a gamble than a color these days. See, everyone wants *Torment's* finest offerings and few will care about *Judgment's*, and that means treachery and backstabbing right and left. Gone are the good old days where you and your neighbors would massage one another through forty-five cards: now, the guy on your left wants you to dull the black for the guy on his left so that third guy will pass the second guy your hard-earned cards.

Drafting black now is a lot like drafting white was a month ago. No, you don't need to get yourself a tailor-made t-shirt or anything create lyrical variations on ten year old Tone Loc tunes, but cutting and cutting hard is very much the key. If you open a weak pack whose two best cards are Dirty Wererat and Gravedigger (which come together quite often), what's going to happen? You take the one you prefer (my personal choice is the Rat), the guy you're passing to or the girl they're passing to take the other and come *Torment*, you're accepting their sloppy seconds with a meagre 'thank you sir, may I have another?' Then in Judgment, you get all the Treacherous Werewolves you can get your hands on. That's not a winning strategy.

If you open that pack with two strong black cards at the forefront, I think you have to seriously consider passing both. *Odyssey* black is ok, but a deck it will not make, and while the Soul Scourges and Mesmeric Fiends you'll have passed to you in lieu of Butchers and Fatigues will suffice as a second color if your other color is strong, let's be frank: when you draft black, you're going for the 3-0: Fiends and Scourges won't do that for you. 

The key is watching the stuff you're passing, not the stuff you're taking. Frankly, you don't need to have a single black card from *Odyssey* to draft the almighty black deck: what you do need is to remember the cards you've passed in *Odyssey* and make a decision based on their content about whether the two players behind you will be black drafters or not. If you've passed one Dusk Imp, an Innocent Blood and a Filthy Cur, they won't be. If you passed any of Ghastly Demise, Morbid Hunger, Afflict, Patriarch's Desire or Dirty Wererat, they probably will be.

The real decision comes at the beginning of the second pack. Faceless Butcher, Crippling Fatigue and Cabal Torturer are all among the top five commons in *Torment* for the purposes of Limited play (Skywing Aven and Fiery Temper, to answer the inevitable question), and each come with hefty color considerations, so when you're looking at the 16th pick Butcher as a possible foray into black, the real consideration is going to be how much black you'll be picking up in order to support your choice to put swamps in your deck. Splashing for Butcher is a sure fire way to screw up your mana base to what will eventually be the point of no return, and yes, the power level of your cards could carry you to a three-oh, but its more likely those swamps, drawn instead of the basic land types that could fuel the other four spells in your hand, will leave you a wasted gamer in the realms of one and two. There really isn't a lot of halfway here: either you're going black or you aren't. Start drafting the format and learning your pro-black or anti-black situations. Once you get used to discerning which is which, drafting with these sets will get a whole lot easier.

### If No One Wants It, It's Got to Be Good

One of the oldest rules in draft is 'draft what they don't'. Now obviously, one has no choice but to follow that code in that no one has selected the cards you pick up post-pass, but there is a greater meaning here.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>Red is everyone's whipping boy.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Invariably, each draft environment has that one color that around half of the educated won't touch, and what that color is really doesn't matter all that much, because things have a way of evening out. If you're splitting green five ways and red two ways, which do you think is going to be your better color? Yes, green has strength, depth and power, but on average there will be exactly the same number of green cards as red ones in any given draft, and regardless of their strength, when you have three more players gunning for them, their value is going to be decreased.

This time around, red is everyone's whipping boy. In an environment with 'the black set' and 'the green-white set' and blue's waters running as deep as any of the others, on a card for card basis red is definitely the patsy. With generally weak creatures, burn that faces superb damage prevention effects, spells that offer your opponent their choice of effect and no way to deal with Wild Mongrel, the card that more than any other defines the environment, red is surely undraftable, right?

Actually, that isn't correct. Red-black is basically dead and saying that red-blue is awkward is an understatement, but the white and green variations on this theme, providing suitably aggressive support structures for red's damage based cards, are alive and kicking. Red-green, the more classic of the two archetypes, is likely the weaker of the two now, with *Torment's* common slots offering less than a lot to that player wanting to build around little things like 'mana curve' and 'creatures', but either green or white is entirely capable of helping red in the chore it accomplishes best: twenty points of damage.

Red's 'burn or no?' cards look particularly janky on the surface, but they really do add up. Your opponent plays Longhorn Firebeast on turn three, you go to fifteen. Then they Blazing Salvo your Looter and you're down to ten. Soon they've Firebolted you and played a Dwarven Scorcher, when they suddenly drop Browbeat onto the table, and before you know what's happening, they're drawing three cards and ruining your hopes of a flawless victory.

Like Browbeat isn't bad enough, Book Burning may be the even bigger beating. Cast on turn two, you have the option of giving up a third of your life total, hardly the heart-warming ideal. If you have that slow draw, you can hardly afford to have your opponent getting that kind of jump on you. Next thing you know, Anurid Barkripper comes down turn three, Springing Tiger turn four and Metamorphic Wurm turn five. Talk about your rock and hard place. 

The point here isn't that red is awesome though. I had warnings of red's weakness coming before the set's release, and most of you figured out what I'd been told pretty quickly. The point is this: when you sit down at a table with seven players who insist they won't draft a color, and there are just as many cards in that color as any other, if you draft that color you'll have strong deck. I wouldn't go forcing red were I you, but if you open Firebolt, or better yet Shower of Coals or Kamahl, don't be afraid to explore your more violent tendencies.

This card was the buzz at U.S. Nationals, but now I've had a chance to play with it, I'm fairly convinced that in blue-white or blue-black you should be taking Wormfang Drake over it. Yes, Keep Watch offers the potential for massive card advantage that few commons have, but in blue-black you won't have enough creatures to take it beyond two cards more often than not, while in blue-white you'll have stabilizing cards that will keep your opponent for making all out assaults (Barring your getting a Battle Screech). Blue-green however, is a different story.

It's amazing how the presence of one card can affect the value of another so drastically. If you're drafting green-blue these days, first picking Acorn Harvest is far from the worst thing in the world, because that card can turn into four in a hurry if you get your hands on a Keep Watch, let alone Scrivener or multiples of the instant. You can't pick the Watch early and rely on your opponent to provide the fodder for it; you need to find its inspiration yourself. In blue-green, it's pretty easy to do that. If you're any other combo, take the Drake and get yourself a few Benevolent Bodyguards, Hapless Researchers and Cabal Trainees. If you're blue-green though, get ready to draw some cards.
