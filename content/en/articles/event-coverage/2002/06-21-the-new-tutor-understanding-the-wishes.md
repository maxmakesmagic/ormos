Silver bullets are nothing new in **Magic**. The strategy of relying upon Tutor effects and "hate" or "hoser" cards that are extremely powerful yet geared narrowly toward defeating a particular deck has been around as long as there have been unrestricted ways to search your library. The introduction of the *Mirage* Tutors spawned this trend years ago. The silver bullet strategy caught on quickly, and it wasn't long after their release that players were using Enlightened Tutors to find that single Karma or CoP: Red after sideboarding, or even in their main deck.

Many players have met with high level tournament success with this sort of strategy. One notable example is Bob Maher at Pro Tour-Chicago with his Enlightened Tutor-based Oath of Druids deck. Bob's championship deck contained single copies of extremely powerful enchantments and artifacts in both his main deck and sideboard, and four copies of Enlightened Tutor allowed him easy access to whichever was most effective against his opponent's strategy at the time. Oath itself shut down nearly all creature-based strategies, while Sylvan Library punished slower control decks, Ivory Mask nullified the discard and direct damage effects of Necropotence decks, and more. Bob's sideboard contained even more of these silver bullets, like CoP: Red and Arcane Laboratory, ensuring that he had something potent to search for against every major archetype that could, in many cases, singlehandedly win the game.

Bob's success with silver bullets at PT-Chicago (and, subsequently, GP-Seattle) was certainly no fluke. The extremes to which modern decks are tuned to do one job and to do it well very often leaves them open to cards that prey on their vulnerabilities. Jon Finkel won the U.S. National Championships in the year 2000 with a Vampiric Tutor-based deck called "Napster" (because it let you get whatever you want), largely designed by Mike Flores, that exploited these vulnerabilities by including single copies of cards such as Perish, which decimated his quarterfinal opponent and teammate-to-be Frank Hernandez, as well as Persecute, whose power against slower monocolored decks was showcased in Jon's three game trouncing of Chris Benafel in the finals.

For all their merit, however, silver bullet strategies have always had several notable weaknesses. Most prominently, they are often extraordinarily weak against decks for which they have no "bullet", as they give up consistency for access to hate cards of such high raw power. Both Maher and Finkel's decks required extraordinary draws to defeat focused blue control decks such as Draw-Go or Accelerated Blue, simply because the card disadvantage inherent in their use of Tutors was so painful when fighting such battles of attrition, and because, despite all the weapons at their disposal, there was hardly an "I win" card to Tutor for against either deck. Maher's best bet was to resolve an early Sylvan Library and ride its card drawing and selectivity, but even this would often be insufficient, while Finkel had to hope for an unanswered Phyrexian Negator or Skittering Horror, all the while hoping against hope that his opponent couldn't find Morphling in time. Neither of these is nearly as attractive as "Tutor for CoP: Red, win" or "Tutor for Perish, win".

The second drawback to playing a Tutor-based deck is the potential to have a number of dead cards against any given opponent. Sure, your main deck Perish is your best friend against Stompy, but draw it against Rebels and you'll certainly be much less happy with it. In the case of the main deck, there are only so many hate cards you can fit before diluting your basic strategy to the point that your deck's consistency drops precariously. One must strike a careful balance between having enough tools to handle the major opposing decktypes and maintaining some semblance of a consistent deck.

Lastly, there is the issue of the Tutors themselves. All of the *Mirage* Tutors were relatively cheap, but paid for their low mana costs in card disadvantage and a time delay - rather than putting the chosen card into your hand, they merely placed it on top of your library. This issue was generally alleviated by the enormous advantage given by the Tutored for card, but because of the limited number of silver bullets one could fit against any given deck type, each Tutor after the first tended to drop in power quickly, with the second and third Tutors providing very much diminished returns. The notable exception to this rule was Vampiric Tutor due to its unlimited versatility, but even in that case the life loss and card disadvantage of multiple Tutors could add up quickly.

Why is all of this important to grasp now, so long after the *Mirage* Tutors rotated out of the Standard environment? *Judgment* has offered up the newest in the line of Tutoring power in the form of the Wishes that, like the *Mirage* Tutors, will have their own unique impact upon the world of deck design. Whereas previously when building silver bullet decks, one had the luxury of taking the Tutor base into account when constructing the sideboard, in the case of the Wishes, one has to design the deck and sideboard together as a single unit. The sideboard suddenly must be viewed as an important consideration at every part of the design process (as it should be), rather than something that can be thrown together haphazardly right before handing in a deck list. All seventy-five cards on your deck list are relevant not just between games, but in the course of every game you play.

The way the Wish mechanic functions - that is, retrieving a card from your sideboard rather than one in your deck - affords users a decided advantage over the *Mirage* Tutors in a number of respects. Most notable is the ability to have access to ANY of your silver bullet cards in every game. Whereas a Tutor deck may only have access to its most powerful silver bullets for a particular deck type after sideboarding, the Wish deck can access any of the hate cards in its arsenal starting in game one.

Decks built to abuse the Wishes can take several forms. The most obvious is the sort of "silver bullet" deck seen in the examples of Bob Maher's Oath deck and Jon Finkel's Control Black. The power of these decks, however, relies largely upon the strength of the silver bullet cards available against the other popular decks in the environment. Maher Oath and Napster Black had access to cards that could single-handedly decimate opposing strategies, like Oath of Druids itself against creature decks, or Stromgald Cabal in the Napster deck against Replenish.

With the restrictions on card type imposed by the Wishes and the general weakening of "hoser" cards in recent times, it seems unlikely that this sort of strategy can be to replicated in the current Standard with similar levels of success. There are no Perishes, no Oaths of Druids, no Stromgald Cabals or Ivory Masks. There still are Composts and Circles of Protection, as well as Teferi's Moats and Ensnaring Bridges, sure, but getting artifacts and enchantments is a bit more pricey than finding the other card types. Enlightened Tutor's price has skyrocketed in its transformation to Wish form, and Vampiric Tutor's life cost looks cheap next to the Infernal Contract-esque Death Wish. That leaves Living Wish, Cunning Wish, and Burning Wish for truly viable Tutoring options. Creatures are notoriously weak as silver bullets, and there isn't much more Living Wish can do in that department that Eladamri's Call hasn't tried and failed at doing, as the additional flexibility to fetch lands hardly helps. The scope of devastating instant hosers is similarly narrow, leaving only Burning Wish to have true silver bullet-fetching potential. There are a lot of very powerful sorceries out there, and while examining all of the possibilities goes beyond the scope of this particular article, one need only examine a deck like Brian Davis's "When Sorceries Attack" from U.S. Nationals this year to get a taste of the possibilities.

Clearly, the Wishes would allow for easy access to silver bullets like Persecute and Haunting Echoes, as well as other possible hosers like Mutilate, Epicenter, or Obliterate - only testing can truly tell what the proper measures should be. Additionally, the use of Burning Wish can provide easy access to key cards of a non-silver bullet variety. For instance, Brian Davis played one copy of Soul Burn in his main deck at U.S. Nationals - an odd choice, by all accounts, but this allowed him to access this single copy in game one with Diabolic Tutor if for some reason he had need of Soul Burn's specific function. With Burning Wish, such cards are easily accessible in every game - they need only be waiting in the sideboard. One need not worry about having answers for every possible situation in the main deck - "playing it honest", as Pro Tour legend Shawn "Hammer" Regnier used to always call it - as you can have whatever solution you need almost whenever you need it, so long as you thought to include it in your sideboard.

Take, for example, a Psychatog deck designed with the use of Cunning Wish in mind. Where cards like Opposition or Squirrel Nest may be problematic under normal circumstances, having instant access to copies of Teferi's Response or Recoil in the sideboard can shore up such holes. Merfolk Looter and Meddling Mage alike can fall to a game one Ghastly Demise out of the board, and cards like Mana Short or Opportunity can provide powerful options for control battles. While not a silver bullet Tutoring plan, per se, the utility offered by just using Cunning Wish in conjunction with a standard sideboard can nonetheless prove extremely effective at providing a way out from difficult situations.

All of this versatility and raw power does not come without a price, however. Like the Tutors, the Wishes have their own particular set of problems. While their price is not paid in card advantage like their predecessors, neither is it so small that it can be ignored. First and foremost, as with any card, one must pay its price in mana. This may seem too obvious to be worth mentioning, but it is, in fact, the biggest drawback of the Wishes. The difference between one and two mana is tremendous, especially in a format as tempo based as the current Standard. Using a significant portion of your mana in the early game to play a card that does not have an immediate impact on the board can set you significantly behind in development. This is the primary reason Living Wish seems to have much less potential than either Burning or Cunning Wish even simply as a tool for deck consistency and versatility. Committing your second turn in an aggressive deck to Tutoring for a threat card rather than actually playing one is hardly an effective beatdown strategy almost regardless of what one fetching with the Tutor.

The second major problem with the Wishes is a similar tempo issue, and that is the fact that all of them - with the exception of Cunning Wish (which pays for it with a higher mana cost) - are sorcery speed. This means that all of your access to "hosers" is time delayed unless you have the mana to play both the Wish and the Wished-for card in the same turn. While this may not seem especially significant in many situations, it does significantly cut down on your ability to react to your opponent's immediate threats by Wishing for the appropriate answer - indeed, it instead gives them time to anticipate and play around your Wished-for card. Burning Wish for a Wrath of God, and your opponent isn't likely to play another creature, while you continue to take the beats for a turn while you wait to untap and cast it. Living Wish for a Flametongue and just see how eager your opponent is to commit a creature right away. It may be but a brief delay, but the setback on Wishes time and mana-wise can be extremely costly in a close game.

Lastly, the Wishes impose a design constraint upon your sideboard and dilute your ability to use it for games two and three. Whereas non-Wish decks can just sideboard in their more effective cards and sideboard out their weak cards, Wish decks are forced either to rely upon their best cards remaining in the sideboard to be accessed by Wishes in post-sideboarded games or taking the Wishes out entirely in favor of the cards that would otherwise be Tutored for. This distinctly weakens the deck's ability to fully take advantage of the tuning qualities of a good sideboard, and it's entirely possible that because of this Wish decks - while quite potent in game one - lose a certain level of their effectiveness after sideboarding.

While this is by no means a comprehensive review of the Wishes and their potential, hopefully it can serve as a springboard for you to better understand what goes into constructing a deck that utilizes Judgment's most interesting new cycle.
