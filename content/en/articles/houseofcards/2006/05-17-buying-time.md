Welcome to Tempo Week! I wasn't sure what I was going to do this week. I was worried that I was going to have to build a bunch of Man-o'-War/Fallen Askari decks, but luckily, my editors had some other ideas. To switch things up a little from my usual material, I thought I'd talk about *how* I go about building decks. In particular, how I avoid getting steamrolled, even though I play with what most people think of as bad cards or \*gasp\* “bad” decks.

### Using that “Wacky” Combo

A “Johnny” is many things, and perhaps the most obvious of those things is “Combo Player.” Although I am clearly a Johnny, I haven't actually built too many decks that can really be defined as “combo” decks. There have been a few, and all of the decks have featured cards that work well together. They rarely do what “pure” combo decks do, however, which is win in a single turn after doing something dramatic.

When you want to build an “impure” combo deck, coming up with the combo is the easy part. Just find two cards and put a plus-sign in between them. For example, Phytohydra + Pariah's Shield. Bam! There's a combo. Djinn Illuminatus + Shock. Pow! There's another one. Might of the Nephilim + uh, one of the Nephilim. Zlop! That's three combos and it only took me two weeks to figure them out. After all that effort, though, what I'm left with is two card names and plus-sign. I probably won't win too many games with just those things, not even in Canada. I still need a deck. That's the difficult part.

Imagine you are going through the *Saviors of Kamigawa* spoiler. You come across a card you think is neat and you decide to build a deck around it. The card is Maga, Traitor to Mortals. There are a number of ways to build a Maga deck, of that there is no doubt. Maybe you want to keep replaying Maga each turn by exploiting Skull Collector's drawback like I did during Orzhov week. Maybe you want to do the same thing with Cloudstone Curio like reader Gabe did. Maybe you'd prefer a mono-black control-style deck so you can use Cabal Coffers to make big Magas. Or maybe you'd rather build a deck with an infinite (arbitrarily large) or near-infinite (near-arbitrarily large) mana engine, ensuring that Maga will be lethal each time you play him.

There is often a perceived divide between the so-called “casual community” and Pro players, but as a casual player myself, I like the Pro Tour. I like Constructed more than Limited, naturally, and Rogue decks in particular. As I hope to demonstrate, I think that there is lot we can learn by looking at Pro Tour decks, even if we don't play in tournaments ourselves. Let's take a look at the combo deck *du jour*, Heartbeat, which Max Bracht took to a sixth place finish at Pro Tour--Honolulu.

How do you win with this deck? Make twenty-three (or more) mana and play Maga, Traitor to Mortals (or Invoke the Firemind).

More importantly, how do you win on turn 5 or 6 and not on turn 23?

### Speeding Yourself Up

Due to the fact that a lot of Johnny cards are what are lovingly termed “Reject Rares,” and often cost more mana than you would like, it is quite possible that you will be dead long before you get your combo up and running. Take Phytohydra and Pariah's Shield, for example. Assuming one land per turn, you will be able to play your Phytohydra on turn 5, Pariah's Shield on turn 6, and you will finally be able to equip the Hydra on turn 7. Maga, Traitor to Mortals is even more of an extreme example. Again, that's if you keep playing land each turn. The other problem is that, barring assistance, you will have to draw into your combo naturally. That's not guaranteed to happen in a timely manner. For some of the more convoluted combos out there, it is next to impossible. 

How do we solve these two problems? 

### Mana acceleration!

Heartbeat of Spring, Sakura-Tribe Elder, Kodama's Reach.

![](http://www.wizards.com/global/images/mtgcom_daily_cm19_picMain_en.jpg)

I bet if you go back through the [House of Cards archives](https://web.archive.org/web/20060517000000/http://www.wizards.com/default.asp?x=/default.asp?x=mtgcom/columnarchive&column=HouseofCards), you'll discover that cards like Explosive Vegetation, Rampant Growth, the *Mirrodin* Talismans, and the dynamic, unambiguously-good duo (Elder and Reach) are among the most consistently used cards. I know for a fact that I've made extensive use of the *Ravnica* Block Signets and *9th Edition's* Fellwar Stone. These cards allow to effectively skip ahead a turn. Instead of playing a three-mana spell on your third turn, you can play a four-mana spell. While your opponent is stuck at three lands, Elder and Reach let you send them a postcard from turn 5. This is a good thing for Pro Tour decks, but it's probably even better for fun casual decks, where the cards are by nature less efficient.

### Card drawing, tutoring!

Drift of Phantasms, Weird Harvest, Compulsive Research, Muddle the Mixture, Sensei's Divining Top.

While not “tempo” cards *per se*, card drawing spells and tutors will nevertheless speed up your kill by whole turns. For the Heartbeat deck, you will often want to play Heartbeat of Spring, multiple Early Harvests, and Maga, all in the same turn. That's a lot of specific spells. Over time, Sensei's Divining Top will allow you sculpt your hand to some extent. The Transmute cards and Weird Harvest are much more reliable, however. With enough mana, all you need is a single Muddle the Mixture to get things started. Muddle gets you Weird Harvest, which gets you Drift of Phantasms, which gets you Early Harvest and Maga. Later versions of Heartbeat used a single Train of Thought which can be found with Muddle the Mixture. Without the power to find the cards you need when you need them, the deck would be much slower. This is pretty obvious, but I see a lot of decks Online that end up doing a whole lot of nothing because they can't get their combo pieces together before they are facing a lethal attack.

### Slowing Your Opponent Down

Even if you do assemble all the pieces of your “combo,” it won't matter too much if you are so low on life that your opponent can just kill you anyway. Take the Djinn Illuminatus + Shock combo for example. I mentioned last week that if you untap with the Djinn in play, you should win the game. You're going to need a few things to go right for that to happen. You need the two cards and the appropriate mana, and you will need enough of a life-cushion so *your opponent* can't untap and win the game. Here are a few ways to ensure the latter:

### Walls!

Sakura-Tribe Elder, Drift of Phantasms.

Everyone who's ever built a Wall + Burn deck knows how fun it can be to sit in your impenetrable fortress while you lob direct damage spells at your opponent. In this case, playing a Wall of Stone is a small price to pay to be able to focus all of your burn on your opponent and not on his or her creatures. In Heartbeat, blocking creatures is not what the Elder and Drift of Phantasms are there for, primarily, but that doesn't mean they can't do it in a pinch. It's often worth investing in an obstacle for your opponent's, if it means you will have some extra time to get your act together. Elder in particular is great, since it can thwart early attacks and act as a one-creature “Fog” later in the game. I've built a lot of decks with these cards, as well as Carven Caryatid, because they handle creatures so well while still remaining useful at nearly all points in the game.

### Counters!

Remand, Muddle the Mixture.

These guys serve a few purposes. Remand is a procrastinator's dream. Your opponent wants to know when he can have his turn 2, and you say, “How about turn 3?” For slower decks, this is a boon. It's really fun to draw a Remand off of a Remand, too, which lets you buy even more time. Max's deck can win the game with this extra time, but many Johnny decks cannot, which is why Mana Leak or Muddle the Mixture is often better. Those two spells have their own limitations, but can still handle early threats or counter key late-game spells.

### Sweepers!

Max didn't have any in the main deck, but his sideboard featured three copies of creature-sweeper Savage Twister. Other versions of the deck that I've seen have used a single copy maindeck, or a single copy of Pyroclasm, or both, since the two cards can be fetched by Muddle the Mixture. A turn 1 Savannah Lions can take off half of your life total by the time turn 6 rolls around, and if you add anything else to the mix, you could be perilously close to death by the time your deck starts doing its thing. This is less of a problem in Max's case, because generally, when his deck “does its thing” it wins the game. Other decks don't have that luxury, so it's often necessary to devote more slots to the board sweepers if you don't want to lose to just about any aggro deck. Pyroclasm should be a staple for just about everybody, and I've used it more often than not when I've built a slower Red-based deck.

With these things in mind, how about some decks?

### The Other Plow

After reading a [Ben Bleiweiss article](/en/articles/buildingonabudget/2006/04-17-gruul-the-wilding-part-2) in which he was using Gruul War Plow and Savage Twister, I was inspired to build a deck. You see, those two cards have a lot of synergy. Savage Twister is fine mass removal. It kills a lot of creatures (*This includes your creatures*). Gruul War Plow, meanwhile, is an artifact that moonlights as a creature. While Savage Twister is busy sweeping the board, the War Plow is parked on the sidelines, chilling out, relaxing with the other plows. After the board is clear, Gruul War Plow gets off its lazy butt and says, “What did I miss?” before attacking for four.

There are other cards that function like Gruul War Plow. Reprinted in *9th Edition*, Jade Statue is the original artifact-that-becomes-a-creature. Jade Idol and Slumbering Tora do the same thing, but they require a deck focused on Spirits and Arcane spells. Genju of the Spires and Genju of the Cedars, however, fit right in. By sheer (happy) coincidence, Jade Statue and the Genjus *love* Gruul War Plow's Primal Rage ability.

Since most of your creatures require mana to get going, you're going to need to have access to a lot of it. Sakura-Tribe Elder, Kodama's Reach, and Farseek help to ensure that you can cast your spells and make your artifacts and lands come alive. Pyroclasm lets you double up on the board sweepers, while Sensei's Divining Top helps you find what you need when you need it. Homura, Human Ascendant is in there just for fun. He doesn't really care if you sweep the board while he's in play, and he makes your statues and plows and genjus into flying dragons, which is never a bad thing. I tried Skarrgan Firebird, too, but there just wasn't enough mana to go around.

I wouldn't normally include Sensei's Divining Top in a deck. It's not that it's not a good card. It's great. I just don't like playing with it. There are a few reasons for this, the most important of which is that it prolongs games. After I used my Top at the end of my opponent's turn and then again during my upkeep (looking at the same three cards!), I realized that it was not the card for me. Unfortunately, R/G is not known for its card-drawing, so the fact that there are so many shuffle effects in the deck make the Top a natural inclusion. Mikokoro, Center of the Sea helps to keep the cards flowing as well.

### Live by the inferno, die by the inferno

When building decks, don't hesitate to blatantly steal take inspiration from any source. I got the idea for the next deck from a [Saturday School question](/en/articles/feature/2006/04-01-its-not-all-black-and-white). It had to do with the interaction between Living Inferno and Cowardice. As John says, “With eight points of power to divide up, this means Living Inferno could bounce as many as eight creatures with each activation complements of Cowardice.”

Living Inferno and Cowardice is a neat combo, but it's a little on the expensive side. The other problem is that you don't want to pay eight mana for a creature only to have your opponent bounce it. I originally had Gelectrodes in the deck as well, but it's no fun when your opponent bounces them, either. That's why, in the end, I decided to play with creatures you don't mind bouncing so much (Sparkmage Apprentice and Steamcore Weird) and just two copies of Living Inferno. Captive Flame is a much cheaper way of targeting creatures, and since it isn't a creature, it doesn't have the same vulnerability to Cowardice. While we're in U/R, we might as well take advantage of all the goodies the Izzet offer the Cowardice player. Electrolyze and Pyromatics can often be one-sided Evacuations, but you can also send that damage straight to your opponent's head or use it to kill some creatures pre-Cowardice. Its comparative lack of versatility is why I ended up cutting Gigadrowse.

Mana Leak, Sparkmage Apprentice, Pyromatics, and Electrolyze should let you get through the early turns relatively unscathed. If you don't need to slow your opponent down, feel free to speed yourself up with the Signets and the all-powerful, uber-broken Ur-Golem's Eye. That mana will come in handy when you want to cast Living Inferno, but it will rarely go to waste because of the Replicate spells. If the game goes long enough, you can use Captive Flame to start bouncing your Sparkmages and Steamcore Weirds.
