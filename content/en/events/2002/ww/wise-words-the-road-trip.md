If you discount the incomparable Shawshank Redemption, my favorite film of all time is Field of Dreams[1](#1), a 1989 film which, more than any aside from maybe Ken Burns' Baseball, defines the magical, mystical qualities of the American game. When I was a teenager, baseball was the only thing I could speak with my father about without boiling blood and pulsing veins coming into the conversation, and it's this film that so accurately defines my relationship with my now five-years-deceased dad. I think it's a great film anyways, but it's those personal connections that can really bring a movie closer to one's heart.

Field of Dreams has many themes: baseball, the father-son relationship... but the one that strikes me as especially important today is best defined by that magical voice in the corn field when it says: "Go the distance". Everyman Ray Kinsella, perfectly portrayed by Kevin Costner (who can actually act as long as you involve sports and leave accents by the wayside) listens to the voice, knowing intuitively he must get in his rusty old van and drive from Iowa... to Boston.

Guess what I'm doing Sunday.

At 12:55 PM, the Greyhound bus from Sarnia, Ontario will be arriving in Toronto with one Geordie Tait on board. Josh Rider and I will be waiting for him to disembark, and when he does, the three of us will be starting a two week road trip that will take us first to the Hobart House in Pittsburgh, then to The Only Game In Town in New Jersey, and then finally to the Pro Tour in Boston. The official purpose of the trip will be to find me some practice time with Boston teammates Mike Turian and Eugene Harvey, find Josh a team to play with either in the PT or in the Last-Chance Qualifier, and find Geordie his muse and a more intimate understanding of some of the Pro Tour's more colorful characters.

I'm a big fan of G Tait's. "Of course you are, Gary," you might say. "He's the only writer on the net who doesn't slag you off constantly." But it's not that. Geordie writes about the game with an enthusiasm I haven't seen in print in a long time. It goes beyond Magic, reaching all the way to the Pro Tour and back again, regarding the Tour and its personalities in a way I've always hoped they'd one day be regarded, instead of the stupid pro hate that never seems to wane in popularity.

I asked Geordie along for a couple of reasons: first off, six hours to Pittsburgh, four more to New Jersey, another four to Boston and ten back to Toronto is a lot of driving for one person to do alone, and the company is welcome (This was before Rider signed on; his three month European adventure ended after less than a month). More importantly though, I think the one thing missing from his repertoire as a writer is a more personal familiarity with PT denizens and their habits, and figured this would be a good chance for him to be exposed to all that. The result, Geordie's own On The Road-type experience should be showcased on Star City over the next couple of weeks.

With so much on the horizon, I have Field of Dreams on the DVD, just finished my coffee and bagel and am trying to piece together a road trip born of poor planning and my own stupidity. Of all the people reading this as you're doing so, none is laughing harder than Kai Budde or Patrick Mello.

See, the last time I ran a road trip like this one was when I came home from GP-Columbus a couple of years ago. I met Kai, Pat and Brian Selden there three weeks before 2001 Worlds, which was held in my native Toronto and for which the three would be staying at my house. The trip home was fraught with mishaps, ranging from Brian leaving on my never-before-used hand brake (leading to a strange burning smell ten minutes down the road) to desperate four lane-switches, to constant unintentional detours, all of which I was able to explain with a wave of my hand: "it's a road trip." Things are supposed to go wrong, you're supposed to get lost, and if everything goes according to plan, you're planning too much. The great American road trip is about screw ups and mishaps, points A and B and an adventure in between. That's why I'm looking forward to the next couple of weeks.

All this road trip talk reminds me of a particular classic I took home from PT-NY 2 with Jeff Donais, who recently departed as head of the DCI.

Jeff and I, with Windsorites Brian "Goat" Goethals and Tom "Jester" Byrne in tow were getting ready to leave Rye when Terry Borer, who'd just won the Pro Tour, approached us with his oversized novelty check in hand. Terry explained that he didn't want to take the check on the plane as he'd have to explain and declare, declare and explain, but nonetheless, he wanted the check as a souvenir and asked us to drive it home for him. Knowing full well this meant leaning the thing on our heads for the entire twelve-hour trip, Jeff eagerly accepted for reasons unbeknownst to me until about two hours later.

Night fell; we got underway and needed nourishment. We pulled off the road, then off the off-road, then off that one too. The road was black, completely unlit, until after a few miles we can upon a small gas station that held rations within. We went inside where we found a guy behind the counter and an old man reading a newspaper. Jeff looked around, said "be right back" and darted out of the store. A moment later, he was back with the check, having me take a picture of him and the old man with it.

The next ten hours went by in a whirl of stops and photographs: me, a guy with his Camaro and the check. Me, a freakish all-night McDonalds employee (one eyebrow and eyelash white, the other brown) and the check. Time and again, we stopped and took pictures with bewildered passerby. Ourselves and the check, eventually hitting up a beautiful municipal worker, a mounted policeman, the Mountain Dew traveling promotional team, a gunnery sergeant... each one bewildered, each one confused, eventually getting the check back to Terry, who was none the wiser until six months later, when the photos showed up at a Toronto PTQ that he happened by.

"It's a road trip!"

Indeed.

The road trip is the epitome of everything that's been great about Magic for me: sharing life experiences with friends, endless exhaustion for the cause of good times, pushing the extra mile to fulfill life's desires. Okay, maybe not everything... driving hundreds of miles has nothing to do with paychecks, for example, but money aside[2](#2), most of my best and worst Magic experiences can be found on the road.

It's strange to me that Jeff won't be at Pro Tours any more. We've known one another going on eight years now, and Magic has furnished our friendship with good times and experience. Time's running short, so before I end this, a quick story about our former benefactor:

The summer after Jeff and I had met, I'd returned to Toronto and my parents house, a large suburban number with more than enough room for whichever guests I deemed fit to come crash when shelter was needed, usually the night before a tournament. With an Ice Age-Alliances sealed PTQ coming up, it was agreed that Jeff and Mike "his brother" Donais would come and stay for the weekend. 

A couple of months earlier, at a tournament in Ann Arbor, Michigan, Jeff, Mike, Braumbacher and an old buddy of ours, John 'Fish' Oleniczek gave me a gift: a starter box full of pieces of torn up Goblin Artisans, the card I'd collected almost 800 of[3](#3), to which, of course, I swore vengeance. Jeff's decision to come to Toronto gave me a chance for revenge. 

I called Eric Tam, Paul McCabe, Terry Borer, Gab Tsang and Elijah Pollock and let them know what was up, and a plan was hatched. The story was to be that a rash of cheating in sealed PTQs throughout the Midwest had convinced Jason Carl and the DCI to call for a PTQ format change, with the new 1.5 to replace Ice Age Sealed. Eric typed up an 'official' letter from Carl, who, of course, had sent similar letters to every player in Toronto and how did Jeff not get one? We called our local T.O. to make sure he's play along if Jeff called for verification. We each built 1.5 decks that we'd be 'playing in the PTQ', covered every angle we could and prepared for the boys' arrival.

Of course, Jeff's first reaction was disbelief. If nothing else, my Living Plane land destruction deck appeared to be 'lacking', and the entire thing seemed preposterous, but our collective upper lips stayed stiff, and slowly he started coming around, finally offering to use the product he'd brought to do some drafting or sealed deck anyways (we of course chose sealed to get some practice in), and after building, he and I settled down to play a match.

It was during our first game that Jeff stopped dead in his tracks with the realization that the players from London, Ontario, had also not received Carl's letter, and as a result would arrive at the tournament completely unprepared. Not wanting to give up the joke, I asked if we should call and warn them so they'd at least have a little time to adjust before the next morning. Jeff thought for a moment, looked up and said two little words. "F\*\*\* 'em". With the joke still intact, thanks to his callous disregard for his friends, Jeff and Mike tested 1.5 until three in the morning, got virtually no sleep, and after Jeff had received word of the joke the next morning, found scrawled on a piece of paper on the 22nd page of my Artisan binder, he went 0-2 drop. Revenge is sweet.

Now, Jeff's gone to Upper Deck, to Yu-Gi-Oh! and away from the game we all know and love. He left on the brink of the Magic event of the year. With the 10th Anniversary just celebrated, Jeff is gone.

Goodbye Jeffrey, you'll be missed. 

Have a good week.

Until now, I haven't written an article in which a response has been appropriate, so I've been waiting a while to write this: you people need to read the book again. Just because Paradise had no money, doesn't mean he didn't care about it. Every expenditure is mentioned, every financial worry mulled over. Money is as American as apple pie, much like that book, and its role in the story seems crucial to the plot in that if Sal had more, his journeys would have differed greatly, and the same can be said for his companions. To suggest money is irrelevant to Paradise is naïve silliness. Do you like apples?
