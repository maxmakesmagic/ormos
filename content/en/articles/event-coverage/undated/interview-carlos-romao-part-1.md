### 

When you think of the term "**Magic** World Champion", dominance comes to mind. True superstars such as Kai Budde and Jon Finkel highlighted their legendary careers by winning the one tournament that confirmed them as the best in the world. This isn't surprising, since the format lends itself to skill more than any other tournament. With eighteen rounds and three different formats, there is a lot more room for losses to mana screw and bad matchups than at your usual Pro Tour. As a result, the Top 8 is usually filled with plenty of veterans, battling among each other for the game's biggest honor. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/worlds02/507.jpg)

This year in Sydney, however, things were much different. Carlos Romao, who had a fair share of success with a Latin American Championships Top 4 and a Grand Prix Championship under his belt, shocked the world by taking the World Championship trophy to Brazil. Argentinean player Diego Ostrovich made Top 4 in the same tournament, placing South America on the map. Suddenly, an area that was never considered much of a threat showed the world they were not to be taken lightly. Yet, in the aftermath of Sydney, many issues remain up in the air. Was this tournament a fluke, or will Latin America and Carlos Romao continue to have success? How has the economic situation within Latin America affected the **Magic** community? I sat down with Carlos at Pro Tour-Boston, and fellow Brazilian player and teammate Victor Galimbertti served as a translator for some questions. He also added some thoughts to the interview, providing a second perspective on the state of Latin American **Magic**.

**Sideboard:** How did you first get into **Magic**?

**Romao:** I learned the game with some friends. First I played **Spellfire**, then I moved to **Magic: the Gathering** when it was released in Portuguese. 

**Sideboard:** Which set was that?

**Romao:** Fourth Edition.

**Sideboard:** What did you like about the game?

**Romao:** Competition. It's about the tournaments. I really like competition.

**Sideboard:** So it was never about casual play? It was always about tournaments to you?

**Romao:** I started in tournaments with friends. Casual play was just when I started. A little while later I started to play in tournaments because I wanted the competition.

**Sideboard:** What inspired you to take the step from playing casual to tournaments?

**Romao:** I wanted more of a challenge. Playing casual is all about fun, and when you play in a tournament you do want to have fun, but you also want to win. Before I played **Magic**, I played basketball on a Brazilian team. I always waited for the basketball tournaments because I enjoyed that more than practicing. 

**Sideboard:** Did basketball help you in any way with **Magic**? Mentally, did it prepare you for playing in tournaments?

**Romao:** Yeah, it helped a lot. Some **Magic** players don't have the psychological strength to play in a Top 8, and basketball helped out a lot with that. In basketball, the crowd creates a lot of pressure, and that prepared me for the pressure of a **Magic** tournament.

**Sideboard:** What do you like better, Limited or Constructed?

**Romao:** Limited.

**Sideboard:** Why?

**Romao:** In Constructed you have to test and decide which deck to play, and it's very difficult. In Limited you don't have to do this. 

**Sideboard:** So you're saying that Constructed is harder than Limited?

**Romao:** No, it's just difficult to decide on a deck. For example you'll have Psychatog, and Opposition, and then you have to pick one. To me, it's very hard, and sometimes very boring.

**Sideboard:** What kind of decks do you like to draft?

**Romao:** I like to draft blue. Always blue!

**Sideboard:** Any format? It doesn't matter if it's *Invasion* Block or *Odyssey*… it's always blue?

**Romao:** Yeah, because I like to draw cards! Blue is the color for that. But, if the set changes, then *you* have to change. In *Mercadian Masques*, white had a way to draw cards: Rebels. So, I liked to play white in that format, but blue is my favorite color. 

**Sideboard:** What kind of Constructed decks do you like to play?

**Romao:** The best! It doesn't matter if it's control or beatdown. 

**Sideboard:** That must be helpful, because most players tend to lean towards a certain deck type. Dave Price will always play beatdown no matter what, but in your situation you'll just play whichever deck is the best.

How did you figure out the strategy to not counter card drawing spells in the Psychatog mirror match? 

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/05/14/0514938b416bd98a5cb7450a0bf5d98d1ce3ba6529ebd74435424c90e4cc1d72.jpg"><br><i>It must be countered.</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**Romao:** You can go against some archetypes of Psychatog… like let's say I play a mirror match against my deck, it's a bad idea because there are a lot of counterspells. But, if you play against… in the finals I played against Mark Ziegner, and his deck had three hard counterspells - it had three Circular Logic, and three Memory Lapse. He can draw lots of cards, but he'll never have more than two or three counters. So, I didn't have to spend my counterspells on his card drawers. In the finals, in I think the fourth game he drew eight more cards than me, and he didn't have a counterspell to stop my Upheaval. 

**Galimbertti:** When he was playing, myself and another player from Brazil were listening to the commentary. They said "he's not countering Fact or Fictions!" and stuff like that, and we didn't even realize that sometimes we don't counter it, and that you guys counter it every time. If you have only one counterspell in hand and your opponent has two cards and he plays Fact or Fiction, if you to counter it, he can Upheaval next turn or something like that and you won't have the counter. So, it was natural for us. We didn't even realize there was a difference. 

**Romao:** We play to counter the main cards. 

**Sideboard:** Some players like Zvi Mowshowitz say that the strategy is wrong, regardless of the fact that it worked for you. How would you respond?

**Romao:** I can't say because it's his point of view. 

**Sideboard:** The story going around at Worlds was that Latin America united for the first time to prepare for the event. Obviously the result was very successful. Is the team going to stay together for future events?

**Romao:** Yeah, we are planning to test for Houston. (Diego) Ostrovich and I are going to test for the Invitational. I think the team will continue and we haven't finished.

**Sideboard:** Gary Wise said that Latin America used to fight internally, and as a result you couldn't get any better. But, now that you're united, the area has become very successful. What caused the team to form in the first place? 

**Romao:** Latin Americans never did too well in Pro Tours. We just had enough and we had to do something about it, and it just came. 

Galimbertti: One of the reasons, I think, is that there wasn't a Latin American Championship. When we had it in 2001, we were practicing to play against each other. Before Grand Prix-Sao Paulo, we didn't exchange information because of that. After it, we arranged to start practicing together.

**Sideboard:** Do you think that if the Latin American Championships had stayed around that you guys wouldn't have gotten together?

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>It's like Gary Wise said in his article for Sideboard: now people will respect Latin America.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Galimbertti: I think we would, but after the Championships, not before.

**Sideboard:** It's very rare that a country works together for a **Magic** tournament, much less an entire continent. Why do you think Latin America was able to accomplish this whereas other countries and other areas haven't?

**Romao:** All the people on the team had a common goal, which was to do well at Worlds. Before we formed the team, we were friends. We didn't test together, but we were friends. There was one Brazilian World Champion last year who exchanged information with Bandana, the Argentinean part, and we used this to build the team- Argentina and Brazil. The Chileans were very good friends with the Argentineans, and now they're very good friends with us. The Venezuelans I didn't know too well until Worlds. There are no superstars on the team, so no one thinks he's better than anyone else. So, there's no ego conflict.

**Sideboard:** Do you think that's a reflection of the culture in some way? I don't think you'll ever see everyone in America working together. Or is that just because Latin America really never had a breakout tournament and you guys wanted to make it happen?

**Romao:** That was the only way to make a strong team. 

**Galimbertti:** For Houston, we are planning to practice with Brazilians who helped us test for Worlds even though they weren't qualified. Now some of them are qualified for Houston, so we're going to test with everyone possible.

**Sideboard:** Do you feel that other countries were getting lazy with practicing and that your hard work gave you an edge?

**Romao:** I don't know, because I don't talk to players from other countries about things like that. We have no way to know if they're practicing more or less than us.

**Sideboard:** What is **Magic** like in Brazil?

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/worlds02/518.jpg)

**Romao:** The players are serious about the game, but they have other priorities. They have to study or work, and do a lot of other things. So, **Magic** starts to be a hobby. Life being harder there means you have less time to play **Magic**. I can focus on **Magic** now because I have the time. I won the World Championship so now I have money to travel. Money is a problem for **Magic** players in Brazil, because it's very expensive to travel to America or Europe for a Pro Tour. Players don't work too hard to qualify because they're going to have to spend lots of money to go, so they're not as excited about it.

**Sideboard:** Do you think the financial situation puts Latin America at a severe disadvantage? 

**Romao:** Yeah, because people get scared about going to the Pro Tour. Lots of people win the PTQs, and then just never go. They're scared that they'll spend all this money to go, and then they'll lose.

**Sideboard:** Do you think that will improve at all now that you've won the World Championship?

**Romao:** No, I don't think so. People can be happy because I won and be like "Yes, we have the World Champion!" but I think the main problem is money. 

**Sideboard:** Do you think there's any solution that could be reached to help out the situation?

**Romao:** No, I think it's a problem for the country. If the country grows, more **Magic** players will arrive. Before Grand Prix, you see people playing a lot more, because it costs very little money to play, and there's a good prize. 

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/worlds02/544.jpg"><br><i>Ostrovich vs. Romao</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

**Sideboard:** Latin America is an area that wasn't really on the map for **Magic** until you won Worlds, and Diego Ostrovich placed in the Top 4. Aside from being a personal victory, you must be very proud to have elevated Brazil and Latin America at the same time.

**Romao:** Sure. It's like Gary Wise said in his article for *Sideboard:* now people will respect Latin America. It's a great victory for us, because before most people didn't respect us. Now I think they will, because they've seen that we can place in the Top 8 of World Championships and even win it.

**Sideboard:** That must have been a very good week for you! You became the World Champion, you put your country on the map and you put Latin America on the map. That's a pretty big deal.

**Romao:** Also, the Argentinean National team came very close to making the finals of the Team Championship at Worlds, so it was an amazing week for Latin America. We had six or seven players in the Top 64.
