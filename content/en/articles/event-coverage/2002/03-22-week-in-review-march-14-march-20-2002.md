### Pro Tour-Osaka

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTOSA02/677.jpg)It has been exactly a year since American players won a Pro Tour (although to be fair, Kai won more PTs than any continent in that time). Ken Ho, who only played in his first Pro Tour last season and was in solid contention for the Rookie of the Year title in 2000-2001, brought the trophy home. Ho tested with The Magic Colony for this Pro Tour, but when the Canadians left for Vancouver, he went with them. He played the same deck as Terry Tsang and Jeff Fung, and was the only Colony affiliate to make Top 8. In fact, the only other Colony resident to finish in the money was Jeff Cunningham.

If the Colony experiment did not work out as well as its residents may have hoped, my open testing experiment results could be interpreted in many ways. Judging by my personal performance it was a total failure, my record being 2-4 drop. I got pretty unlucky though, and several players who tested with me right before the PT did well - Roger Sorino finished 19th playing the exact copy of my deck and sideboard. I also did not miss a major archetype, which is what I was mostly afraid of.

Speaking of archetypes, u-g and monoblack dominated this Pro Tour. Psychatog and monogreen were present on Day 2, but their numbers were dwarfed by what are clearly the two best decks in the format. Final match was between Ho's u-g deck (different from most of the archetype in that it ran Squirrel Nest main deck) and Olivier Ruel's monoblack deck. This is Ruel's best finish in a Pro Tour to date. Until now, his brother was the top magician of the family thanks to Antoine's Top 8 finish at Worlds. Now they are again on an equal footing.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTOSA02/699.jpg)Antoine and Olivier Ruel both qualified for the **Magic** Invitational six months ago, but it was not one of their most successful tournaments. The brothers shared 16th place. That is not all that bad - over the last few years, a player who came in last at the Invitational always came back to win a Pro Tour over the course of the next season. Did Ken Ho break this streak? Antoine and Olivier have two more chances to keep it alive before this season ends.

Ruel was representing the most successful country at this Pro Tour. France managed to place a total of four players in this Top 8! Among them was Les Plus Class member Nicolas Olivieri, whose team placed second at Pro Tour-New York last year - and little known Sylvain Lauriol and Christophe Haim. This is Haim's first Pro Tour - making his finish even more impressive. 

Another rookie to make Top 8 at Osaka was Osyp Lebedowicz. A Neutral Ground player, he got a deck from Team CMU and navigated it to their best finish (CMU's best finish was 18th place by Brian Davis). Lebedowicz started playing on the Pro Tour this season and is in a solid contention for the Rookie of the Year title with a string of money finishes. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTOSA02/714.jpg)Rounding out this Top 8 are veterans Jens Thoren and Rob Dougherty. Both placed in Top 4. Thoren placed second in San Diego and followed it up with an excellent finish, coming into a well-deserved second place of the Player of the Year race. Although he is 10 points ahead of me (I retain third place, though competition is edging closer) - he is still far enough behind Kai. Thoren would need to win one of the two remaining Pro Tour events this season AND outperform Kai at a number of GPs and the other Tour in order to take the title. Finally, Your Move Games' Rob Dougherty earned his fourth Pro Tour Top 8. He designed the successful Reanimator deck that placed two of his teammates in Top 8 of Pro Tour-San Diego, but lost to Dave Humpherys in a match that would determine which of them was to advance to the Top 8 there. Luckily for Dougherty, he faced no teammates in the final round, and advanced to place in the Top 4.

Among Japanese players, Tsuyoshi Ikeda and Tsuyoshi Fujita placed 13th and 14th respectively. Tsuyoshi Fujita, finalist of PT-Tokyo, was all set for a repeat performance at the end of Day 1 as one of only two undefeated players (the other being Jens Thoren) but he did not do well enough on Day 2 to advance into the Top 8. 

Osaka was a success story for Gary Wise and Dave Price. Both of these popular players have recently struggled to remain on the gravy train. Wise's second Top 16 finish in a row takes care of any worries he may have had and launches him closer to the Masters. Price needed a Top 32 finish and earned it, qualifying for yet another Pro Tour. Kai Budde was looking like he was about to have a second finish out of the money in a row as he barely made Day 2, lost his first round and then actually got a bye. He rallied, winning most of his remaining round to finish in a very respectable 24th place, further separating himself from the competition in the Player of the Year race.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/MASTERSOSA02/477.jpg)Budde and teammates Baberowski and Blume were far more successful in the Masters, where they took down Panzer Hunters in the finals. Hunters repeated their story of barely making it to the Masters but then going all the way to the finals from last year. Will the DCI points they earned here qualify them for next year? 

Notoriously absent from Osaka were New Orleans finalist Tomi Walamies and San Diego champion Farid Meraghni. I never got a chance to learn why Walamies wasn't there, but Meraghni did not come because he does not like Constructed as much as Limited, and felt that the trip would be too expensive. His 39 Pro points is enough to qualify for the next two individual Masters tournaments, so he is very likely to show up in Nice.

### Coming Up: Grand Prix-Barcelona

![](https://i.ormos.uk/fd/df/fddfee672c07d16fbb35b69d1c892b2453f3a711d8f9fb6f318517601453edca.gif)The European Grand Prix returns to Barcelona - but some of the top players will not be coming. I was told (though not directly by Kai) that Budde will skip this one. Most of the North American players will not attend either - it is spring break in America, and tickets to Barcelona run over $1000. I won't be attending for that reason either, skipping a second European GP in a row. In addition to the top Spanish and Portuguese players, most of the French pros will come, as should quite a few top pros from around Europe. Just not as many as a lot of us are looking to rest a bit after a long trip to Japan.

### Rules Clarification

![](https://i.ormos.uk/dc/b4/dcb41a2de60b08bbca5b28d17b7bb034595c4ce2f07b224f3a8602fdb8fb9a75.jpg)In [last week's column](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/03-13-week-in-review-march-6-13-2002) I posted a trivia question the answer to which required you to put a madness card on the stack, play a land, and then cast it. I received many emails from players and judges claiming this does not work. I was skeptical at first too, but it was checked with Paul Barclay before I posted this, and it remains a legal and correct play. Here is a letter from rules guru Dave DeLaney, explaining how this rules loophole works in detail:

To see why you can do this, it helps a lot to know exactly what is going on in madness. It's got a replacement effect, a triggered ability, and a delayed triggered ability in it... and in between the last two is the 'window' where this land-playing can, sometimes, be done. 

Say you're discarding as part of the resolution of a spell or ability, and that it's the only thing on the stack... so that once it's done resolving, the stack is empty. And say it's your Main phase, and you haven't played your land yet. If you discard a madness card, and choose to remove it from the game while you do so: madness' first ability triggers. It goes on the stack after resolution's complete (so the stack has only the madness ability on it). Time passes, and so do players... and it resolves. This affects you: specifically, you get a sticky note taped to you saying "Until you next pass, you can play this RFG-ed spell, paying its madness cost instead of ..." \[the rest isn't important right here\]. This is an effect with a duration; it's not 'something sitting on the stack'. The stack's empty right now... and a player's getting priority. And since it's your Main phase, you're that player: you now have priority. 

What can you do? Well, you could pass and end the effect (and trigger the second part of madness); we're assuming you don't, yet. You can play an ability - a mana ability or other activated ability. You can play a spell; including, but not limited to, the madness spell sitting there RFG that started all this. Or, since it's your Main phase, the stack's empty, and you haven't played a land yet... you can play a land. Playing a land doesn't involve passing, and gives you priority again afterwards... so the "until you pass" doesn't end yet, if you do this, and afterwards you could again pass, play an ability, or play a spell... including the madness spell. Say you play a mana ability: specifically, the ability of the land you just played. Now you have mana, and you get priority back; \_again\_ you can pass, play an ability, or play a spell. (You can't play a land, because you already did so this turn.) Now go ahead and play the Madness spell and hey, there's some mana floating there; hopefully it's the right kind to help pay for the spell... 

\[What you CAN'T do is play the spell, -then- try to play the land; the moment you start announcing the spell, it goes on the stack. And you can't play a land when the stack's not empty, nor can you play one during a spell's announcement...\]

### Play of the Week

Courtesy of xiao926:

![](https://i.ormos.uk/41/31/4131a4ebae1e6a689b56464f33f89c8cfdee8418ce8bff8f70d6341ec1f4e73d.jpg)"At Friday Night Magic during the finals of an OD-OD-TO draft, Sheng and John were in game 3. Sheng had a bunch of 2/2 and 3/3 creatures and attacked. His opponent John just had his only creature killed in combat. John had 2 cards in hand, a forest and an Anurid Scavenger. He also had only 1 card left in his library. John was at 8 life and would die next turn. Sheng was at 22 and looked to be in a comfortable situation. However John's last card would save him. It was Time Stretch! John played a forest and cast the Scavenger and Time Stretch. At the beginning of his next upkeep, John returned Time Stretch back to his library, drew it, and cast it again. With all of Sheng's creatures tapped, John had an infinite number of turns and continued to attack for 3 each turn eventually winning the game, the match and tournament."
