A few weeks back as I was getting ready to fly out to Japan to cover Japan Nationals tournament, *Sideboard* editor Thomas Pannell emailed me, suggesting I do an interview with a Japanese player while I was there. I had the same thing in mind about a year ago, and even wrote up some questions for Katsuhiro Mori for an interview at Worlds in Sydney. It didn’t work out at the time, since we couldn’t put aside an hour when we’d be free and could get a translator. In retrospect, this worked out pretty well. While Japanese **Magic** was interesting a year ago, the country has absolutely exploded on the **Magic** scene this season. Akihiro Kashima was the second Japanese player to come in the Top 8 of a Pro Tour at Venice, and at the same event, Mori, Kuroda and Morita of PS2 won the Team Masters. As if that wasn’t good enough, Masashi Oosio made the Finals of Pro Tour-Yokohama to pull far ahead in the Rookie of the Year race, while veteran Tsuyoshi Ikeda made the Top 4. Without a doubt, 2003 has been a huge year for Japan when it comes to **Magic: the Gathering**, which makes an interview with a Japanese player much more intriguing. Plus, despite all of Japan’s success, American and European players are still not entirely familiar with the country’s top players. I thought that a history of the game and how it evolved in Japan, as well as some insight into where it’s headed would be very interesting.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gputs02/989.jpg)

Still, there was a huge dilemma in front of me: Who do I interview? I wanted to talk to a player who had been around a long time and could paint a good picture of how **Magic** used to be like in Japan, and how it got to where it is today. I went through Japan’s Pro Tour players in my mind and couldn’t make a decision, until Ron Foster (Japan Organized Play Manager) brought up a name that surprised me a little: Ken’ichi Fujita. Fujita is a veteran player who was playing long before there were any Japanese **Magic** cards. He’s won two Grand Prix tournaments (including the first one ever held in Japan), has been on the Japanese National Team, and came one match win away from being the first Japanese player to ever Top 8 a Pro Tour back at 2000 PT-LA. 

Ron brought up Fujita as we were heading into the last round, and although I was a bit skeptical since I wasn’t very familiar with him, I covered his final match in the Swiss. In his article on Japanese **Magic** players, Gary Wise said that Fujita had been nicknamed “the Bad Guy” because he always appears to be in a bad mood when he plays. Sure enough, with Top 8 on the line, Fujita stared down his opponent and played his cards forcefully on the table, creating an intimidating aura as he won the match. For those who may be wondering, Fujita’s glower may even be better than Dave Price’s infamous “Stare of Death”. I was excited about the prospect of interviewing a player who was in the Top 8 and could possibly make the team or even become National Champion, but it was not to be: Fujita came in ninth on tiebreakers. Regardless, he was very willing to sit down with Ron and I for an interview, and provide some insight into Japanese Magic.

*Translation by Ron Foster*

**Sideboard**: How did you first get into **Magic**?

**Fujita**: I first saw an article about it in a magazine, and it looked interesting. I saw some of my friends playing, so we all started playing together. 

**Sideboard**: And when was that?

**Fujita**: *The Dark*.

**Sideboard**: Really? That was long before there were any Japanese cards.

**Fujita**: Yeah, a year, maybe a year and a half before the first Japanese set came out.

**Sideboard**: How did you and your friends learn how to play when all the cards were in English?

**Fujita**: A computer magazine called “Log Out” actually had articles about **Magic**, introductory pieces and things like that. That’s where we picked up most of what we knew about the game, and the rest just kind of involved having a dictionary in hand, trying to figure out all the cards and what they meant.

**Sideboard**: How did things change once sets started coming out in Japanese?

**Fujita**: Obviously, the first big change is that more people started to play. It was more accessible. That changed everything because it became a bigger business. More people started to play and more tournaments were run.

**Sideboard**: Why did you like **Magic**?

**Fujita**: I’ve always been a gamer. When I was in college, which was the same time I started playing **Magic**, I was playing Mahjong a lot. I like competition, so **Magic** was just an extension of that. **Magic** is a game that’s really good for competition, and it’s really exciting. I just got hooked.

**Sideboard**: Is there anything specific about the mechanics of the game that you like?

**Fujita**: More than the mechanics, the reason I’ve played **Magic** for so long is because all of my friends play too. It’s really just become a part of my life.

**Sideboard**: At what point did you decide to play competitively? Was that your intention from the start?

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/natjp03/a975.jpg)

**Fujita**: I was playing at a local hobby store, and back then when there was no Japanese edition of **Magic**, only really hardcore card shops had it. People who played **Magic** tended to hang out in the same places, since they were very few and far between. There happened to be one of those hobby stores by where I lived, so I was going there a lot, and I was playing, and I was winning. So, I was kind of the king of the hill in that little community. Someone I knew at the time, Katsuya Shiroki, who is actually working with Satoshi Nakamura at their game company; he comes up to me at the time and says “Oh, you play **Magic**?” So we started talking, and he says, “I know places where a lot of people get together regularly to play **Magic**”. That was at a McDonald’s in the Yoyogi neighborhood. So, everyone was hanging out there to play **Magic**, and in the same neighborhood, there was a store that Hobby Japan ran. They started running tournaments, and put up flyers that said something like, “Tournament on Saturday, 500 Yen” There was no Type 1 or Standard, it was just “bring your deck and play”. So, I went to that and every time there was a tournament at that store, I was there. 

**Sideboard**: What do you like better, draft of Constructed?

**Fujita**: Everyone says that I’m a Limited specialist, but I personally don’t feel that way. I play whatever the format of the event is. I don’t really have a preference. Limited is more interesting and fun, but it doesn’t mean that I don’t like Constructed or I’ll never play it. 

**Sideboard**: Do you have any advice for people on drafting?

**Fujita**: If anyone has any advice on how to become a better drafter, I’d like to hear it myself. Probably, the most important thing is to remember what you passed. This way, you can figure out what other people might have, and if you can remember when it gets back to you, you’ll know what they’re drafting.

**Sideboard**: What kind of decks do you like to play in Constructed?

**Fujita**: Black.

**Sideboard**: Why?

**Fujita**: I’ve just always liked black. I don’t want to play blue, ever. I just don’t like it at all.

**Sideboard**: Why’s that?

**Fujita**: Because it’s too hard. So it’s a process of elimination here. I don’t like blue, and green is too simple. “Here’s my big creature, attack. Oh, you killed my big creature? I’ll make another one.” That’s too boring.

**Sideboard**: What about white and red then?

**Fujita**: White is too narrow. You can’t do enough with white, its options are limited. That leaves black and red. If I can I’ll go monoblack, but I’ll often play black-red decks as well.

**Sideboard**: What do you think black offers that those other colors don’t?

**Fujita**: With the exception of a lack of enchantment removal, there isn’t much black can’t do. It has solid creatures, it has elements of control, it has ways to manipulate the deck, and ways to gain life. It has a lot of the elements of other colors. Green has big creatures? So does black. Blue can manipulate the deck? So can black. White can gain life? So can black. Of course, the best reason to play black is so that you can strip away your opponent’s hand. If your opponent has no cards, he can’t do anything.

**Sideboard**: To switch gears a little, I know that when Japanese **Magic** first started, groups consisted of four or five people together, keeping deck ideas secret. What many American and European players probably don’t know is that you started one of the first Japanese **Magic** teams, “Boss Fujita’s Gang”. What made you decide to form a team?

**Fujita**: First, I’d like to set the record straight right here. There never was a Boss Fujita’s Gang. That was actually something that was created by the mass media (laughs). It’s actually a myth, there never was a gang.

**Sideboard**: So you never had a team?

**Fujita**: Maybe there was, but I didn’t consciously set out to form a team and certainly never would have called it Boss Fujita’s Gang. 

**Sideboard**: Aside from the name, why were you motivated to move players away from the isolated mentality, and get them to work together in a bigger group?

**Fujita**: Back then, there were only two stores you could regularly go to if you wanted to play **Magic**. There was Shibuya Offbeat, which was a store that was started by the first Japanese National Champion. That’s actually what he did with his prize money. Then, there was Hyper Arena in Shinjuku, which was run by Yellow Submarine. These were the only two places that regularly ran tournaments. What happened was a division among the players. You had the Shibuya crowd, and the Shinjuku crowd. The people at Shibuya were guys like Nakamura, Maki and Ishida. That was their little club, and they were considered the top group in Japan. All the guys who were on a National team, or were going abroad to play in tournaments were in Shibuya. I lived closer to Shinjuku, and all my friends went to the Hyper Arena, so I belonged to the Shinjuku clique. There were more people in Shinjuku because they had a bigger play space, and it was a more convenient location. More people gathered there, but if you had to do a comparison and put a label on it, they were more casual players. The Shibuya group was more like a hardcore think tank, so they were the ones who were trying to process the first internet tournament reports and decklists. They were actually doing playtesting. They’d sit down, and build decks and play them against each other and try to rank cards. They were very serious about trying to find *the* best deck, and they thought of themselves as the best players, so they were a very elite group. On the other hand, Shinjuku was much more like you just get together and “Hey, I’ve got this funky *Ice Age* deck” and you just sat down and played. There was competition and tournaments and people wanted to win, but there was no “tech”. It was like when your entire collection fit into one shoebox and you’d bring it and go “oh this looks like a good card” and put it into your deck.

This is not to say that the groups were entirely separate. People would go play in the Shibuya tournaments, and guys from Shibuya would come play in the Hyper Arena tournaments. You’d talk, but there were never any real discussions about decks. No one offered information to share, and the guys from Shibuya would show up with some deck that was actually tuned, and had synergy, and usually placed first or second with it. If you went to a Shibuya tournament, that’s the way it would be. You’d come with your Orc deck or whatever, and they would just have the Necro deck. They figured out the Necro deck, and you still thought it was a crappy card, and so they would win. I was playing at Hyper Arena, and I’d finish high in the qualifiers, and I was starting to get recognized. After I lost in the finals of a Pro Tour-Dallas qualifier, I got noticed by the Shibuya crowd. They were like “Hey, didn’t you come to our store and come in third last week?” “Yeah that was me.” So they started to remember me because I was becoming a good player. Basically, what it works out to was that there never was a team at the Hyper Arena. The Shibuya guys thought of themselves as a team, so when they go to Shinjuku they’d see the same faces a lot. I’m pretty large, and I was one of the better players there. I seemed to be looked up to by the other players, and they’d often play the same decks as me. So the Shibuya guys thought, “He must be the leader of the Shinjuku team”. They started calling it Boss Fujita’s Gang. It was really something that was invented. It’s nothing I ever thought of myself. The team existed in the minds of some people, but it was never formally organized. Then, for whatever reason, it took on a life of its own. 

**Sideboard**: I heard that the “Boss” nickname has stuck.

**Fujita**: I can’t figure out why people still call me that. Even when I’ve tried to explain to them “there never was a Gang, I was never the Boss, stop calling me that!” they still call me Boss Fujita. I don’t like it.

**Sideboard**: You have a very stern attitude when you play. Is that meant for intimidation, or is there something else going on?

**Fujita**: It’s nothing intentional at all. 

**Sideboard**: Is the “Bad Guy” personality an extension of who you are, or is it something that just comes from competition?

**Fujita**: Again, it’s not intentional. It’s not meant for intimidation or to put pressure on my opponents. It’s probably something that when I’m playing intently and focusing on my game, I just get that demeanor. Because I have that reputation though, of being The Bad Guy, I often try to joke with my opponent or talk to them, and just be very casual. 

**Sideboard**: Well, I was just watching your final match, and from my perspective you were playing your cards on the table forcefully, and were staring down your opponent with this look of, “How dare you even think you can beat me?”

**Fujita**: I don’t really remember what happened during the match. I was just letting the game play itself. 

**Sideboard**: I heard a story that you won the first ever Japanese Grand Prix, and broke the trophy by accident. After that happened, you talked to Andrew Finch to see if you could get a replacement, and he told you to “Win another Grand Prix”, so you did. Was the trophy actually motivation for you?

**Fujita**: I don’t remember Andrew Finch actually saying that to me.

**Foster (translator)**: I can confirm that actually did happen, because I was at the Grand Prix and on the stage, and was the one who actually interpreted that exchange.
