As far as tournaments without American players go, it doesn't get any bigger than Euros. Each year, The finest players this side of the ocean (as I write this from London) gather to take home their share of what this year amounts to $50,000 in prize money, a little glory and a little more fame. This year, though, seems special.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptnor01/a933.jpg)

With National pride having been put on the line of late thanks to the World Cup, the importance of the three letters in brackets annotating a player's nationality seem a little more important. It isn't just Kai Budde, it's Kai Budde (Germany) who will be competing this year, potentially at the same time his country is trying to capture the World Cup, and with him, a number of the world's top stars including reigning World Champion Tom Van de Logt (Netherlands) and reigning European Champion Eivind Nitter (Norway) will be going for gold as well.

Euros, like most National Championships, will be using two formats: Limited (*Odyssey-Torment-Judgment* booster draft) and pre-*Judgment* Standard. Most players have had ample time to prepare themselves for both formats, with Standard still devoid of those *Judgment* cards that are sure to shake up the environment for Worlds. With these two somewhat established environments, you'd have to think that Budde, established as best player in the world in the minds of most, is the favorite coming in.

With a win at Pro Tour-New Orleans and a Top 8 finish at PT Nice, Kai has had success in both Limited and Constructed this year. He won his National Championship and if [Misetings.com](https://web.archive.org/web/20020627000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.misetings.com/modules.php?op=modload&name=News&file=article&sid=425&origin=sideboard_ww_20020627) is to be believed, the JSS also, and with all of his impressive accomplishments, his resume is still missing a European Championship. That may make him bear down a little harder come tournament time.

In addition to his obvious dominance, this will be an interesting tournament to watch Budde's results in because of his recent split from Godzilla. Working with a local team including Patrick Mello, Christian Luehrs, Marco Blume, Dirk Baberowski and Hamburg's other top pros, it will be interesting to see how Kai will perform without the stable of decks his old team had provided. With the Standard environment as established as it is, it shouldn't make a huge difference come Sunday. Here's a rundown of the other favorites:

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptnor01/a865.jpg)

**Punisher** - Both Tomi Walamies and Jens Thoren, then relatively unknown, made it to the Top 8 of last year's event in Milan, and while neither made the final, it was the start of something big. Since then, Walamies has made a PT final and won the Finish National Championship, Thoren a final and a semifinal and Anton Jonsson has twin Top 8 finishes in Constructed and Limited and the Swedish National Championship on his resume. With a strong crew of players and deckbuilders behind this trio, Punisher should be there in the end, though which members could make for interesting viewing as they'll likely have to square off at some point in time.

**Norway** - You can't argue with history, can you? Last year, Eivind Nitter was crowned champion with his finals win over Dave Montreuil. Three years ago, it was Nico Herzog taking home the title, and the year before that it was Sturla Bingen who won the first ever European Championship... three for four isn't too shabby. With all of the usual suspects, from Oyvind Odegaard to Sigurd Eskeland to Bjorn Jocumson likely to make the trip, another strong showing from the Norwegians seems entirely possible. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/a6/85/a685a7f8dee8ec924083c2f13a7a72ad01b8ed83c4fb2811b12cda90c9cb4533.jpg)

**The Dutch** - No one, not even Kai himself, has been as good at tournaments involving both Limited and Constructed as Tom Van de Logt of late. He won last year's World Championship, made Top 8 in the 2000 World Championships in Belgium, has won multiple National Championships and narrowly missed the Top 8 a year ago. That's a frightening level of consistency. Noah Boeken is the only non-Norwegian to ever win the event, and while the Dutch, similarly to the Americans, are essentially draft specialists, Standard's established metagame bodes well for them as they likely won't be forced to build anything new. Kamiel Cornelissen and Jelger Weigersma will also have the home field advantage, more accustomed to Amsterdam's frills and thrills than most visiting luminaries. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/38/b9/38b9acfcb7124249113d18481d302b1982da1723bac18a75a3a2c4d3affb4aaa.gif)**France** - If you were to change the results of some half dozen matches throughout Magic history, France might be considered on par with the Americans. Lestree's loss to Loconto at New York 1, Hernandez' loss to Blumke at 1996 Worlds, Levy's loss to Boeken at 2000 Euros… results like these led to France's status as also-rans instead of champions, but Farid Meranghi finally ended the victory drought with his win at San Diego and France has been on the rise all year. Les Plus Classe (Amiel Tanenbaum, Gabriel Nassif and Nicolas Olivieri) took second place at New York, Farid won San Diego and floodgates opened with four Frenchmen making Top 8 at Osaka for what has to be considered France's best season to date. With so many players doing well right now (Raphael Levy, Franck Canu, Christophe Haim, Sylvain Lauriol, Pierre Malherbaud and, of course, the Ruel Brothers), where France's challenges will come from is anyone's guess.

**England** - With the amicable split with American and German teammates completed, Hampton Court Palace has teamed itself with some of England's lesser knowns as a warm up for World Championships. With the core of Ben Ronaldson and John Ormerod still intact, Ollie Schneider, Craig Jones, Chris Clapton, Pete Norris, Kevin O'Connor and Sam Gomersoll, all with multiple Pro Tours, will be joining me in Amsterdam. This team doesn't look as strong as those listed above on the surface, but with what may still be the world's best deckbuilding team up in Ormerod/Ronaldson, it will be interesting to see what this team can produce.

### Going Back

One thing I like about the coming of this time of year is the reminiscences it forces upon me. I can't help but look at Euros, for example, as not only this year's tournament, but also as the latest chapter in the story of European Championships. Here's a reminder of all that came before it:

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/EURO01/831.jpg)

**2001** - This tournament served as a caution of things to come. Eivind Nitter, who walked away with the crown, would go on to win Pro Tour-Nice less than a year later. Jens Thoren and Tomi Walamies, both of Team Punisher, would go on to have the best seasons of their respective careers after both Top 8'd in Milan. John Ormerod's 6th place finish foreshadowed his 8th place finish at Worlds in Toronto, while Sturla Bingen, a name from the past, was able to manage one more Top 8 finish. The biggest story of this tournament though had to be Dave Montreuil, a Canadian-turned-Swiss music student who had come out of nowhere to win his National Championships. Montreuil took a train to Milan where he slept on park benches en route to a miraculous second-place finish worth $10,000, thanks in part to a monored deck with Ensnaring Bridge in the sideboard. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/GPTUR01/boeken.jpg)

**2000** - Noah Boeken fulfilled all of the promise and hype with his win in Paris. Playing the same Stompy deck teammate Ryan Fuller won Canadian Championships with, Noah defeated home-nation favorite Raphael Levy and his Replenish deck in a very close five game final. It was Levy's second consecutive Top 8 finish in this event, while Wolfgang Eder notched his first of two consecutive Top 8 finishes at Euros. Eventual Grand Prix winner Rickard Osterberg also made it to the semifinals.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/Worlds2001/985.jpg)

**1999** - The summer of 1999 brought about Kai Budde's emergence as one of the greats, but it also trumpeted Nicolai Herzog's arrival. The surprise winner of the Norwegian National Championships, Nico went on to capture the European crown with a win over Dirk Baberowski and his innovative Gamekeeper deck in a powerhouse Top 8 that included Raphael Levy, Tommi Hovi, Pierre Malherbaud and Mattias Jorstedt, all in the comfort of Berlin. Nico would go on to make the Top 8 at Worlds that year playing the same Stompy deck that carried him to his Euros success.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/Worlds2001/891.jpg)

**1998** - The first European Championship, held in Brussels, featured two of the game's early European greats facing off in the final. Sturla Bingen defeated former World Champion Jakub Slemr in a final that pitted two Draw-Go decks against one another, with the Norwegian emerging victorious and establishing a trend for the next few years of Norwegian dominance.

### Mini-Colony

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/magic_colony.jpg)

Magic Colony was one of the more interesting political developments on the Pro Tour year, and while that team's success outside of Ken Ho was questionable, its example is about to be followed in a big way for World Championships. Deciding that locking yourself and a couple of friends up for five or six weeks is a good thing, Eric Froehlich, who in the last year has been playing **Magic** Online five or six hours a day and catapulted himself into the consensus top five **Magic** players in the United States, has taken it upon himself to host Brian Kibler and Ben Rubin in his home for an extended period of time leading up to World Championships in the hopes the trio, who have worked well together in the past, can exploit the two new Constructed formats (*Judgment*-Standard and *Odyssey-Torment-Judgment* Block) being played in Sydney, and hopefully break them.

The team, which is being called Mini-Colony, like its predecessor, will furnish the *Sideboard* with articles on their progress, but what makes this group interesting is the international flavor they'll be taking to Worlds. While Brian Hegstad, who has withdrawn for World Championships, was originally supposed to be their fourth, Mini-Colony started using their internet ties (all three are frequent users of IRC) in search of a group of players they could exchange their findings with. The feeling was that three players just wouldn't be enough to break two Constructed formats, with the inbred thought processes inspired by close living ties potentially limiting their creative capabilities. Froehlich, Kibler and Rubin started sending out feelers, developing an idea of who they might want to work with for the biggest tournament of the year.

One of those people, not surprisingly, was Kai Budde. Kai, as mentioned earlier, had recently broken off from Godzilla, taking the German contingent with him. He'd approached Team Punisher in the hopes of working with them, but they decided they'd been doing well enough without outside help and wanted to stay the course they'd laid out. It was around this time that Kai and Eric had that first talk that would eventually bring their groups together.

The Germans will be holding their own Mini-Colony on this side of the Atlantic while the Americans do the same, with the German contingent being Budde, Patrick Mello, Felix 'Flix' Schneiders and Austrians Ben Klauser and Helmut Summersberger. With such a massive store of talent and deck-builder extraordinaire Dirk Baberowski (who is likely to miss Worlds) combining efforts with the Kibler/Rubin tandem, this team could very easily produce this year's World Champion, and should probably enter the tournament as favorites.
