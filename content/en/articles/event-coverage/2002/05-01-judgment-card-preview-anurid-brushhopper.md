*Judgment* means to balance out the injustice green and white have suffered at the hands of *Torment's* black, and Anurid Brushhopper leads the charge. This cheap fatty with a powerful and subtle ability is going to warm the hearts of a lot of players, and is well suited to both beatdown and control strategies. It's not powerful enough to unbalance any formats, but we should definitely see some decks built around it.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/43/2d/432d4e04319105e039322b14e4e32d7648cd3477ae99f236ae4a4eaa8326786d.jpg)As a rare, it won't show up too often in Limited, but let's start our analysis there. A 3/4 vanilla creature for ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Green Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/d9/d5/d9d556256faf839099c06019302393fab57e6e9a77769d6f6813a7c961c4cb59.gif)![White Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif) would be a high pick in draft, but the Brushhopper's ability is what pushes it up to first-pick status and making it worth splashing.

Some of the Brushhopper's combat tricks are fairly obvious. Your opponents won't be able to let a 3-power creature go unblocked for long, and when they gang up on him you can put damage on the stack and save him. He'll even come back untapped - like the police, his motto is to protect and to serve. The Brushhopper can also give you threshold for no mana cost, although you have to take him out of the game to do so. And, of course, he dodges removal spells all day - any time a spell would remove the Brushhopper, you have the option of changing the spell's name to Bog Down, no kicker. How tricky! And it's not even blue!

*Judgment*, however, adds a new set of potential discard tricks that the Brushhopper will love. First we have the Incarnations, somewhat overcosted creatures that provide a benefit once they're in your graveyard. In some cases, these cards are worth more dead than alive (and certainly worth more dead than in your hand), and they can get nasty with any effect that lets you discard. Put Valor in the graveyard, for example, and suddenly your army has first strike.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/37/eb/37eb4bdc2f4ebf5a322bcb90be72a78ffe5107b0bce10e63c681ad22eab5a667.jpg)Another little trick is Prismatic Strands, an instant that prevents all damage done by sources of a color of your choice for the rest of the turn. At ![2 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif)![White Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/55/78/55785924f8cda9ab2a85364f807ba172c7530e43d94b14aad222ec4bbf2589ca.gif) it's a decent spell already, but its flashback cost is the mere tapping of a creature of your choice. That means you can discard it, and before the Brushhopper heads off to RFG land, you can tap him to pay for it. Won't it be nice to know that you can tap out and have an emergency escape hatch if the Squirrel army facing you suddenly beefs up with Overrun or Rites of Initiation?

Where Brushhopper will really make an impact is in Constructed, both Standard and in the upcoming Block PTQs. Whenever we look at a creature whose ability isn't game swinging on its own, we have to start with the basic stats. Are they enough? The Brushhopper's stats make it a serious card in its casting cost. 3/3s for 3 have often been playable even with slight drawbacks, and a lot of decks can't easily deal with a 3/4 creature on turn two (courtesy of a turn-one Bird or Elf). The fact that Brushhopper fights with Elephant tokens and wins is certainly not trivial.

However, it's the Brushhopper's special ability that pushes him over the top. Mana cost is often the dominant variable in Constructed formats, and cards like Force of Will have long demonstrated the value of being able to do things for zero mana. Brushhopper's ability to dodge trouble makes him a powerhouse that both beatdown and control decks can be built around.

For a beatdown deck, the Brushhopper is an early threat that never goes away. He's not cheap enough to get by permission on his own, but he'll sometimes do it anyway with the help of a mana creature. If he doesn't, Wild Mongrel and Basking Rootwalla will put on enough pressure to make the control player do something about them, at which point the Brushhopper can come out to play.

Once he's out, the Brushhopper is a nightmare for control decks. Two cards isn't cheap, but madness and flashback help and it doesn't take long for three points a turn to add up. Alone he probably won't do the job, but with a little help from his friends the Brushhopper can go all the way. It's also worth noting that the Brushhopper is fantastic against Upheaval, currently one of the most important cards in the control player's arsenal.

This Brushhopper deck borrows some ideas from the PTQ Jank deck from a year ago (designed by Flores and Senhouse):

4 Birds of Paradise  
 4 Llanowar Elves  
 4 Basking Rootwalla  
 4 Wild Mongrel  
 4 Noble Panther  
 4 Anurid Brushhopper  
 4 Arrogant Wurm  
 4 Call of the Herd  
 4 Armadillo Cloak  
 2 Beast Attack  
 4 Brushland  
 2 Sungrass Prairie  
 4 Plains  
 12 Forest

This deck is pretty single-minded. With eight mana creatures on turn one, you should play a 3-power threat on turn two pretty consistently. With both Mongrel and Brushhopper, casting your Arrogant Wurms shouldn't be too difficult. Against a control deck, you have enough continuing pressure to put them on the defensive and get them low enough that a Brushhopper can finish the job. Against a beatdown deck, your cheap fatties should stand well in the early to mid game, and a single Armadillo Cloak can finish things off. Violent Eruption and Flametongue Kavu make four the magic number for toughness, and a Cloak puts all of your offensive creatures out of range.
