Anyone who knows almost anything about me knows that, in general, I’m a night person. It’s not really so much that I prefer being awake at night, but rather that I stay up pretty much as long as I have something to do or someone to talk to. This gets really dangerous when friends of mine are spread out across time zones, because it often means that I’ll never get any sleep. What sleep I do get, then, I savor to the utmost. I even have the windows in my room in my fraternity house covered with tapestries so light doesn’t shine in and wake me up. This all leads to one very important detail that is quite relevant to Pro Tour and Grand Prix events: I hate alarm clocks with a passion that burns like the fires of a thousand suns. 

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i> I was not what one would call a “happy camper”…</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

So, when the room alarm went off, and then my cell phone alarm chimed in right afterwards, I was not what one would call a “happy camper”. Of course, that’s the price one has to pay for making Day 2 and, quite frankly, the main reason that missing out on Top 64 after making the cut at a Pro Tour is so incredibly frustrating. It’s not so much that I played a whole day of Magic and didn’t make money – it’s that I woke up early and didn’t get anything for it. I’m quite excited about the prospect of playing Magic full-time once I graduate, in large part, because I’ll almost never have to wake up to an alarm. Yes, I know I have messed up priorities. 

I decide better of going back to sleep, though, shower and get dressed for the tournament. I throw on my In the Gruv – Surreal Sunday shirt, which was from a party thrown in Atlanta the week before on Sunday night since most people had Monday off for President’s Day. I didn’t, but I went anyway and I just thought I’d give a shout out to my friends back in the Atlanta party scene by wearing the shirt. Hey, maybe Sikora will give me free entry sometime for giving him the publicity. It was all the more appropriate because I was listening to one of his demo CDs – Turtle Tracks – throughout the entire tournament. The man can spin his breaks. [www.djsikora.com](https://web.archive.org/web/20030303000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=%20www.djsikora.com&origin=sideboard_strategy_20030303a) – check him out.

Unfortunately, I find that somehow my headphones have mysteriously broken overnight, so I grab my backup earplug phones and wait for Ben to get ready. For some reason, he decides that he wants to run over to the site, so despite waiting for him, I end up walking over alone. I’m still on time, of course, with the seats for deckbuilding not even posted for at least ten minutes after we get there. After filling out the tax information forms with the usual “ON FILE”, I head over to table one to get my draft on.

I’m being fed by Darling and feeding Breider. I hadn’t talked to Darling before the draft, so I didn’t know any of his preferences, but I knew going in that Breider liked green and white and was even willing to draft g-w if the situation arose. I didn’t have enough of a bead on the format to formulate a draft plan, really, but I was very familiar with all of the pre-Legions archetypes, particularly those including white and I was told that many players were avoiding white like the plague, so I was willing to give it a shot.

My first pack fits right into this plan, giving me a Gustcloak Savior which I picked over Centaur Glade. I think I’m one of the few people who doesn’t like Centaur Glade that much; it’s certainly a powerful card, but it’s tremendously slow. Savior, on the other hand, is absolutely incredible, making it impossible for your opponent to force you into a disadvantageous block and allowing you to just alpha strike until they’re dead without losing a single creature. I was more than happy to first pick it.

The white kept coming for the next few picks and I managed to pick up a red spell here and there to complement it. Unfortunately, the white started drying up and I got the impression that Darling moved into the color after starting out drafting something else. Afterwards, I learned that this was in fact the case and that he got some incredibly late Pacifisms and such to convince him to switch. When we started going the other direction with pack two, I could immediately tell that Breider did, in fact, end up drafting g-w. I ended up with the scraps, but still managed to salvage something resembling a deck going into Legions, if only because of the red-free window Breider provided me with. 

Legions was where suddenly I ended up with a playable deck. I first picked a Wingbeat Warrior, which wasn’t especially exciting, but then got shipped Lavaborn Muse and Rockshard Elemental in quick succession, which went right into my stack. I rounded things out with some beaters like Daru Mender and Deftblade Elite, all in all ending up with nothing especially impressive, but certainly something I felt like I could win with. 

### Round 10 – Aaron Breider

I’d played Aaron once before, I think, back at PT-Barcelona, if my memory serves me correctly. He’s clearly a fairly solid player, at least, but in this match, I was not especially impressed by his play. Game one, though, he didn’t have to play especially well to win, as he mana stumbled and I committed a huge number of creatures to the board in order to attack around his Improvised Armored creature. His reason for drafting g-w was soon clear as Akroma told me she has some beef with my team and soon they’re all in the graveyard. Things ended quickly from there.

Game two, I know to play around Wrath, but once again, I’m forced to commit a sizable force to the board and it goes to the graveyard just as quickly. I had reserves this time, however and my Lavaborn Muse came down when he was low on cards to take sizable bites out of his life total. Lavaborn Muse is simply amazing – if you play it when your opponent has one or no cards in hand, they either have to remove it immediately or try to hold their board position without playing out cards in their hand. I had enough pressure on Aaron that he couldn’t afford to let his board position slip any further, so the Muse got in a few points here and there. I drew Savior and soon I was swinging with my entire team every turn, despite him playing out Jareth – not a bad second white rare. As I didn’t have to fear his Wrath anymore and he couldn’t effectively maintain a defensive position against my Savior and Muse, pretty soon Aaron’s defenses crumbled and he was going back to his sideboard.

Game three revolved around one card: Daru Mender. I drew it fairly early and knew that it was going to win me the game, so I played it face down and never used it or risked it in combat. Aaron played out his entire hand to try to stop my creature assault, and I dropped my Lavaborn Muse that I’d been holding for just this situation. Two turns later, Aaron peeled his Vengeance, but Akroma has much more in common with Nevinyrral than she does with God and my Mender flipped face up to regenerate my Muse, much to Aaron’s dismay. Two turns later, he died a flaming death.

Matches 9-1, Games 13-3

### Round 11 – Joshua Wagener

Josh had just taken down my partner in crime, Ben Rubin and was sitting pretty at 10-0. Well, I just couldn’t have that. Ben told me Josh was playing u-g, with at least one Echo Tracer and at least one Willbender, as he’d been wrecked by each of them in one of their games. I refused to let that happen to me.

Clash of the titans, take one. I’d never played Ben in a sanctioned event prior to this one and amusingly enough, we’d meet twice more before the day ended. The draft started off with a bit of a hitch to begin with, as my general plan – especially when drafting next to a teammate – is to draft white. My opening pack had literally no white cards I’d even consider putting into a deck and what is worse is that there were no black or red cards either. The only playables were Snarling Undorak and Riptide Replicator and I’m not even all that happy to play the artifact in draft. In sealed deck, it’s fine, since it can break the creature stalemates that the slower format often grinds down to, but it costs two turns and loads of mana before it has any impact on the board, making it less than effective in the faster draft format. I took the Undorak and hoped I could cut the green well enough that Ben didn’t end up drafting it behind me.

That plan worked fairly well, as I received a Wirewood Savage next, followed up by a Spitting Gourna, Vitality Charm and Treespring Lorian. Green was looking pretty open for me and it looked like I was managing to avoid pass-and-cutting Ben, so I was pretty happy. I picked up an Erratic Explosion and a Wave of Indifference to start in on another color and by the end of the first pack, things were looking alright.

Pack two, however, wasn’t quite so hot. I stuck with green, but didn’t see much and got lured in by an Infest Ben shipped to me second. I was thinking it was possible that EJ to my right is r-b, since I didn’t see much of either in the first pack, but Infest is good enough in g-b that I figured it might be worth the switch. I was thinking g-b for most of the rest of the pack, but I still picked up a second Wave of Indifference quite late, so I had the option of going back to g-r if I so decided. I picked up a Tribal Unity somewhat late over a Wirewood Elf - which EFro thinks was a mistake, but I’m not so sure. Wirewood Elf seems less important given the two drops in Legions and Tribal Unity can just auto-win otherwise unwinnable games in an Overrun-like way. It certainly did just that for me at one point during this draft. Ahhh… foreshadowing.

Coming into Legions, I didn’t have quite enough cards to play either g-b or g-r unless I got a good chunk of playables. I picked up a first pick Timberwatch Elf and a bunch of big beasts like Needleshot Gournas and Brontotherium. Nothing exciting, but enough filler to put together a deck. I passed up a Crashing Craghorn, thinking I was going to end up g-b, but came to regret it when it came time for deck construction and I realized my black just wasn’t worth it. I went with the traditional green plan – a bunch of fatties to block and Waves to eventually win. Hey, it worked.

### Round 13 – Lucas Glavin

Lucas has apparently been on a bit of a tear in the Boston area as of late, winning their half of the Grudge Match (and, it would seem, the finals against Neutral Ground – congrats for breaking their streak, Lucas). I’d played him a few times in drafts a ways back at Your Move Games and hadn’t been impressed, but I’m sure the same could be said about the me from not long ago. Apparently he’s been doing the Magic Online thing quite a bit which, as we’ve seen, can do wonders for your game.

Lucas is g-b, which made for a rather boring game one. We pounded on each other a bit early and then started amassing huge armies that neither of us could attack through. Game two was equally as uninteresting, but this time because I stalled on land and Lucas didn’t. He was playing fatties while mine were stuck in my hand and soon enough, we were shuffling up for the decider.

As it happened, I was able to attack back with all of my creatures and – despite Lucas flipping Skinthinner to kill one of my unblocked men – Tribal Unity on insects to do something like 24 damage to win the game and the match.

### Round 14 – Ben Rubin

Clash of the Titans, take two. Ben had been sitting to my left during the draft and had ended up with a solid r-w deck featuring Insurrection and Slice and Dice – what’s up with the Master and his bombs? In any case, I felt like I had a good matchup considering my deck’s concentration of fat creatures, though Insurrection would give him a solid shot at taking games that drag on too long before I can Wave him out. 

Game one went precisely as normal draws between our decks ought to go – he got in some early damage until I reached my six mana flashpoint and started dropping beasts. I Waved with damage to spare before he could Insurrection and we were on to game two. Ben got quite the aggressive start this game and I couldn’t seem to find my lands. I started to stabilize, but Ben had his own Wave to end it.

Game three was back and forth. I established significant creature superiority, with a Snarling Undorak, Needleshot Gourna, and Timberwatch Elf to his pair of morphs. Clash of the Titans, take three. So we meet again. Ben had heard the murmurings about how good my deck was and even said at one point during the match “I’m not sure who I want to win this”, since it was clear I had a better chance of going on to win the whole tournament. Quite frankly, if I was going to lose to anyone in any tournament, I’d want it to be Ben, but I was sick of losing to him that day. If I was going to pull one out, I was going to make it the one that counts – in the single elimination rounds. My mother told me that she’d been cheering for both of us when she watched our match during the Swiss, but at this point, she just wanted me to win and started rooting against Ben. Apparently, it worked. 

This match is covered [here](/en/events/2003/gpbos03/quarterfinals-brian-kibler-vs-ben-rubin) on the Sideboard, but the reporter missed a few key details – like the fact that I missed my third land drop in game one and STILL won. Ben even managed to dispatch my quadruple amplified Daru Stinger with Cruel Revival, but it didn’t matter. My deck was THAT good. 

Matches 14-3, Games 22-10

### Semifinals – Mark Zadjner

Before this match, I had multiple players come up to me and tell me various things. Some people told me that I was going to lose, because his deck was absurd, featuring double Shapeshifter and double dragon. Some told me that I COULDN’T lose – not because I had some sort of huge advantage, but just because they couldn’t bear to see Zadjner win. Everyone was in agreement that whoever won our match would go on to win the Grand Prix, however, since our decks were simply in a different league than the rest of the Top 8. I told them that I would win, then, because there was no way \*I\* was going to lose to dragons. 

Mike Flores covered our match quite well on the Sideboard [here](/en/events/2003/gpbos03/semifinals-mark-zajdner-v-brian-kibler). Game one, I simply had a draw that wasn’t aggressive enough to beat his start and I rolled over to his superior fliers. Game two was quite the opposite, as a huge Daru Stinger showed up very early in the game and I was way too far ahead in tempo for him to ever catch up.

It all came down, then, to game three. Actually, it really all came down to one critical turn. Zadjner had just activated his Riptide Shapeshifter and fetched Quicksilver Dragon and offered his deck to me to shuffle and cut. I handed it over to the table judge, Jeremy Smith, who gave it a quick shuffle and offered it to me. I said “I trust Jeremy – go ahead and draw”. Apparently my faith in Jeremy was unfounded, however, as he managed to shuffle Lavamancer’s Skill to the top of Zadjner’s deck. With my team consisting entirely of two toughness creatures, it seemed like the game was all but over at this point, with a Mistform Wall just waiting to turn into a creature killing machine on Zadjner’s side of the board.

Mark had other plans, however, and Skilled up his Mistform Seaswift. I can’t imagine how I kept anything resembling a poker face when he then shot down my Gustcloak Harrier, but he seemed genuinely surprised on the following turn when I took out his game-winning creature with Crown of Suspicion. His reasoning was that he was playing around Swat, so he didn’t want to enchant the wall, but I only had one black mana available and he’d already seen the Crown as well. Even then, if he’d just killed Whipcorder rather than Gustcloak Harrier, he probably would’ve had enough time to kill me with his dragon before I drew another Whipcorder – but instead, he said something to the effect of “Brian, I know how much you want to win this tournament, so here you go – enjoy your trophy”. Quite generous of him, really.

Matches 15-3, Games 24-11

### Finals – Matthew Cory

Matt was apparently quite surprised just to be sitting down across from me. I suppose I would be too, in his position, since the Finals of a Grand Prix is hardly what you expect from your first sanctioned Limited tournament. Right before I sat down for the match, my mother made a special request: “beat him quickly so we can go eat”. I almost mentioned that to Matt during some of the turns when he sank deep into thought, but I figured that might be construed as somewhat unsportsmanlike.

After the nail-biter of my last match, the final was almost relaxing. At no point in either game did I really feel like I was in any position to lose, as you can probably tell from the coverage [here](/en/events/2003/gpbos03/finals-brian-kibler-vs-matt-cory). In game one, I even went so far as to play around pretty much every mass removal spell known to man and eventually finished things off with Akroma. Game two was even more lopsided, if that’s possible and I dropped the big Angel on the last turn, even though it was simply gratuitous. Because really, it’s all about style.

Matches 16-3, Games 26-11

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gpbos03/871.jpg)After the tournament finished, I went to a victory dinner with my mother and the big trophy I was carrying around was the subject of more than a few questions. People even honked at me as they were driving by in their cars and gave me the thumbs up. It was a really good feeling. It’s been almost six years since my last big tournament win, back at the third Grand Prix ever in Toronto in 1997 – so I’m now the answer to the trivia question “who has the longest stretch between Grand Prix wins?” I’m sure that little tidbit will make Mark Rosewater happy. I know winning makes me happy. I like it. I think I’ll do it more often.
