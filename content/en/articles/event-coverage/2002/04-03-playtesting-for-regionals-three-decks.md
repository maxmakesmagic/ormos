It's coming. Just over the horizon, it marks the day of reckoning for thousands of **Magic** players across the country, playing for prizes, bragging rights, and eight coveted invitations to Orlando, Florida, to compete with the nation's best for a spot on the U.S. National Team. It's April 13th, the day of the U.S. Regional Championships, and perhaps the single most immense day of **Magic** throughout the year. Last year's Regional Championships saw players come out of the woodwork in droves, driving tournaments to eight, nine, or even ten rounds of Swiss, testing the endurance as well as the skills of the competitors. This year promises to be no different.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>For a tournament as huge and unpredictable as Regionals, nothing is simple.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

The U.S. Regional Championships are very interesting, as they are some of the first large-scale tournaments in the new Standard environment. Granted, there are JSS challenges and sporadic Regional Championships in other parts of the world that provide a glimpse into what is to come, but U.S. Regionals are truly what defines the metagame for the Standard environment in their wake. Because of this, testing for Regionals is much like testing for a Pro Tour. Unlike PTQ seasons, where metagame shifts from week to week can be tracked as results come in from all over the internet, Regionals is a single day of competition - only one chance to get it right.

Making the best of that once chance takes preparation. For a tournament like Regionals, however, preparation doesn't mean sitting in the local card shop and playing your r-g deck against your friend's Psychatog deck for hours at a time. It doesn't mean figuring out what beats the two or three decks you might come up against over the course of the tournament. For a tournament as huge and unpredictable as Regionals, nothing is simple. Preparation for a relatively unknown field means determining what the most powerful strategies available in the format are and figuring out why they win, why they lose, and how best to exploit their strengths and weaknesses. 

Sounds complicated, doesn't it? That's because it is. Thankfully, however, this process does not occur in a vacuum, and tidbits of useful information can be found in even the most unexpected of places. I've done my best to locate, extract, and evaluate this data in an attempt to consolidate it into an overview that should hopefully keep you from having to do quite as much grunt work yourselves. This article will not go into great detail about the nuances of any particular deck or strategy, but rather will provide you with a solid basis from which to work to make your own choices for your regional championships. 

### Red-Green Beatdown

![](https://i.ormos.uk/18/5e/185e7e318427f84c96bea9f51b1f21e9072b32c5ab59b80ef3c558e66c2e38e9.jpg)

First things first - the beatdown. Last year it was Fires-fueled Blastoderms and Saproling Bursts that mauled the unprepared - this year it's Wild Mongrel, Flametongue Kavu, and their ever growing herd of elephantine friends who will separate the quick from the dead. This unruly menagerie promises to punish the unprepared, and in the game of **Magic**, you don't have the saving grace of only having to outrun your slowest companions to survive. It's you against the beasts, and you had better be ready.

Were there Vegas odds on what decktype would qualify the most players for Nationals, I'd put a substantial sum of money on r-g to top the list. This isn't to say that I think it's the best deck available, or that it's what I'd play if I were competing, but r-g beatdown has all the characteristics of a successful Regionals deck. It's consistent, straightforward, and powerful - a sleek combination of some of the most efficient cards in the environment. With the ability to take the advantage quickly with Llanowar Elves and Birds of Paradise to accelerate threats and the tools to maintain and extend that advantage in the form of direct damage and the format-defining Flametongue Kavu, r-g can never truly be counted out.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/a4/66/a4662692f3afa80eeff7f490ee71b304fa3ed34e3ba37073f1d18a90fdcef4f8.jpg)

A further attractive feature of r-g is its ability to punish poor draws and give opponents little time to implement their own strategies. Much like the Sligh decks of old, which fought through Chills and Circles of Protection in their heyday, r-g beatdown can power its way past sideboard cards like Teferi's Moat and Worship through sheer speed. In the immortal words of Dave Price that have oft been repeated - there are no wrong threats, only wrong answers. It doesn't matter whether you're beating them down with a Wild Mongrel, an elephant token, or a Raging Kavu - their life total is going down to zero all the same, while those silver bullet sideboard cards are sitting quietly somewhere in your opponent's deck.

The possibilities for r-g beatdown are wide and varied, as there are a plethora of cards available for the strategy. While all have the capacity for success in a particular field - from ultra-fast weenie decks packed with burn to neo-Fires decks with Kavu Titans and other large creatures, those that are most likely to succeed in Regionals are probably somewhere in the median of these extremes. While Raging Goblins and Yavimaya Barbarians may put a hurting on slow control decks, they're going to look pretty silly if you run up against r-g decks with larger creatures, and a field like Regionals is more likely to punish such extreme metagame choices than it is to reward it. 

So for those who want to engage in some good old fashioned beatdown, this is the deck for you. For the rest of you - be sure you're ready, or you'll just become more meat for the carnivores. To beat r-g, be sure your deck can withstand an early creature rush, and don't rely too heavily on specific cards like Worship or Pernicious Deed as catch-all solutions. Beatdown's advantage lies in its consistency and redundancy, and without consistent means with which to halt the inevitable assault, you're doomed to fall before it.

### Black Beatdown

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/70/24/702418a3d6882df4eef58bbd9bf395e4deb98633c2e001e2a041e5fed8eaca68.jpg"><br><i>Dodging the hate is going to be tough for black.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

The other most prominent of aggressive strategies right now is base black, splash colors optional. I focused on one permutation of this design in my last article, and am still of the opinion that the red splash version is the one with the most raw power. Flametongue Kavu's ability to swing games is hard to surpass, and while other colors offer many attractive options, none can quite live up to the lofty standard set by FTK. This having been said, however, it is likely that the red version of the day may not be the correct choice for Regionals. Were the metagame largely open, as it was when I designed that version of the deck, I would stick to the red. As things are, however, with the metagame defined in large part by *Torment*-powered black decks, one can expect to face a great deal of hate that might not otherwise be present.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/04/d7/04d72164c3fe7ac43dc3b5a578612b446f34892be4c724744ab9a27d16fb796d.jpg"><br><i>You better believe she'll be breaking hearts at Regionals</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

While it is possible for the red-splash deck to win games despite cards like Circle of Protection: Black, Compost, and Teferi's Moat, it's an arduous task, and with the mirror match likely to be a very significant factor, it's probably a better metagame choice to have ways to deal with these hate cards, as well as to have access to some of them yourself. As Alex Shvartsman's [recent article](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/03-26-regionals-tech-noir) shows, white provides access to a number of powerful options, most notably Vindicate. While Alex's build seems to be a perfectly viable option, I'm more inclined to keep the splash to a bare minimum, to avoid mana difficulties, as Verdict and Spectral Lynx don't add a great deal to the deck that can't be found in black. Neutral Ground Grudge Match repeat champion Eric Ziegler played a b-w Braids build much more to my liking, preserving the fundamental core of the deck while using white solely for Vindicate and sideboard cards. Ziegler chose to play four Circle of Protection: Black and a pair of Disenchants, but the options are nigh-endless, from the mighty Worship (which is particularly powerful with Mortivore) to the humble, but effective Aura Blast.

At the same tournament, in which a full four of the Top 8 (including both finalists and three of the top four) played Braids variants, Josh Ravitz played a version of the deck splashing green, and he, like Ziegler, chose to keep the splash in the maindeck to a minimum, running only four Call of the Herd, which not only provides an answer for Circle of Protection: Black and Teferi's Moat, but is also a great addition to the mana curve as well as possessing powerful synergy with Braids. The more attractive reason to run green, however, is for sideboard access to four copies of Compost, which is absolutely devastating in the mirror, as well as against any other black based strategy. Pernicious Deed, while having poor synergy with the rest of the cards in the deck, nonetheless provides a catch-all solution to problem enchantments and artifacts in much the same way as Vindicate. 

So if straightforward beatdown isn't your style, but you still want to feel the rush of attacking for two, or if you inwardly smirk while watching your opponent squirm with no permanents in play, perhaps some version of the Braids deck is for you. If winning with r-g beatdown is like clobbering your opponent with a sledgehammer, winning with Braids is like wielding a surgical knife - it takes a bit more patience and a bit more skill, but in the end it's a lot cleaner, and you get less of a mess on the furniture. If this sort of sadism isn't your thing, be ready to handle a three pronged assault on your hand, your permanents, and your life total. Much like against r-g beatdown, your best bet is consistency. Either be sure you can force your opponent to react to your threats so he can't set up a quick Braids lock on you or be prepared with enough cheap answers to withstand a barrage of hand destruction trying to clear the way for the creature assault, or expect to find yourself buried in Braids' backyard.

### Blue-Green

One aggressive color combination that hasn't gotten nearly as much attention of late as the above mentioned is u-g. Perhaps this is because it's just plain less powerful, or perhaps it's simply because few prominent writers have chosen to explore the possibilities. Both of these answers seem a little odd, as in Osaka u-g beatdown was among the two most popular and most successful decks, sharing that pedestal with monoblack. Why is it that so many have worked to convert the black decks to Standard, and yet so few, it seems, have done the same with u-g?

![](https://i.ormos.uk/e6/63/e6632160b19524e012f85bdedfa2e88a5cdaf93852c1aec52d72bcf04b4210b2.jpg)

The most likely answer to this question is simply the excitement surrounding the black cards in *Torment*. It's very easy to see a card like Cabal Coffers or Nantuko Shade and immediately want to figure out the best way to use it in a deck. Many of the cards that made u-g such a contender in Osaka aren't new - the biggest culprit being Wild Mongrel - and even those that are, like Basking Rootwalla, Arrogant Wurm, and Circular Logic, while clearly powerful cards, don't have nearly as much hype surrounding them as do their black counterparts. That having been said, however, it's definitely possible to build a powerful deck utilizing these colors and these cards - it just takes a bit of effort, and keeping a few key points in mind. 

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/07/85/078585a1ce704f94ec712e472edd0af83d109f79582b29689a7cb073177801a3.jpg"><br><i>Will this power card show well?</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Most attempted builds of u-g, in my opinion, try to get too tricky in what they're designed to do. I've seen countless u-g builds that are Opposition based, featuring one or both of Static Orb and Squirrel Nest. I've seen u-g Upheaval decks with Nimble Mongoose and Wearbear, topping off at Krosan Beast on many occasions, and I've seen u-g madness decks with the whole gamut of madness spells, featuring not only Rootwalla, Wurm, and Logic, but with Obsessive Search as well. These decks, while they can be in principle sound, are in practice not nearly as effective as more straightforward decks, like r-g or Braids. A basic principle of deck design that I keep in mind for every non-combo deck I build is that every card ought to be independently effective - that is, that individual cards ought not rely on combination with other cards in the deck to be useful.

One example of a card that generally fails this test is Squirrel Nest. While obviously extremely powerful in concert with Opposition, Squirrel Nest alone is generally too expensive and too slow to be independently useful. Similarly, Nimble Mongoose, while a devastating attacker after an Upheaval, is scarcely worth the cardboard it's printed on in the early game against most decks, and Obsessive Search, while clearly useful with cards like Wild Mongrel and Merfolk Looter, is hardly synergistic with the strategy of generally wanting to beat your opponent about the head and shoulders as quickly as possible. u-g decks often seem to suffer from an identity crisis, not knowing just what they want to do, confused as to when to be the beatdown and when to play control. Focus and consistency - not cute tricks - are what you want in a big tournament like Regionals, and a more streamlined u-g deck is more likely to provide that than some clunky Opposition/Squirrel Nest monstrosity.

This list is untested, but ought to provide some idea as to what sort of design I'm talking about. Wild Mongrel and Merfolk Looter (perhaps the least-hyped superstar in the current environment) provide a means to abuse madness and threshold, and yet the deck can operate without them. Upheaval serves as the occasional reset button to get out of a sticky situation, but more often just says "I win" when followed up with a couple of Rootwallas and a Werebear - keep in mind that discarding your Rootwallas during your post-Upheaval cleanup step lets you madness them into play just as well as using a Mongrel or Looter. Roar of the Wurm provides a huge body against beatdown decks, and Burst and Logic are the requisite tempo-advantage elements that define the color combination. 

Keep in mind that this is just a rough sketch, and can in all likelihood be improved upon immensely, perhaps even with the addition of another color. In any event, if you want to go the beatdown route, but you just can't pry yourself away from your islands and countermagic because you're some kind of paranoid control freak, u-g is probably your best bet. If not, be prepared for degenerate Rootwalla-Mongrel-Arrogant Wurm-Roar draws - they happened in Osaka, and they'll happen to you. Just remember that Wild Mongrel and Merfolk Looter must be shot on sight whenever possible - in u-g decks, they're public enemy number one.
