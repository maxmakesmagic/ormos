*"You got so lucky, you drew one of the four cards in your deck out of 240."*

*"You're a stupid opponent. Not cool at all."*

*"You #$^&, that's bull#&$^, you're cheating!!!"*

*"You know, that card is banned in real life."*

*"What a crock of @#$%"*

*"Why don't you make your own deck, you unoriginal son of a #$%^&!"*

Ah, such are the joys of playing Battle of Wits on **Magic** Online. I'm not going to bore you with yet another article on the program itself since that's been done plenty at this point, but there's one thing I absolutely love about it: Battle of Wits is so easy to play. Granted, part of the fun of the deck is to watch the expression on your opponent's face as you shuffle up your 240+ card monstrosity, but I'll gladly substitute the annoyances of shuffling, tutoring and sideboarding with such a big deck when I can do it digitally. **Magic** Online shuffles it for you, makes sideboarding a snap, and tutoring is very easy thanks to the layout. Say what you want about it, but any program that can let me play Battle of Wits without all the inconveniences gets my stamp of approval. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/d5/a1/d5a14963c437de0971660b06b6772c37511e9cf75a0807bc848ff5fd963d6fe0.jpg)A lot of players that have emailed me over the past few months about Battle of Wits decks have had to make substitutions for cards, because it's hard to put together a large deck with so many rares. If you're one of these people, I highly suggest trading for and building a Battle of Wits deck on **Magic** Online while it's still in Beta testing, and packs are free. Besides all the previously mentioned conveniences, there's one added element that I used to cringe at, but now fully enjoy.

People on **Magic** Online HATE losing to Battle of Wits.

Sure, people aren't exactly thrilled to lose to it in real life either, but at least some opponents can appreciate it. It seems that practically every opponent I play against on **Magic** Online throws a cyber-temper tantrum when I cast Battle of Wits. In case you're wondering, all those quotes above have actually been said to me. I used to cringe in anticipation of verbal abuse as I tapped my five lands to bring forth my favorite card in **Magic**, but now I simply smile and receive the insults, take the win, and go on to the next round.

I haven't been told "that's cheesy" in reference to anything strategic I've done in a game since playing Mortal Kombat 2 in Middle School. Apparently, it's very applicable to playing Battle of Wits, but I just give the same response I did years ago when I'd use Mileena's teleport kick to the dismay of my opponent: "Hey, it works, you lose to it, figure out a way to beat it or stop complaining." A few players such as Brian Kibler and Ben Bleiweiss have Battle of Wits decks made on **Magic** Online and play with them often, and have told me that it's some of the most fun they've ever had playing **Magic**. It's annoyed people to the point that I've seen "60 Card Only!" Standard tournaments being run online. What, like it's so hard to sideboard a few Aura Blasts?

Now that I've suggested playing against unarmed opponents in the virtual world, I'd like to shift focus to **Magic** in the real world. Specifically, Regionals is approaching, and the post-Torment metagame has started to take shape. Psychatog and Kibler's Mystic Enforcer deck seem to be the forerunners, while red-green beatdown and *Torment* assisted monoblack decks are also intriguing. In the interest of staying true to my convictions, and to be honest, because I don't want to put much effort into playtesting something new, I will be playing Battle of Wits at Regionals. *Torment* introduced three cards that each found a space into the deck, and I've also considered the pros and cons of my creature based version when compared to Ben Rubin's version. For reference, analysis of these two decks and their differences can be found [here](about:blank)

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>When I saw Ben Rubin's version of the deck being played by Jensen, Parker and Linde in San Diego, my first response was to assume it was better than mine.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

When I saw Ben Rubin's version of the deck being played by Jensen, Parker and Linde in San Diego, my first response was to assume it was better than mine. Brian Kibler had been playing my version of the deck that I won the Grudge Match Qualifier with on **Magic** Online for a few weeks before San Diego, and he confirmed this notion to me. I accepted it, but deeper contemplation made me reconsider. My major issue with their version was the lack of win conditions. Besides the four Battle of Wits and eight tutors (Diabolic Tutor and Wild Research), the only way the deck could win was through burn in the form of Urza's Rage, Prophetic Bolt, Fire/Ice, and Agenda to bring them all back. Granted, this can be effective, but I was worried about drawing due to lack of time, and liked the idea of riding a Magpie or Finkel to victory. Essentially, I felt their version was more combo oriented in the sense that it was narrow but focused, while mine could go to the backup plan of attacking with creatures. This has its drawbacks, since one of the benefits of playing Rubin's version is that the creature removal your opponent draws is practically worthless.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/51/0f/510fd3606b66693320deacf9831becc1f107a8ea68c71a7e2b75bc233f517ee2.jpg)I left these questions somewhere in the back of my mind, and decided to come back to them once *Torment* came out. When it did, I saw the answer to the question. The missing piece of the puzzle. The moment I saw Insidious Dreams, I knew it was perfect for the deck. As I mentioned in my article on the *Odyssey* Block version of Battle of Wits, card disadvantage doesn't matter much when you can go get a card that says "I win". It's even better in the Standard version of the deck, because you have more options. Once you get to twelve land, you can go get two Urza's Rage, and finish off the game with an uncounterable kill. Obviously, it's best to go for a quick Battle of Wits when you can, but I'll often need to set up my deck so that I'll draw something along the lines of Terminate to stay alive, fifth land, Battle of Wits. I've often said that Battle of Wits lets you play an unfair deck without the dirty combo feeling, but Insidious Dreams turns the deck into a true combo deck, in the sense that you have to think through your next few turns, which will hopefully allow you to resolve Battle of Wits and stay alive long enough for it to trigger.

Another great thing about Insidious Dreams is that it's an instant. I never feel quite comfortable casting Diabolic Tutor on turn four, especially against an aggressive deck. It means tapping out on turn four to get Battle of Wits, and then tapping out on turn five to play it. This gives my opponent two full turns to do whatever they want, and I can't stop them. Being able to restrict that window of opportunity to one turn makes me a lot more comfortable. Plus, you can use Insidious Dreams to ensure victory against certain decks. I'll often discard most of my hand to put four Battle of Wits on top of my library, just to make sure they won't have enough Pernicious Deeds, Aura Blasts or Vindicates to prevent a Battle from ending the game.

And yes, in case you're wondering, it feels really evil when the top four cards of your library all say "I win the game".

The other added benefit of Insidious Dreams is that it bumps your tutor count up to twelve, and gives you the option of going for the burn plan in the late game. Simply put, this adds a lot of consistency to the deck. For this reason, I felt fine with running a creatureless version. It means relying more on the Battle of Wits plan, but then again, lots of control decks only run Morphling. If a sixty card deck can have three Morphlings as its win conditions, then it should be fine to run a 246 card deck with four Battle of Wits as win conditions when you have twelve tutors that can go get them.

The main reason I decided to switch over to a version similar to the one played at Masters is that the Standard environment simply isn't creature heavy enough to run a removal base of Wrath of God, Rout and Teferi's Moat. Since the Psychatog and Enforcer decks run minimal creatures, and can protect them when they come out, it's inefficient to rely on a high casting cost sorcery to deal with them, because you'll often lose the resulting counterwar. By using a cheaper targeted removal base, the deck can deal with these threats much better. Unfortunately, this makes the matchup against red-green and green-blue aggro decks a lot more difficult. If they can get ahead of you and play a ton of creatures, it's hard to bounce back unless you can stay alive long enough to get a quick Battle of Wits. If your local metagame is heavy in beatdown, I suggest adjusting the main deck to make room for the Wraths and Routs.

For this reason, I decided to keep a fair amount of white in the deck, while the Masters version only splashes it. I found myself in far too many situations where I kept telling myself "If I only had a Wrath of God here, I could win so easily". After sideboarding, things are a lot more even. Against beatdown decks, I will frequently Insidious Dreams for Wrath of God and Battle of Wits, which is usually enough to win the game. 

Here are some brief notes on some of the cards that were taken out of my first version, cards that made it into this version, and what I didn't bring over from the Masters decklist.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/6f/cf/6fcfbafb18d48d3b1a2eb77b1c01346402aa4d6ed66aef76e6f29db74cff72f8.jpg)**Standstill:** This was absolutely amazing when I had creatures and could follow them up with Ancestral Recall. Without any spells that provide significant board pressure, cutting it was an easy decision.

**Spite/Malice:** Seemed like a natural fit, but was almost always sideboarded out for something else. It's especially poor since it can't kill Mystic Enforcer, Psychatog or Shadowmage Infiltrator. While it can still be used as a four mana counterspell, that isn't efficient enough for it to make the cut.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/8c/79/8c790dcd10c74051fa2f0acabe8ab5ec8f131ad0fb459c58df3042fa8a656f1a.jpg)**Shadowmage Infiltrator:** I was really torn cutting this card because it can win games by itself, but realized that if I played such a creature light version of the deck, Johnny Magic would become a lightning rod for all the removal that accumulated in my opponent's hand. Even though I can still protect it with countermagic in such a situation, I'd rather not allow my opponents to use their creature removal to trade for a card of mine. It may find its way into the sideboard to be brought in after game one when opposing creature removal is sided out.

**Terminal Moraine:** Every time I play with this card I appreciate it more, and can't understand why Rubin's version didn't run it. I'm sure he has a reasonable explanation for why it didn't make the cut, but I doubt it could convince me to not play this great mana fixer.

**Diamonds:** Rubin's version of the deck ran twelve Diamonds total, and while they would fit in well with the new, more focused version of the deck thanks to Insidious Dreams, I'm still skeptical. The first few turns are very important, and tapping out for a Diamond means not being able to counterspell a Wild Mongrel, Psychatog or Shadowmage Infiltrator. If you have a creature removal spell, then this is acceptable, but that won't always be the case. Between the deck's heavy cantrips and mana fixers such as Terminal Moraine and Chromatic Sphere, I don't have much of a problem getting the mana I need. For that reason, I'd rather have twelve more action cards than twelve cards that just produce mana, although the acceleration is tempting.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/d8/87/d887fd1e83bdc0da0fb55c38d7d2bfeee6ffa8ff89e3cdcb613fe76f78198978.jpg)**Circular Logic:** A ![2 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) counterspell is a great bargain, because it lets me counter even if I don't have ![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) available, and the madness ability can be abused with Insidious Dreams and Wild Research. An obvious choice for the deck.

**Gerrard's Verdict:** I'm a bit undecided with this card because it seems somewhat inefficient. I often can't play it on turn two because of the come-into-play-tapped lands and *Odyssey* filter lands, and when I do, it often just causes my opponent to discard creature removal spells that were worthless anyway. Still, it's won me games against beatdown decks, but that's not enough to make me reconsider playing this card. If I cut anything before Regionals, this will probably be it.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/10/61/1061310db264f2e91d0de77619e0304b04bbf7e506ed79eba2f5369e477a9c1d.jpg)**Opportunity vs. Concentrate:** Rubin's version ran Concentrate, I run Opportunity, and you'll have a hard time convincing me otherwise. In my opinion, being able to keep mana open for countermagic and play this card on your opponent's end step greatly outweighs the extra two mana cost. Besides, it gets you four cards instead of three. It can also be searched out with Wild Research, which I do quite often against permission when I'm searching out threats to run them out of countermagic before I go for Battle of Wits. The only drawback that concerns me is the fact that Divert can make Opportunity very ugly for you, but this isn't enough to convince me to run Concentrate.

**Teferi's Moat:** This keeps bouncing in and out of my sideboard. It's great against red-green beatdown, except for the fact that Wild Mongrel gets around it. The final decision may come down to the popularity of monoblack in the new metagame. Teferi's Moat stops black armies cold, and as we all know, enchantment removal is definitely not black's forte. 

**Hibernation:** A potential sideboard card against red-green, but it doesn't get the job done if a Skizzik hits the table. It would be sideboarded instead of some Wraths obviously. The fact that it's an instant, cheaper, and a lot easier to cast is something to consider.
