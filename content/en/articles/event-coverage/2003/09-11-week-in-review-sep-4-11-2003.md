### The 2003-2004 Season

![](http://www.wizards.com/protour/images/pt_lg.gif)The new Pro Tour season has been kicked off with several Grand Prix over the last two weekends. The most important change is that the final pro standings after this season will see the top pros rewarded with significant amounts of cash. Although Masters Series is now defunct, its prize money will be distributed based on the season’s final standings. It will be interesting to see whether this will prompt pro players to travel to Grand Prix events as much as the Masters qualification race did. My prediction is that it will be somewhat less enticing, since the reward is far away and it is not as clear how many additional points a player would need to pick up from that extra Grand Prix.

In addition to switching to this reward system, DCI unveiled a number of other changes last week. Most notable is their initiative to switch **Magic** “seasons” to standard calendar years, rather than beginning and ending them in August. This will be a two year long process, with 2004 season ending in September, and 2005 season ending in December. This also means that the current season will include an extra Pro Tour stop – a Team Pro Tour which has been moved from autumn to late summer – and finally culminate in Worlds which have been moved to September. We will also see the **Magic** Invitational pushed back a few months to November-December.

Another change may be an extended number of rounds at each Pro Tour stop. The DCI is experimenting with this in New Orleans, where we will play 8 rounds on Day 1 and 8 rounds on Day 2. The Day 2 threshold has not been announced, but one can assume it will be 5-3. This will eliminate a large number of intentional draws in the last round of each PT, where it takes a 4-2-1 record to advance to the second day. Although this makes a Pro Tour schedule even more grueling for players and especially judges, it is hard to imagine that this innovation will not end up being adapted for future events.

Finally, it was announced that Ilja Rotelli will be taking over as head of the DCI. This position was held by Jeff Donais for several years. Recently Jeff left to pursue a job opportunity at Upper Deck. Ilja has a vast amount of experience with organized play programs, having proven himself as head of DCI Italy over the years. It will be interesting to see what new ideas and policies he brings to the table in his new capacity.

### Brave New Extended

Having seen the state of Extended at Worlds, DCI announced its first major bannings in a long time, removing three cards (and perhaps three decks) from the format. Goblin Lackey, Entomb and Frantic Search have all been banned in Extended.

Those of you who watched coverage of Worlds closely may note that no one deck was truly problematic there. The field was diverse and no one deck dominated the standings the way Mirari’s Wake builds dominated in Standard. So why the bannings? Upon reflection, they were probably necessary.

Japanese players ported their Goblin Bidding decks to Extended, manipulating their goblins with Lackey and Recruiter. The deck performed well at Worlds. I am sure that it would be a major archetype in New Orleans had the bannings not taken place. Monored remains a viable choice in the format, but it can no longer get ludicrous with turn 2 Siege-Gang Commander or Goon.

Entomb powered Reanimator and Angry Ghoul – both extremely powerful decks that were capable of all but ending the game on turn 2 in many matchups. Although I am not personally happy to see Entomb go (I loved playing Reanimator at Worlds and would very likely play it again in New Orleans if not for the changes), I must grudgingly admit that the format is better off without a Tutor-type card for one black mana.

Finally, the ban of Frantic Search is aimed at Mind’s Desire decks – the most talked-about archetype at Worlds which, nevertheless, failed to produce very impressive results there. Of course, it was partly due to everyone not playing Desire metagaming heavily against it. It is not clear to me though whether removing Frantic Search makes playing Desire out of the question, but it will certainly keep it fair. This also potentially hurts Enchantress decks and, to a small degree, u-g madness decks.

A few of the format’s staples do walk away unscathed. Decks like The Rock and Tinker lost nothing. Even so, the metagame they will exist in changed significantly, and so everyone qualified for New Orleans has quite a bit of work to do. Once *Mirrodin* is added to the mix testing will begin full speed. It is likely that we will see a new deck archetype or two in New Orleans.

### Grand Prix-Yokohama

![](https://i.ormos.uk/d8/84/d884503dd00ee2d5be64bb84dc0b20be214ca47d8837d03588e507b8ab9d3547.jpg)This tournament did not disappoint in size with just over 800 players attending. However, there was no international attendance as it took place on the same weekend as GP-London. Almost half the field played monowhite or white-red control decks, so it was no surprise that six of the Top 8 decks were r-w. One goblin and one Zombie Bidding deck sneaked into the Top 8, but it came down to a r-w mirror match in the finals, where a relative unknown Shu Komuro emerged victorious. 

### Grand Prix-London

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gplon03/846.jpg)Since GP-London was held only a couple of weeks after Worlds, many pros ended up traveling around Europe for a while in order to attend this event. Many of these – including Canada National Champion Josh Rider, Jose Barbero, Antonino de Rosa and eventual winner Diego Ostrovich ended up in Top 8. 

This tournament advanced Block technology far more than Yokohama. U-w decks were here in force, an archetype previously not seen at Grand Prix level. Brian Kibler placed 9th with his version of the deck, while Mario Pascoli made Top 4 with his. The most interesting deck of the tournament was played by Yann Hamon, who managed to build a strange g-b-r variant with Silklash Spiders and Cabal Conditioning. Although his deck was a metagame choice to beat both goblins and r-w, it was Ostrovich’s goblin deck that eventually took him down in the finals

### Grand Prix-Atlanta

![](https://i.ormos.uk/fc/f4/fcf44dd39dee92918d434f8f96b4656fd1514c3c1cf139aa206107cc6435b900.jpg)American and recently Latin American players have been invading European Grand Prix for a long time now. This is the first time that European pros struck back in significant numbers. In a similar story to that of Grand Prix-London, this tournament is only two weeks away from a Pro Tour on the same continent, and so many pros opted to come out early, and then spend the next two weeks traveling in America and practicing for Boston.

Although Marco was the only non-U.S. player to break into Top 8, there was a slew of Top 16 finishes from players like France’s Gabriel Nassif and Jeroen Remie from the Netherlands. The Top 8 was a mix of established pros and newcomers. Among the more recognizable names were U.S. National Champion Joshua Wagener, Matt Linde, and Zvi Mowshowitz. Like Gab Walls, Wagener has been doing very well since first coming to public light at U.S. Nationals. It was Marco Blume who took the gold though, playing what is arguably the format’s strongest deck – Mirari’s Wake.

Blume has had a tough time trying to live up to the reputation of his celebrity teammates Kai Budde and Dirk Baberowski. He is, in fact, a very strong player on his own. Blume won German Nationals a few years ago and has a number of money finishes on the Tour. This Grand Prix win is a very strong launchpad for his bid at Player of the Year, especially since no one doubts Phoenix Foundation’s ability to do well next weekend.

There was much speculation as to the size of this tournament. Held at Dragon-Con, some predicted the turnout of close to a thousand people. In fact, it was a solid but not groundbreaking turnout for a North American Grand Prix, with attendance falling a bit short of 600 players.

### Play of the Week

Jason Rubinfeld was playing a draft match on ***Magic** Online*. He drafted a monoblack deck featuring a horde of zombies, including a pair of Vengeful Dead. His opponent’s controllish deck managed to quickly get the two players in a creature stall. However, Jason managed to summon both of his Dead as well as a Carrion Feeder and a number of other zombies. Neither player could get through for combat damage. Nevertheless, Jason saw an opportunity to win the game. At the end of one of his opponent’s turns (who was at 19, having taken one point of damage early in the game via Vengeful Dead), Jason sacrificed most of his zombies to the Feeder, making his opponent lose two life points each time, and then unmorphed an Infernal Caretaker! On his turn he replayed the zombies and sacrificed them again, able to deal exactly the remaining nineteen points of damage. He won the game without ever dealing a single point of combat damage.
