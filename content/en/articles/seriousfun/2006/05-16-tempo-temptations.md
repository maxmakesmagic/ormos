During tempo week, it's important for an article to get to a prompt start so that we don't lose momentum to our opponents. Let's look at two alternative formats, each with a few easy variants, that stress ideas of tempo.

### Format #1: The Four-Turn Game

*The premise*: Start play normally, with whatever restrictions you normally apply to decks. The only way to win the game is to generate (and keep on the board) as many non-token, non-land permanents as possible, in four turns. After four turns, count up each player's non-token, non-land permanents in play and give that player one point for each. The player with the most points wins. Ignore all other victory conditions. Skip all combat phases.

*What it teaches about tempo*: Many multiplayer enthusiasts do not pay enough attention to the first few turns. This format forces a group to pay *all* their attention to those turns. The nature of the format excludes all but the most extraordinary combo decks from operating well (and a simple Standard deck restriction should take care of those). Likewise, most control decks are useless. The format prefers cheap, aggressive decks. It's a great way to practice building and playing decks with fast starts.

*What it doesn't teach*: Interaction will be difficult – but players who can manage to be interactive in a short time, without a combat phase, may have an advantage. The format also lacks a way to reward control-based tempo decks, which can be just as interesting as aggressive ones.

*Strategic considerations:* These games will be very fast, since players are just dumping out cards and may not be bothering with what other players are doing. If you play with cheap creatures with comes into play effects (like Man-o'-War or Sparkmage Apprentice), you may have a small advantage.

Another path to victory? Land enchantments like Utopia Sprawl and Overgrowth. Not only do they count as permanents, they get you a burst of more permanents the following turn.

For more ideas on good cheap cards in multiplayer, [see my article from beatdown week](/en/articles/seriousfun/2005/06-29-the-first-two-turns). Beatdown is not the same concept as tempo, but there is an obvious intersection. 

*Tweaks*: Three ways to go here. First, emphasize quality. Quantity of permanents is nice – but quality should have some weight too. As you count up permanents at the end of the four-turn stint, give each player one point for each non-creature permanent (i.e., enchantments and artifacts), two points for each non-token creature with combined power and toughness 2 or less, three points for each non-token creature with combined power and toughness 4 or less, and four points for each non-token creature with combined power and toughness of five or more.

Second, add a combat phase and add points for however much damage your creatures do over the course of four turns.

Third, after the fourth turn, the player who would "win" should draw three cards, gain ten life, and return all cards from their graveyard to their hand. Play then proceeds normally, with regular victory conditions restored.

### Format #2: The Banishers

*The premise*: Start play normally, with typical deck restrictions. In addition to those restrictions, every deck must contain at least 20 creature cards. No non-basic lands are allowed. Each time a spell or ability removes at least one non-land permanent from play, the controller of that spell or ability receives one point. (Only one point per spell or ability – so Wrath of God slaying twelve creatures gains you the same single point that Swords to Plowshares does.) After five turns, the player with the most points wins. Ignore all other victory conditions. Skip all combat phases.

*What it teaches about tempo*: This format is more interested than the first in interaction – and in control. The point system is geared to reward lots of little tempo control spells, rather than grand sweeping stuff. Learning proper targeting is very helpful in learning how to control tempo.

*What it doesn't teach*: Of course, without a combat phase, there are very few consequences to poor removal spells. It doesn't really matter if you get rid of a creature (or artifact, or enchantment) for good – as long as you get it to leave play, you get a point. This format is about learning a philosophy, more than card quality.

*Strategic considerations*: Look for permanents that can activate a control ability multiple times – preferably without investing more mana. For example, Parallax Wave will get you five distinct points.

Because of this, some ability-based combos (such as Goblin Bombardment, Enduring Renewal, and Shield Sphere) will work in this format. If your group hasn't seen stuff like this before, go ahead and try it. If they have, come up with something new.

*Tweaks*: If you wish to give more weight to global kill, you can always provide a point for each permanent removed from play (instead of one per spell/ability activation).

Another variant is to test the control decks a bit more by allowing creatures to attack for damage, and for the attacking creatures' controller to earn a point each time a creature deals damage. Can a control tempo deck earn more points than an attacking deck can over the course of six turns?

A third variant, parallel to one of the variants in the first format, is to give a bonus after five turns to the player with the most points – again, three cards, ten life, and a raised graveyard seems reasonable – and then continue play normally.

### Other Tempo Format Considerations

![](http://www.wizards.com/global/images/mtgcom_daily_aa228_picMain_en.jpg)

While I believe tempo means less in the later stages of a multiplayer game than it does early on, the idea of timing can still have critical importance. Cards like Vedalken Dismisser and Tidespout Tyrant can be back-breakers, because they can remove key blockers at times when your opponent is just hoping to stabilize.

If you decide to design a format that emphasizes later tempo (and see [last week's article](/en/articles/seriousfun/2006/05-09-the-self-generating-format) on some advice on such an undertaking), you may want to try to isolate the "turn period" you want to emphasize. Restricting cards and/or action to, say, turns 6 to 10 can help you define the allowable card pool so that silly combos don't wreck the fun and/or learning experience. You can even set a restriction on the converted mana cost of each deck's spells.

If your group is knowledgeable and mature enough, you could also award points for "good tempo moves". Make sure to read the other articles here at magicthegathering.com during tempo week to get a good grounding in what tempo actually is. Come to a common understanding among your group as to what criteria will qualify a "good tempo play". Then, during the course of a normal game, you might reward such plays with additional bonuses – if I play Plow Under on you, I might get a 1/1 green saproling token; or if I Stun your hopeful blocker and swing for heavy damage, I might get to deal one damage to a target creature or player. It's not important what the exact rewards are – only that you get the group to agree to guidelines in advance.

### Cautionary Note

As with all of my articles that offer alternative formats, the reader has to spend some time thinking about how each would actually work in his or her group before suggesting it. Groups with more experience, and deeper card pools, are likely to find ways to "break" one or more of these. Use your head and make modifications as necessary.
