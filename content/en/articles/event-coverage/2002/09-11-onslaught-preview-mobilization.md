Looking back at the blue-white control archetype over the years, there have been probably three kill conditions that we would consider its classics. First, in the hands of Weissman, there was Serra Angel, that belle of a beater, who as a non-tapping flyer, was both able to bypass opposing blockers and defend against whatever attackers the opponent could sneak over Brian's all-defending Moat. Several years after the original Weissman deck, in the heyday of blue-white, there was Kjeldoran Outpost. Possibly the most efficient finisher the archetype ever had, the Outpost did many things, all of them well (the specifics of which we will explore in more detail, below). In between, and after, and around, other kill conditions, including splashing for Gaea's Blessing (Brian Schneider) and replacing Serra with Blinding Angel (then they printed Flametongue Kavu), there has been Millstone. A steady choice in the archetype whenever other cards didn't quite fit, Millstone had its own drawbacks and significant upsides for blue-white.

### What Made Them Work: The Classics

### Serra Angel

In the beginning of the blue-white archetype, Serra Angel was Weissman's girl. In his plan with the original The Deck, Brian would play methodically around a system of card advantage light years ahead of his opponents. His base strategy was to lay Moat to stall ground attacks -- that is, the bulk of most opponents' offenses -- and clean up any stragglers with Swords to Plowshares. Then he would deploy Disrupting Scepter and carefully strip away the opponent's hand. In an age before Fact or Fiction or Necropotence, Disrupting Scepter's long-game card advantage would remove not only potential threats, but more importantly, opposing spot removal that could destroy his own vital permanents.

With the Scepter lock in place, Weissman would then lay Serra Angel. The opponent would have to play off the top of his deck, while Brian would be cracking with a sizable 4/4 threat. With a Counterspell or two in his grip, Weissman would routinely have enough action to keep his lock cards in play for the five short turns necessary for Serra Angel to end the game. As I mentioned before, because Serra Angel did not tap to attack, she was redundant defense with Moat, just as she made short work of the opponent.

In more recent times, the printing of more powerful aggressive cards has forced control decks to be faster and less deliberate than Weissman's original design. In modern Standard, it is unlikely that a blue-white mage would be able to set up so precise a combination of defensive cards, and then deploy a creature kill condition, in competitive -- and time sensitive -- tournament play. One of the things that made Kjeldoran Outpost so good, that I think Mobilization shares, is that it takes on more than one role with a single card.

### Millstone

<table><tbody><tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/65/b4/65b44cec90bda938bbb278a79acba1f455a38a19b714807bea68c253b77de30d.jpg"></td><td></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/dd/0a/dd0a617788694ee0e82eed58b0746ab9dbdbdb6e709ee5390dcfb32556a6990e.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/96/f5/96f5d53e3ef958055c4fdf5bf835be99797bd18449a68a882c96ab3801bae612.jpg"></td><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/db/ef/dbefca9f06d49857d2fa84236770f0e97cb6a65d3286c71c6437656f44991ee0.jpg"></td><td></td></tr>&#10;<tr><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/a0/a9/a0a91e69ef087e17e689f758224146196f0a29fad934eb2639d2fb73d4cf3c75.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/c0/e2/c0e24bff2fa206011911cb4b5cb20b4559cb82301a77e757b6e86de3c1ae659a.jpg"></td><td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/fa/ab/faab219b94a5d6a8405ff77aefe6f815ba2bba52fc447652a633eec85e227836.jpg"></td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Millstone has had many critics over the years, primarily because it has little to no real effect on the board in most matchups. If the opponent is coming in full blast with creatures and disruption, the last thing a defending mage is going to want to draw is a spell that doesn't even block. In modern Standard, Millstone actually facilitates flashback and threshold for some opposing decks, making it an even less impressive kill card choice. On the other hand, Millstone's strength in the "mirror match" has never been questioned.

For one thing, Millstone's low mana cost is the blue-white-on-blue-white equivalent of a Nantuko Shade. It can slide under early counters, and by the time the opponent gets around to dealing with it, it can have taken out a large chunk of the other guy's deck. Because blue-white control designs traditionally have so few kill conditions, every random Mill can have a profound effect on the long game. For another thing, Millstone isn't a creature, which means that the other player's creature removal will just amount to dead draw after dead draw.

Conversely, if the opponent is playing with Disenchants or the like, Millstone gives him a viable target.

### Kjeldoran Outpost

The Outpost was basically the best kill condition blue-white ever had for basically every format in which it was viable; contrary to most players' perceptions, Kjeldoran Outpost remained viable in Extended long after the release of *Tempest*'s Wasteland, and was an defensible choice even when Morphling was also available.

When Kjeldoran Outpost first appeared, it was the death knell for an opposing control deck. At the time of *Alliances*, blue-white could handle it strictly with Strip Mine, which itself was restricted. That meant that getting Outpost advantage was profoundly significant when two white decks paired off. Even g-w Armageddon decks played it! G-w was traditionally weak against blue-white's combination of permission and creature removal, but because Kjeldoran Outpost couldn't be countered, and was so difficult to remove, it shored up that matchup nicely... Matt Place credited the presence of this card in his g-w deck for his appearance in the Top 8 of the 1996 World Championships. Incidentally, we could see some of the early genius of one Jon Finkel by his splashing for Stone Rain (mirror tech) in the Pro Tour-Columbus Top 4 Counterpost deck way back in the summer of 1996.

Kjeldoran Outpost, besides being a huge threat against blue-white (or other blue-white) decks, did one thing extremely well: it generated a ton of card advantage. While it seems fairly obvious that a card that costs you a land, but then spits out a 1/1 every turn is going to make back that card loss eventually, the card advantage of Kjeldoran Outpost was not just in the proactive realm, but also facilitated the rest of a deck's card advantage. If the opponent had a significant threat in play, say an Erhnam Djinn, the Outpost would be able to hold that card off by itself, forever. The opponent would eventually be forced to commit another threat -- one with greater than 1 toughness, mind you -- to the board. All of a sudden, the defending blue-white mage gets 2-for-1 with his Wrath of God, and still has an Outpost to tackle the next wave of attackers... or swarm the opponent once he's run out of relevant spells.

### The New Kid on the Blocking Front

So now we have Mobilization. This spell combines a lot of the best elements of the previous blue-white kill conditions, and improves on some of the up-and-comers of the past few years. The first, and most obvious, comparison is going to be between Mobilization and Kjeldoran Outpost itself. On the one hand, Mobilization can be countered by another permission deck, but on the other hand, once it is in play, the blue-white mage will probably completely control the tempo of the game, barring mana issues, regardless of his opponent's archetype. Remember what we said about Kjeldoran Outpost setting up the board against creature decks? What if, instead of tapping three lands for one creature, the blue-white mage could tap six for two, or nine for three? His ability to milk a creature deck's resources would be profoundly enhanced. The opponent would both be forced to commit more threats to the board, and each of the blue-white deck's Wraths would take more cards as well.

Look back at Counter-Trenches. At GP-Milwaukee and tournaments around the world, successful Counter-Trenches players were willing to add a third color to the core blue-white archetype to run, essentially, a less-impressive token generator than Mobilization. While Goblin Trenches certainly had a more mana-efficient outlook to token generation in the short term, the fact that lands were lost with every pair of goblins put into play forced Counter-Trenches players into a swarm-or-die long game in many games. While the raw number of goblins may have been impressive for those decks, the Psychatog decks of last spring could not Burning Wish for Pyroclasm as they can today. I believe Mobilization's ability to drag out the game, churning out a steady number of tokens, may be one of the best signals for a viable blue-white deck in Standard for some time.

But what about Psychatog? In the post-*Invasion* Block (post-Fact or Fiction) Standard metagame, I'm sure that Doctor Teeth will continue to flash his winning smile... but how good is he going to be against a resolved Mobilization? Pat Chapin once taught me that his strategy for Squirrel Nest against Psychatogs put the u-g player in the "control" seat, forcing the Psychatog deck to "win" the game. A lone token generator would essentially hold off a Psychatog, meaning that he could reserve all of his permission for the Upheaval and Upheaval cover spells. That strategy is even more relevant for a blue-white deck with Mobilization than a u-g deck with Squirrel Nest. Not only can blue-white follow essentially the same game plan, but because it can generate multiple Soldiers with one Mobilization, the blue-white descendent can actually go on offense while defending with just one token for every Psychatog. Because the tokens don't tap, offensive tokens can later be used to defend, all while massing larger and larger token armies. If Psychatog decks continue the World Championship trend of lower permission counts, it becomes more and more likely for Mobilization to resolve in the early game, especially with Nightscape Familiar absent, eventually putting a patient blue-white player's permission on anti-Upheaval, rather than anti-"other spells", duty. Remembering that Recoil will follow Fact or Fiction in the "rotated out" line... perhaps we will see a new dominant control archetype.

### Effect on Strategy and Deck Design

If Mobilization turns out to be as good as I suspect, we may see a return to the 1996-1997 style of deck design. Before there were balls-to-the-wall aggressive decks, before Andrew Cuneo played -- gasp -- fourteen permission spells in his first Draw-Go deck, the utility-minded decks of those bygone days had, instead, a little bit of this, and a little bit of that. The creature decks played with Disenchant or Nevinyrral's Disk, and the control decks played with half a dozen different kinds of spot removal. While this made them less efficient in some regard, those decks had more interaction between both players' spells than we find today, and in some people's minds, a more skill-based decision making process at the tactical level. At the strategic level, maybe players will have to consider maindeck (or at least Wish-available) enchantment removal.

Then again, the green decks of 1996-1997 only had Erhnam Djinn, whereas today's green decks get to lead with Wild Mongrel... and then ramp up to Erhnam Djinn. Keep in mind a Mobilization will make the current fatty of choice, Phantom Centaur, look pretty silly in short order; on the other hand, the blue-white deck will have to save its counters for Arrogant Wurm and Grizzly Fate, against which Mobilization's effects are pretty minimal.

The really interesting design choices are probably going to be in the land counts of the blue-white decks. It seems to me that blue decks since 1997 have had a cyclical love-hate relationship with their mana bases. First Alan Comer taught us that you can cheat on land by playing with lots of cheap card drawers, and, as [Brian David-Marshall](/en/articles/deconstructing-famous-decks/2002/09-02-deconstructing-counter-post) recently reminded us over on MagicTheGathering.com, even Jon Finkel cheated on his Counter-Post land count back in 1997. Some years, like in the spring of 2000, Patrick Johnson and Zvi Mowshowitz had us running 28 lands and 4 Grim Monoliths; the next year, Adrian "The Corrupter" Sullivan and Bob Maher were playing Comer-reminiscent numbers -- almost no lands at all -- in their "manascrew blue" decks at U.S. Nationals. It seems to me that if Mobilization proves to be big, blue-white players will want to have a lot of land in their decks, as more land essentially means more tokens in the mirror match. More land also means that the control player will both avoid discarding draws beyond seven and have more mana to spend on the counters he is accumulating turn after turn (it's not like he'll be playing active spells with a Mobilization in play). But remember, he's going to have to have Disenchants to go with those lands... if he wants to win the mirror match.

While Mobilization certainly looks like it will have a huge impact on Constructed, a world full of deck explorers may prove this writer's first impressions a little quick... But one thing seems clear: that thing has got to be a bomb in Limited! May you Mobilize many a soldier at your local Prerelease.
