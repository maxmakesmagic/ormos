Part one of this article focused on the viable beatdown decks to play or prepare for in the upcoming Regional Championships. This, the second (and final - I promise) installment of my Standard metagame overview will examine the promising control and combo decks in the format, starting with the once - and perhaps future - king, that pinnacle of dental hygiene himself - Psychatog.

### Psychatog

![](https://i.ormos.uk/fc/d3/fcd303e216c832a96e5657bf356203e657af86193465daa8f87aac71517049b4.jpg)When the San Diego Masters series saw a full half of its competitors, along with three of the top four finishers all playing some version of a deck based around a ![1 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/6b/4c/6b4cf558eab279fb9414f146ebdd0e0705ec92984fec530597dd2a3bce43ce24.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Black Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/6b/e16bcf75cffdd1079b6ba1f9f43c9e6e7253bb89521acdf6b3e93d3c2e63dbb5.gif) casting cost creature without Johnny Magic's ugly mug in the picture, the entire tournament **Magic** playing world took notice. The cries of protest rang in from the far corners of the internet, proclaiming everything from Psychatog to Fact or Fiction to Peek (well...maybe not Peek) broken beyond repair. Yet with the coming of *Torment*, and with it Chainer's Edict, many declared the king dead and buried. Decks relying on a single, hard to remove creature - decks like Psychatog and Enforcer - seemed at a loss to deal with Edict's ability to kill their win conditions cheaply and easily, and with flashback, to boot.

Enforcer decks, as much as it pains me to say it, have, in fact, essentially died out, largely due to the problem of Chainer's Edict, as well as due to a general difficulty in dealing with the heavy disruption of Braids decks. It's possible a more creature-based version, with maindeck Meddling Mages and other creatures like Call of the Herd or Mystic Crusader, could have some degree of success, but Mystic Enforcer's reign as the unkillable board-dominating monster he once was is at an end. But, to paraphrase Mark Twain, the reports of Psychatog's death have been greatly exaggerated.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/6f/cf/6fcfbafb18d48d3b1a2eb77b1c01346402aa4d6ed66aef76e6f29db74cff72f8.jpg)The Neutral Ground Grudge Match, one of the most useful sources of data about the Standard metagame on the internet, has seen Psychatog decks out in force. These iterations of the Psychatog strategy that have met with considerable success lately are not the bounce and cantrip heavy Tog-only decks such as that played by Ryan Fuller in the Masters series, but rather sleek Nightscape Familiar-based time advantage machines. These decks feature a strange, but important card, discovered apparently by accident as Zev Gurwitz describes in his oddly named article series "I Hate Losing" on [www.neutralground.net](https://web.archive.org/web/20020409000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.neutralground.net&origin=sideboard_strategy_sb20020409a).

That card is Standstill. Generally considered a card reserved for extremely aggressive decks capable of seizing the advantage quickly with early creatures like Nimble Mongoose or Basking Rootwalla (as seen in Ken Ho's Pro Tour Osaka championship deck), Standstill looks out of place in the otherwise control-oriented Psychatog build. While Psychatog cannot play a turn one creature to make a turn two Standstill as immediately threatening, the true threat of Standstill in the Psychatog deck becomes clear when one looks at the supporting cast.

The most important card in this evaluation is Upheaval. With Upheaval in the deck, a Standstill in an otherwise neutral position is as threatening as a Standstill with a creature in play. If both players continue to lay lands and allow the Standstill to continue unbroken, the Psychatog player slowly but surely gains an advantage. The longer the game goes on, the more cards in hand and lands in play both players accumulate, making an Upheaval followed by a Psychatog a very real threat. Against an aggressive deck, playing a Standstill before your opponent can resolve a threat is much akin to casting Ancestral Recall, as they HAVE to kill you.

The question that arises, then, is whether this situational advantage provided by Standstill is worth what is certain to be a sacrifice in consistency. Granted, a turn two Standstill in a neutral position almost certainly favors the Psychatog deck due to its powerful long game, but Standstill itself does not aid in establishing such a position. If the deck gets behind, Standstill essentially becomes a dead card until parity is reached, contributing nothing to gaining control of the board from a disadvantaged situation. If those Standstills were additional bounce or counterspell effects, they would at least be able to contribute to equalizing or maintaining the board position. Whether this trade-off is worthwhile is questionable, and certainly one of the most important considerations to keep in mind in the design process of the Psychatog deck.

Clearly, then, the king is far from dead, but whether our toothy friend will retain his grin - and his crown - through Regionals is unclear. In the face of a horde of Braids and g-r decks, the passing of the royal scepter seems likely. Psychatog decks simply do not play as consistently, nor possess nearly as much potential for abusive draws as its competitors. While it is a viable choice for those who simply insist upon playing control, Psychatog does not stand to be nearly as prominent nor as dominant a force as it was in the pre-*Torment* metagame.

### Domain

![](https://i.ormos.uk/34/d4/34d4a55b1e2e81fe3508873a6659ef1a6896aa5975f8b1f7c3e2bab47f22996e.jpg)There are few players in the world who enjoy casting Harrow nearly as much as I do. During *Invasion* block draft, I was known among Pro Tour regulars as one of the most adamant green drafters around, from g-w-x Llanowar Knight/Armadillo Cloak beats in *Invasion* only, to g-r-x in *Invasion/Planeshift*, to all 5 colors, all the time once *Apocalypse* entered the mix. Harrow was thus always a favorite card of mine in Limited, and by the time Pro Tour-Tokyo rolled around, it had become a Constructed pick of mine as well, as I piloted the alpha version of the Questing Phelddagrif Domain deck that Ben Rubin eventually used to win the Barcelona Masters Series. *Apocalypse* turned my Domain deck of choice a slightly darker shade, with the addition of Pernicious Deed into the mix, but through it all, the basic strategy endured - get lots of different lands into play, and abuse undercosted, overpowered spells. There's few things as satisfying as drawing countless extra cards while watching your opponent frantically try to build up enough mana to get one creature through your Collective Restraints.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/8e/eb/8eebd973262ba2969f3db662f22bd8b4c570010b92d7b55bc0eb4330306d78b4.jpg)Today's modern Domain decks maintain that fundamental strategy, but like the rest of the Standard environment, they too must play the inevitable Flametongue Kavu/Chainer's Edict dance. Before *Torment*, it was just Flametongue Kavu who was in the ring, and Standard was like a mosh pit - the big guys who could take the hits from FTK - like Psychatog and Mystic Enforcer - were the ones who came out on top. Now that Chainer's decided to cut in, however, things have become a lot more complicated. The big guys don't have the finesse to keep up with steps (picture Chainer as the Michael Flatley type), while the hordes of Elephants and another assorted animals who were scared off by the big bad Flametongue can just toss a sacrificial lamb to the Riverdance Macabre.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/18/5e/185e7e318427f84c96bea9f51b1f21e9072b32c5ab59b80ef3c558e66c2e38e9.jpg)To extend this already tired metaphor way too far, Domain decks beat the dance by simply not showing up. Rather than bother trying to compensate for Flametongues and Chainer's Edicts, the best domain decks just tell all those dancers to sit down and shut up, because those cards will be sitting dead in their hand while you go off in their face. This incentive to play a creatureless deck is a strong one, and one of combo-domain's biggest selling points. In the current creature-rich environment, nearly every deck is packed with Flametongues and Edicts and Bloods and Repulses and more, all of which are useless or nearly so against creatureless decks. The old combo deck in Standard, Balancing Act-Terravore, rather than exploiting this characteristic of the metagame instead falls prey to it, as Chainer's Edict is problematic enough as to render the deck almost entirely unviable. For those who miss the old days of High Tide and Academy, however, there's thankfully a new combo deck in town.

This combo-domain deck, dubbed "Rice Snack" by Fujita, who played a very raw original version of the deck at the invitational in Cape Town, uses traditional land search spells like Harrow and Rampant Growth to get a great deal of lands into play, all the while improving card quality with Worldly Counsels, drawing cards with Allied Strategies, and hiding behind Collective Restraints, until reaching critical land mass and exploding with Early Harvest and Restock, generating huge amounts of mana and finishing the opponent off with Ghitu Fire or Death Grasp. Somewhat trickier than attacking for two, to be sure, but altogether far more impressive looking when you pull it off. Zvi recently posted a very good article on [Brainburst.com](https://web.archive.org/web/20020409000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.brainburst.com&origin=sideboard_strategy_sb20020409a) discussing this particular Domain strategy, and if you're looking for a more detailed analysis, I'd recommend reading what he has to say.

The sheer power of the cards in the deck is hard to beat, with effects like "draw five cards" seeming better placed in Type 1 or Uno than in the Standard environment, but the deck pays for this power with a level of inconsistency and fragility. While aggressive decks without significant disruption are a near cakewalk due to Collective Restraint and Pernicious Deed, control decks and decks with a level of disruption comparable to Braids strategies can be quite problematic. Psychatog decks are a particular annoyance, packing both disruption in the form of Duress and a fairly heavy complement of countermagic. Black control decks, despite their high discard count, are generally a fairly good matchup due to their inability to stop Allied Strategies off the top, except with Haunting Echoes, a problem which is easily solved with a sideboard plan of Global Ruin. If you believe that your metagame will be aggro heavy and control light, and if you're looking for something outside the creature-happy metagame that Standard has become, it's possible that combo domain is for you. May your Early Harvests be bountiful.

### Black Control

![](https://i.ormos.uk/3e/26/3e26828e468628913f670b16a43bbec560a7ea8d700918137b08197080b44cf6.jpg)If there was one deck everyone tried to build as soon as they saw Phyrexian Arena, it was Necropotence-drain style black control. And if there was one thing everyone discovered very quickly in testing this deck, it was that Phyrexian Arena is no Necro - one card for one life is hardly X cards for X life. Back then, the deck disappeared quietly and without much fuss, but suddenly *Torment* has given us a plethora of new toys to play with in our black decks, and now - once again - the Necro-loving legions of the world have tried to unearth the ancient power of the skull. 

Black control decks have sprung up left and right, packing enough creature removal to wipe out small countries, between Mutilate, Innocent Blood, and Chainer's Edict - the latter of which can be flashed back with ease due to the massive mana production capabilities of Cabal Coffers. Duress and Addle provide an abundance of disruption to strip key spells from opponents hands, while Corrupt and Soul Burn serve as the finishers. Diabolic Tutor brings everything together, while one-ofs like Yawgmoth's Agenda and Haunting Echoes provide some situational punch.

This whole package - card drawing, creature destruction, direct damage, tutoring, and silver bullets - looks extremely attractive, and extremely difficult to beat. After all, how can you beat a deck that can strip your hand, kill all your men, and burn you out, all the while drawing extra cards and gaining life? The answer, of course, is that in practice things aren't quite so simple. One must keep in mind that this is a control black deck, and control black decks historically have had some very significant holes, at least since Necropotence and Nevinyrral's Disk thankfully vanished into the annals of history. 

One very popular control black deck came into being around the time of Pro Tour-Chicago and the introduction of *Invasion* into the Standard environment. The allure of cards like Pyre Zombie, Blazing Specter, and Void was so strong that many players built so called "Machinehead" decks, control decks featuring the powerful b-r cards from *Invasion*. While these decks were seemingly very potent, with a similar discard arsenal to the more modern control black decks, they met with little success due to the simple reality that they were control decks without countermagic. Time and time again, Machinehead players would have the board clear and under their control only to see their opponent rip a Saproling Burst off the top and take them down in a single attack.

While Saproling Burst is no longer an issue, dealing with similar threats is. Haste creatures and off-the-top game winners remain a significant problem for countermagicless control decks. Old school Necropotence control could deal with Ball Lightnings and Viashino Sandstalkers with the instant speed Contagion and Spinning Darkness, but modern iterations have no such options, relying instead wholly on sorceries to take down creatures and sucking up five damage from every Skizzik who wanders into town. Enchantments remain a significant problem, with Compost and Circle of Protection: Black both quite a bit more than annoyances, and while Haunting Echoes provides some protection from simply losing to top decked bombs if they're in the graveyard at the right time, such a plan doesn't always work, with cards like Upheaval and opposing Echoes, as Osaka showed us, coming off the top for the win, with other cards like Global Ruin and Epicenter also proving tremendously problematic. Armageddon itself may be gone, but its descendents can still put a hurting on the mana-hungry control black decks.

That having been said, however, these control black decks aren't bad, per se - they're just hardly the invincible powerhouses some would have you believe. Like combo domain, they have the attraction of being creatureless in a creature-heavy format, leaving many decks with a great deal of dead weight in the matchup. Also like domain, control black simply has access to some of the most powerful cards available. Like Oliver Schneider said in Osaka, the raw power of cards like Mutilate, Mind Sludge, and Skeletal Scrying is more reminiscent of type one than of Standard, with "Mind Twist you, draw X" hardly seeming at all fair. The deck has a tremendous number of options available to it, with Diabolic Tutor not only increasing the deck's consistency in general, but allowing for much more efficient use of valuable sideboard space, making one ofs to solve specific problems (like Aladdin's Ring vs. Circle Black) an entirely viable option.

So if you're willing to brave the field of black hate and want to draw a bunch of extra cards and burn your opponent out without that dirty combo feeling, black control just may be for you. If not, keep in mind the problem cards mentioned above while you're building your sideboard, because you're certain to see more than a few of these decks at Regionals. It can never hurt to be prepared.

This concludes my wrap up of the major control and combo decks to expect at Regionals, and thus my Standard format overview. This is not, admittedly, a complete and comprehensive examination of the format - Standard is too wide open for such an analysis to be possible without committing far more space than two articles would allow. There are a number of other powerful decks that are likely to pop up at regionals, but those covered in this series are the ones that are the most important in defining the metagame, as they have received the most attention on internet strategy sites and are thus the best defined. Many other decks, like u-r Madness/Burn, g-r-u Madness Beatdown, and w-u-r CounterTrenches all seem to have the tools to perform well in Regionals, but are not sufficiently well defined to consider as cornerstones of the metagame. While I would expect the majority of decks that show up at Regionals to be at least based upon the strategies discussed in this article series, simply due to consistency and strength in numbers, I would not be at all surprised to see more rogue strategies take home National invitations.
