In his [Magic Open report](https://web.archive.org/web/20030221000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.starcitygames.com/php/news/expandnews.php?Article=4522&origin=sideboard_strategy_20030221b), Rob Dougherty recently mentioned a deck that has been very exciting in our Standard testing up in New York: u-g threshold. I came upon this specific build, ironically, while working on [Raphael Levy's deck](/en/articles/feature/2002/11-21-world-championship-standard-decks) for a feature on the World Championship Standard decks for MagictheGathering.com. I was intrigued by Levy's ignoring of staple cards like Basking Rootwalla and his inclusion of four Breakthroughs and four Wonders.

Working from Levy's list, which had an impressive one-loss record at the 2002 World Championships, I immediately swapped eight *Onslaught* sacrifice lands into the mana base. U-g was, without question, the most popular deck to come out of *Odyssey* Block, and despite the mass adoption of u-g madness as the u-g deck of choice in Standard and even Extended, I was never convinced that the madness implementation was empirically better than a deck focused on threshold. Factor in that *Onslaught* gave the u-g threshold deck the additional punch of Flooded Strand and Windswept Heath while it gave madness nothing in particular and I had a significant incentive to at least try the threshold deck.

So we did.

The *Onslaught* lands were a godsend. BDM and I had worked exhaustively on threshold hybrid decks during the *Odyssey* Block PTQ season. First, we worked on the Grizzly Fate deck that ended up being one of the dominant decks on the East Coast and then on an evolved anti-Wake version with maindeck Phantom Centaurs and light madness elements. Previously, we would have to find two enablers and an incidental discard engine such as Wild Mongrel to turbo-charge our threshold threats. With Flooded Strand and Windswept Heath in the mix, any two enablers would give us quick threshold. In our testing, quick threshold meant doom for our opponents.

Without a doubt, this version of u-g was the best deck being tested at Neutral Ground for at least two or three weeks. We were beating, if not dominating, Psychatog decks, even the anti-creature versions without Cunning Wish. If the Psychatog opponent didn't have Jonathan Magic, it was nearly impossible for him to keep up with Genesis. The other guy would always have to find Upheaval to win, because we could sit back on Nimble Mongoose, making it impossible for him to send in a lethal Psychatog randomly on the back of some spot removal. Don Lim, the mightiest playtest partner of all the Grounders, was extremely frustrated in the matchup. "I can beat 56 cards in your deck. I can't beat turn 1 or 2 Mongoose. How embarrassing." The sideboarded matchup gave them little, while it gave us access to incredible tools from Phantom Centaur to Compost to Upheaval.

Joshua Ravitz brought the unsung deck of the Chicago Masters, g-r beatdown, to the next playtest session. Sporting his all-new, all-professional playtest attitude of (gasp) shuffling and cutting friends like Gerrard Fabiano and yours truly, Josh declared that g-r was favored against u-g and that I had an uphill battle. Five games later, g-r hadn't won a single duel. "It's not like against the u-g madness. Your guys are too big." Josh's Wild Mongrels were better than mine because of Basking Rootwalla and direct damage, but I could stay in the game with Wonder and Roar of the Wurm. Josh had a particularly hard time with the efficient 6/6 men and no answer whatsoever to Breakthrough. To be fair, he came back with a hot streak of his own, almost evening up the count by the time we hit the 10 game set. Over the next couple of hours of testing this green-on-green matchup, I felt extremely confident with u-g threshold, winning the majority of games, even when Josh had sided in Ensnaring Bridge, finding my lone Ray of Revelation far from useless against his Elephant Guides. This matchup came down to Wild Mongrel superiority, whether I drew Roar of the Wurm, whether Joshie got mana flooded and, oddly enough, play skill. Careful management of threshold (sometimes you don't want it, believe it or not) and chump-blocking allowed u-g to win a lot of games that g-r should have, by all rights, taken.

Silly decks like Tight Sight were of no concern whatsoever. Elden Lee, Neutral Ground's biggest proponent of Tight Sight and the origin point of the deck Gerrard ran in the Master's Gateway, was down something like 30 games before his deck would do anything against this tight(er) little package.

But the real incentive came against Opposition. Franck Canu won a little tournament by the name of the Chicago Masters with Squirrel Nest and Opposition and I was sure it would be a popular archetype. bam Bam BAM. Opposition was a near bye. Other than four Birds of Paradise that could do nothing but block, the deck had no way whatsoever to handle a flying Nimble Mongoose. Wonder, in general, was tough to deal with, but at least the 6/6s and other huge fatties could be held off somewhat by Opposition. Not true for the Mongoose. Playing against a Wonder-enhanced one-drop was a pure race for the Opposition player and given his ability to make 1/1s to the threshold deck's much larger creatures, it was a difficult race at best. My favorite playtest game was against Neutral Ground's Grudge Match Champion, Mr. David Chung, when I mulliganed into a fairly poor hand. I didn't have much in play when Chung accelerated himself into a quick Birds of Paradise, Call of the Herd, Opposition board. By my third or fourth upkeep, he was tapping my City of Brass (my only blue source). I responded by tapping for white and showed him the front-side of Ray of Revelation. I topdecked an island, Mental Noted into threshold and knew that the other Opposition he drew - ostensibly his only way out of a rapidly sliding game - was never going to be relevant.

Scott McCord, ever a proponent of the most consistent decks possible, asked why in the world we would run one Ray of Revelation but only three Seton's Scouts... especially with no access to Quiet Speculation. Part of me (the truth-telling part) wanted to say that Levy had run three Scouts and I hadn't played with the numbers much. The part that does the talking said that we needed some way to mise out of the AstroGlide matchup and that Seton's Scout was the weakest performer among the beaters, sort of halfway between a poor man's Wild Mongrel and a poor man's Werebear.

Though our testing was limited, the deck was absolutely dominant in our arena. BDM and I decided to reveal it at the Boston Open and Mike Short decided to adopt it as well, much to the ire of some of our playtest group. Paul Jordan wanted to know why we would run the group's best deck in public, but Don rebutted, saying that Replenish, Napster and ZevAtog - Neutral Ground's best decks Regionals after Regionals - would never have gotten as good as they did if we hadn't been testing them in the fires of real tournament play.

Here is the sideboard we came up with for Boston:

2 Upheaval  
 2 Krosan Reclamation  
 2 Turbulent Dreams  
 4 Compost  
 2 Ray of Revelation  
 2 Worship

Scotty M didn't want to see Upheaval in the sideboard, especially given the deck's abysmally low land count of 22. The problem is you basically need Upheaval to ever win against AstroGlide in any real game. If you just kill the enchantments, they'll beat you with Wrath of God and Angel superiority. If you just try to fight the threats, they'll beat you with card advantage. In order to take a fair fight, you have to destroy Astral Slide (to keep them from protecting the Angel from Upheaval) and then reset the game with a 3/3 or 4/4 in play. Especially as u-g threshold has no permission, this matchup is next to impossible without the big ups; in order to cast it, we can cheat a little with Werebear mana.

We had literally no testing against the more standard u-g decks. Somehow, we had dozens of test games with either threshold or Ken Ho's deck on one side of the table, but the opponent was always a Psychatog deck or a bad red deck or a combo deck and never the other deck. We did some quick theorizing and tried to transfer our knowledge from the summer PTQs to this matchup. In the Houston qualifiers, we built u-g decks that looked like they would be poor in the mirror match, lacking everything from the size of Roar of the Wurm to the tempo of Aether Burst, but at the end of any 10 game series, the decks would typically split 5-5 or 6-4 or 4-6 with more conventional u-g builds. Barring crazy Aether Burst action, the matchup, over and over, was about one thing: Wonder. One guy got it, the other guy got bashed. If both guys got it, it was a regular fight with regular rules.

The first step was obvious: make sure you're the guy with Wonder. We had 4 Wonders to the typical 3 played by standard u-g decks and 16 graveyard dumps for that most incredible of incarnations. There's nothing worse than drawing Wonder in the mirror match, but having to play it as a crappy 2/2 flyer when the opponent is tapping out for Roar of the Wurm. Even so, we had no hard data on the matchup, only theory. My guess was that we could juke the other guy into countering creatures and then resolve a Breakthough and recoup creatures in the long term with Genesis. Overall, we had more huge men, including an advantage in the Roar of the Wurm category, so the matchup should be favorable. Moreover, u-g threshold plays like a beatdown deck and u-g madness plays like a counter sliver deck, so history seemed to be on our side. That being said, we had no real answer to the long-term power of Merfolk Looter and control elements like Krosan Reclamation and Aether Burst besides "I hope I draw Genesis”.

Furthermore, we knew that the u-g opponents would be siding heavily with Equilibrium and/or Callous Oppressor. We had no such room in our board, but a decent plan. We would take out our Roars (bad against bounce) and then have a huge advantage against Callous Oppressor card advantage. Naming "druid" would mean that our only legal targets would be hounds, largely countering the power of one of the opponent's most highly valued sideboard cards. Our proactive strategy was to control tempo with Turbulent Dreams, establish Wonder advantage with Krosan Reclamation and win with faster, bigger, threshold-enabled, men.

Round one, I got paired against my dream opponent, Canu-style Opposition. I easily took the first game, but kept a so-so hand of land and spells for duel number two. Playing u-g, it is difficult to throw away hands with access to both kinds of mana, but on turn three, I was extremely dissatisfied with my board of forest, forest, island and no action. Given my hand of Breakthrough, island, Genesis, Roar of the Wurm, Wonder and Ray of Revelation, I decided to Break for two, eventually keeping a perfect two-card hand of Nimble Mongoose and Werebear, with plenty of ammunition in the bin. Unfortunately for me, my opponent had drawn his sideboarded Krosan Reclamation and took a great deal of wind out of my sails to the tune of four relevant cards. In game three, the late game saw me with just a forest and an island in play for lands, but three Werebears (no threshold) and two Wild Mongrels against my opponent's three Wurm tokens and three mana creatures. Though I could have played for the win, a single Smother would have taken me out, so I slow-rolled the turn, planning for a win the next. My opponent ended up having two Smothers and killed me with his Wurms. From turn one, Turbulent Dreams - a card that would have guaranteed me victory - laughed at me from my hand.

That's okay, I thought. My deck is excellent. I can easily win out.

I didn't, of course, factor in the game loss I was about to get.

Luckily, I got paired against another u-g-b Opposition deck and won the first game in short order. But then game loss became match loss in an embarrassing way. In a replay of the previous round, I sat on all my green sources while my opponent forgot to create not five, not six, but seven Squirrel tokens. I toyed for a while with randomly discarding Wonder and attacking to see if he would notice my lack of islands, but I decided that if he was playing properly, I would just end up losing valuable beaters. I finally drew an island (Flooded Strand actually)... but it had to be on the last turn of the game, after he had just topdecked Opposition.

So there I was, down twice against my best matchup. It probably isn't interesting that I won out, beating even an AstroGlide deck, as 3-2 was not good enough to make the elimination rounds.

In sum, I think this deck is solid and I don't regret my choice to run it in Boston, but it is still subject to the inherent weaknesses of u-g. The single greatest flaw in u-g is its mana base... the deck wants both forests and islands early, and it doesn't always get them. I chose to mulligan aggressively, going to five without looking back, but this might not be a consistent strategy for a long eight-plus round tournament like Regionals. When the deck is winning, it can dominate almost any opponent (not AstroGlide usually), but when it is losing, it doesn't have a lot of cards that can dig it out of a hole. You can see from my losses that they each came from a lack of just one island. There are cards that standard u-g decks have used to this problem, but Yavimaya Coast is no longer legal and Merfolk Looter doesn't quite fit into this aggressive strategy.

If you want to try this deck, BDM suggests "adding a rudder”, some way to control the tempo of the game, maindeck. Justin Gary told him that his combination of permission and bounce had been invaluable in winning the mirror match, as either countering or sending away a Roar of the Wurm token with Boomerang would ensure victory, even when his opponents had played Quiet Speculation. I have messed around with the card drawing, adding Aether Burst and Basking Rootwalla, but would caution you that the main incentive to this deck is its speed and threat density. Remember that as you fiddle yourselves.

Until next time, may you draw at least single forest and double island.
