The other night, sitting at home, I realized I’ve become worn out of late. Since the day at Heathrow when I was informed I’d be sent back to Boston two months ago, life has been a little crazy. With relocation without my belongings, unexpected re-acquaintance with old friends and family, trips to Philadelphia, Houston, Seattle and L.A., holidays upon us and a brand new Limited environment to learn, sitting at my computer, looking at the **Magic** Online display after losing five or six drafts in a row through blurred eyes, I knew I needed a break.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>I think I’d been sitting at my computer too long. Staring at the screen too long. Making the same mechanical choices that had become second nature…</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

The thing is, it wasn’t because I hadn’t figured out the format. I think my grasp of *Onslaught* Limited is pretty solid, definitely better than my understanding of *Odyssey* Block Limited before I headed to Wisconsin to warm up for Pro Tour-Boston. I think I’d been sitting at my computer too long. Staring at the screen too long. Making the same mechanical choices that had become second nature with daily drafting, not thinking about them as I made them, leaving the pick ‘n click to instinct, not using the more conscious parts of my brain. In other words, I’d allowed drafting to become a monotonous task. I’d ceased to be a gamer.

While contemplating the ramifications of this, as a departure of sorts, I entered the draft queue once again, but this time it was for *Odyssey-Torment-Judgment*. This was the first time I’d taken my place in that queue since *Onslaught* had become available online, and the reasons for this - from a better draft set to a more powerful one to a more enjoyable card pool — were plentiful, but I needed something a little different. Just one of those little changes that forces you to open your eyes, register change and reawake that one thing which differentiates us from all other species (fish, bird, yuppie) on earth: self-awareness.

The *Odyssey* booster was opened and as I stared at its contents, it was abundantly apparent that I’d forgotten anything I knew about *Odyssey* booster draft in the months spent doing anything but thinking about it. I ended up with a very strong blue-white deck, losing the finals to a young woman named Haviva, but despite that frustration, I learned a lot from this draft. Namely, I learned that sometimes you need to separate yourself in order to gain new perspectives. Only with as many perspectives as possible in your arsenal will you be truly ready to go to the tables. With a renewed vigor, I’m ready to share some of my ideas on the format you may not have had shared with you before.

### The Most Misplayed Card I’ve Ever Seen is…

Haunted Cadaver, and when I say that, I mean it seriously. See if this situation feels familiar: your opponent, drawing first, hasn’t done anything before their turn three, while you’ve played a two-drop and a morph. On their third turn, they cast a morph, which after you’ve cast another creature, attacks and flips over, revealing it to be the Cadaver before its sacrifice for the greater good. Your opponent has just gotten 3-for-1 card advantage. They can’t lose, right? 

Not so fast.

Look at that table now. Sure, your opponent has a hand full of cards, but they have nothing facing your three creatures, and you get to attack and play whatever you draw before they have a chance to cast anything else, assuming they haven’t found themselves a two-casting cost creature since the end of their turn 2.

People, winning in **Magic** doesn’t come down to anything as clear and simple as card advantage. It used to, right up until the *Tempest* Prerelease, at which point Cursed Scroll introduced us to an entirely new concept that disregarded card advantage and put the onus on tempo. Now, we’ve reached a bit of an accord between the two.

Everything you have at your disposal within the confines of a game is a resource, from any life points you have beyond your last one, to the lands in play, to cards in hand to creatures on the board. Winning in **Magic**, as much as anything, comes down to understanding when to use which resources.

The sacrifice of the Cadaver in the above situation is almost universally the wrong one (if you’re holding Infest, Starstorm, Akroma's Vengeance or Slice and Dice, things do change). You’ve likely taken 4-6 points of damage by the time the Cadaver has attacked, taking 6 more points the turn after, meaning you have to stave off defeat when down to 10 life and facing three creatures to your none. Seeing the problem here?

When you sacrifice the Cadaver on turn four, it signifies a huge tempo shift in your opponent’s favor, especially when you can’t use your other two mana that turn. Cheap activation costs are useless if they leave your other mana lacking in usefulness, meaning that in exchange for those three discarded cards, you’ve given up a card of your own, turns three and four (the most important turns in any game) and the equivalent of seven mana. Fugue is starting to look like a power card. When deciding whether or not to sacrifice your Cadaver, try taking mana optimization into account. If your opponent is the type who is willing to take a morph attack once, likelihood is that they’d take it again, so it seems better to deal the two damage, keep the creature and attack next turn, when you can flip it over and still have enough mana to cast a morph creature.

Mana optimization, by the way, is the theory that suggests that the higher the percentage of mana you use, the better your deck is going to perform. In other words, for the purposes of Limited play, if you use one mana turn one, two turn two and so forth up to turn five or six, your chances of winning are going to be pretty good. If you can keep your unused mana for those turns under five, you’re bound to do pretty well.

### The Rare you Never Thought of

One advantage **Magic** Online has provided their users is the opportunity to use those cards one might not get to use otherwise. Each year, as a result of this, I seem to find one or two cards, usually rare, that other people haven’t figured out are pretty strong. I’ve made one such discovery in *Onslaught* (and no, I am not claiming myself to be the first to stumble upon the following piece of information, only that I figured it out without reading it elsewhere).

My card, this time around, has got to be the often-passed, seldom-played Death Match. About a month ago, I was drafting green-black in a draft with Eugene Harvey among six others, drafting a good green-black deck, when I opened Death Match in the third pack. Looking at the Match, particularly its wording ‘when a creature comes into play…’, I realized that it could make for some good gaming when combined with green-black staples like Symbiotic Elf and Nantuko Husk, passing it with the hopes it might come back. It did.

In that draft, Death Match single-handedly won four games and won a fifth, in the final against Eugene, when used in tandem with Centaur Glade. As brutal as that combination was, though, I came to realize it didn’t need to be anything so grotesque. All you really needed were cards like the aforementioned Elf, Symbiotic Beast, Rotlung Reanimator, or any other card that produces multiple creatures along with creatures with a toughness of four or greater. 

Time and again since that draft, when I’ve seen Death Match I’ve drafted it for the green-black deck (which best suits its needs) and each time it’s been outstanding, winning games I otherwise would have lost. I shared the info with a few friends too. The other day, I got the following message on IRC from Tim Aten:

“All I can say is that I’m so glad you told me about Death Match.”

It’s not great if your deck isn’t built to take advantage of it. If you’re green-black, this often passed-over rare is of first pick quality, and barring other excellent cards, is definitely too powerful to pass in the hopes of getting it back ninth. Pick it high and give it a shot. You won’t be disappointed.

A few final notes on the composite list:

With a total of 130 slots, white, not blue, seems to be the most underdrafted color in the set, with only nine slots filled by white cards. Interestingly, green and black both beat out red handily, taking 30 and 28 slots respectively, as compared with the set’s ‘best color’, which has only 23.5 of the slots. This may be a reflection on the fact that while red is coveted, those who draft the other colors are actually aiming to do so. Blue comes in with 16.5 slots (10th place tie = .5), while the cycling lands take 23.

<table><tbody><tr><td><h3>Composite List</h3>&#10;<p>1 Green 30/130<br> 2 Black 28/130<br> 3 Red 23.5/130<br> 4 Lands 23/130<br> 5 Blue 16.5/130<br> 6 White 9/130</p>&#10;<p>Cycling Cards 43/130<br> Morph Cards 23/130</p>&#10;</td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

The importance of the lands is felt in the fact that 43 slots, or one third, are filled by cards with cycling, while cards with morph take 23. In other words, the pros are drafting those cards that a) get them closer to their bombs and b) are drafting cards that ensure they’ll have something to do on turn three, regardless of color.

So what can you take from these lists? Simple: if you see card names repeating themselves, perhaps you should be re-evaluating your own take on their viability. If you have cards in your top ten that aren’t shown here, you may be valuing them too highly. Regardless, these lists provide a blueprint: you still have to mortar the bricks.
