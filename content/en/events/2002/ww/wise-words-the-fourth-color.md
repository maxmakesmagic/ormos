Each year, as the new draft set in question lays itself out before us, patterns emerge. In *Mercadian Masques*, white and black were the colors to draft. In *Invasion*, it was green that linked the colors together. In *Odyssey*, blue and green were the colors of choice. And today, everyone and their sister want to be the red mage.

While the ‘which color is best?’ patterns make themselves obvious after a short time, the question as to which is the best color to draft is more difficult to answer. More often than not, the answer to that lies within each individual drafter, with their styles and tastes required to make the archetypes work. With each person only possessed of so many ideas and temperaments, the first try is obviously not always going to be a match.

While red is almost universally considered the set’s strongest color, if last article’s top 10 lists showed us anything, it’s that red is not the only color worth drafting. Some of our samples showed leanings towards blue-red or red in general, sure, but some of the others more or less told us that some of the finest players in the world had seen fit to make sharp departures from those coveted mountains. The thing is, none of those roads leading away head towards the plains.

With all of the negative press blue has gotten over the last couple of months, *Onslaught* Limited’s red-headed step child seems to have found its place in one of the format’s most popular color combinations, alongside red. That means that while blue is still the weakest color in the set card-for-card, it may not be the most avoided. Watch any Pro Tour draft and you’ll find two players, usually on opposite sides of the table, grabbing blue and not looking back. Who’s grabbing white?

White has a lot going for it. The depth is ridiculous, to the point where Brian Kibler has stated he’d rather open Pacifism then Sparksmith so he’d have the excuse to draft it. White has the best flyers in the set, strong morphs, good defensive ground creatures, enchantment kill, creature control and a lot of good tricks. So why is it not being drafted?

The theory I’ve been telling those who’ll bother to listen is that the lack of white drafters exists because of the lack of a marquee common. Red has Sparksmith, Shock and Solar Blast, luring people in from the first pick onwards. You open the pack, look immediately at the rare and uncommons, and if there’s nothing there, you look immediately for the red cards (unless you’re Jens Thoren, who takes the Nantuko Husk even if the rare’s a good one). Likewise, black offers the answer of all answers, Cruel Revival, as well as the Husk, which seems to grow more important to every deck it gets played in with each passing day. Green offers the ridiculous Snarling Undorak and the cornerstone Wirewood Savage (and due to misrepresentation, Krosan Tusker. I’m not a huge fan). Those cards, plus a number of uncommon bombs like Centaur Glade, Slice and Dice and Death Pulse, give players plenty of opportunities to get roped into those colors. So why not white?

Well, it’s strange. Pacifism is as good as most of the commons listed last paragraph, but it gets a bad rap. Because of the blue Wormfangs in *Judgment* and the gating creatures in *Planeshift*, the two Limited environments that have dominated the hearts and minds of all gamers for the last couple of years, have shown that enchanting opposing creatures can be a somewhat futile strategy. While that isn’t true of *Onslaught* Limited, where there’s no common bounce and relatively little main deck enchantment kill (Piety Charm is the only Pacifism removal being played with regularity), the residual memories of crushed hopes and broken dreams seems to have dulled Pacifism’s value in the minds of most drafters.

With white’s primary common dulled in value by so many, the color lacks a marquee common, meaning it’s difficult for a player to get into the color without a rare or uncommon excuse, but even then, with the best uncommons being the undervalued Whipcorder and Improvised Armor, players seem to be avoiding plains like the plague. They’re missing out.

The thing that makes white so attractive to me is its depth. In the soldiers installment of my *Onslaught* Limited Review, I found only one card I considered to be unplayable, Gustcloak Runner, and then days later, while talking to Dirk Baberowski (admittedly a lover of 1/1 guys), found that ‘Juggernaut II’ loved the Runner. When an entire tribe avoids flaw, you know you have something good.

White’s depth allows you to draft cycle lands with more emphasis due to the security of knowing you’re going to get your twenty-three cards, which in turn makes for a more cohesive deck, be it the aggressive soldiers (best with red), the more control-based clerics (best with black) or some hybrid of the two (do what you want). With fewer drafters concentrated on the color, it makes tribal drafting a lot easier with cards like Piety Charm, Grassland Crusader, Shieldmage Elder and sideboard-worthy Unified Strike proving ripe for the picking around 10th, 11th, or later.

Still not convinced? I was looking over the Grand Prix coverage for last season hoping to pick up a tidbit or two to help me in my quest for success in Chicago when I stumbled across this nice little article by Josh Bennett in the GP-LA coverage called *The 3-0’s Speak*. While Robert ‘Brightstone’ Bayardo’s deck isn’t listed, of the seven that are, five were running plains. Time and again, I’ve been hearing stories of players playing white and facing ‘the other white player’ at the table in the finals. The color can support four drafters. If there are only going to be two at a table, I definitely want to be one of them.

With the Limited review focusing on the tribes, I thought it prudent to take a look at white as a whole and rank the cards with a few comments. In the following weeks, you’ll find some similar lists for the other colors in this space. The list assumes your deck isn’t entirely tribal while at the same time recognizing that there are elements of tribal in every deck. The point I’m trying to make is this: you shouldn’t necessarily be forcing white, but right now it’s the most underdrafted color in the set. If you get a third-pick Pacifism, Catapult Squad or Exalted Angel (okay, that’s a bit unrealistic), it’s a signal that those player passing to you likely aren’t running the plains. If that’s the case, don’t you think you should? The cards…
