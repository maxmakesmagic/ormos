### 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/fa/85/fa855dc3ad9c9522fcd7a57d94ed4968b6165c4880f9b360cfc83808e9dfa8b6.jpg)The first thing most players do when attempting to dissect a new set for Limited play is assign values to the fresh crop of cardboard. Not only is this unproductive, but most of the time, it is just blatantly incorrect. Giving definitive values to cards before actually playing enough matches to get a feel for the new format will generally only result in error. Take *Onslaught* as our most recent case in point. Picks like Shock over Solar Blast and Snarling Undorak over Wirewood Savage seem like no-brainers in today’s drafting environment, but if you’ve been here since the beginning, you’ll remember that it wasn’t always so clearly cut. When the set first came out, there were many cards that were difficult to rate and, more importantly, compare to others of the same type. The reason they have become ironed out is a direct product of two basic factors: experience and time. 

Experience is pretty easy to define in this case, as it goes without saying that the more experience you have with a particular format, the better you will understand it. Time, however, is a completely different animal. In my opinion, time is, by far, the more influential factor in the evolution of understanding of a draft format. Regardless of personal experience, the longer a card pool is out there for experimentation, the more defined it will become. Experience is a factor that can be individually controlled, while it’s pretty obvious that you can’t stop or attempt to control time in any way other than abiding by it. It is because of this that jumping immediately into the card valuations when a new set comes out is inherently flawed. I think we all need to take a step back and look at the bigger picture.

Despite all of this, there is indeed much to be learned through analysis. Without looking at specific cards just yet, we can still broaden our field of vision to the global impact of a dynamic set like *Legions*. I say dynamic, because card type restriction is something that has been very loose in the past and Legions is now going to turn up the dial to the utmost extreme. It’s the same type of change that we had last year with the color imbalances beginning in *Torment*. This breed of situations forces us to reevaluate many of the basic principles of the game, which can never be a bad thing.

Essentially, I think you have to look at the set’s effect as a whole before you can get into the cards themselves. *Legions*, in particular, offers a few very important changes to the *Onslaught* draft format we’ve all come to love.

### Spells vs. Creatures

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/3d/60/3d60377e3695a485e3446729f068f6b894fcb9b432612ba3b43b1980ee5389ab.jpg"><br><i> Take tricks higher.</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

One thing I’ve observed during the past few weeks of drafting through my own errors is that a lot of the spells in *Onslaught* are now more important than creatures that were originally ranked higher in triple *Onslaught* draft. Knowing that pack three of any draft is going to contain *only* creature cards goes a long way in terms of changing some of the picks you make in *Onslaught*. Because of the *Legions* pack, a lot of the tricks that are in *Onslaught* are now significantly more valuable to your deck. The times when you’d take Krosan Tusker or sometimes Elvish Warrior over Vitality Charm are coming to an abrupt end. Since you are guaranteed to at least get some guys (and usually more than that) for your deck from the *Legions* booster, a word to the wise would be to start taking the combat tricks and other spells higher than ever before. Tricks like Choking Tethers, Wave of Indifference, and Dirge of Dread are the same way and should certainly earn a promotion in everyone’s pick orders. Removal spells have always been top notch on everyone’s lists and, if anything, should only grow more prevalent now, but I think that goes without saying.

You could argue that while you’ll certainly have a crack at creatures in *Legions*, you aren’t guaranteed to get the good ones. This is definitely a valid point, but the fact remains that a deck filled with only guys is not going to be very flexible. I have made a number of picks that were certainly wrong when I look back on them, based solely on this principle. 

The other point of contention is that the new morph trigger creatures in *Legions* act as pseudo spells. This is indeed true, but they are also much slower than their counterpart spells in *Onslaught* and the environment doesn’t show any signs of slowing down in the tempo department (Skirk Marauder vs. Shock should give you an idea). For this loss of tempo, you do gain card advantage, so it’s not a total loss situation, but the environment is far too fast to rely on only these effects.

While this isn’t a change of apocalyptic proportions, those who fail to abide by it will be at an observable disadvantage. A deck full of creatures that has only morph triggers as tricks has quite a difficult time standing up to the more diverse decks with all the weapons. 

### Two Drops

The central point of a creature mana curve in all-*Onslaught* draft was clearly the three drop. This is simply a fact created by the morph mechanic. Make a set with a bunch of colorless Gray Ogres and the three drop is certain to be flooded with plays. *Legions* does little more than enforce this and also give more options for coming out ahead in it. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/16/94/1694d8892472fdd0e235a40337ee634509a7da41b6618a493db0ff3a296e9e9f.jpg)Two drops are surely the most efficient way to get ahead in the tempo battle, especially the two power ones. *Legions* offers quite a few playable two drops and we should take advantage of them. Case in point: Stonewood Invoker should be taken higher than most of the other green commons just because it is a reliable two drop and gives you a good jump on the damage race and board position. This is nothing new though, as we’ve known this since *Onslaught*.

The other aspect of *Legions* is the large number of high casting cost creatures without morph in the common slot. This goes even farther to promote decks with tight mana curves and plenty of two drops, as these slower creatures can cause decks to stumble, in which case, your consistent curve will be far too much. Basically, there are lots of very good two drops in *Legions* and, therefore, we should be taking advantage of them in our drafting.

### Morphs

When scanning over the new morphs in *Legions*, one thing is readily apparent. Not one of them punishes you for blocking. This was partially covered in my [Battering Craghorn Dilemma article](https://web.archive.org/web/20040917082631/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.starcitygames.com/php/news/expandnews.php?Article=4418&origin=sideboard_strategy_20030213a) on Star City, but it bears repeating, because it is a valid attribute of the set as a whole. The lessened need to block incoming morphs, combined with the rise in value of cards like Skirk Commando and Haunted Cadaver is explained more in-depth in the Star City article, so I won’t get into it here and hopefully everyone knows what I’m talking about. After that, we have a very new and different take on the new morph mechanic that is introduced in *Legions*. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/66/e1/66e186af71ca20c0e8923861b3b5c4f5ccfeeb1c8dee5b722bded7dc902d5e97.jpg)This new approach is really divided into a few parts. The first is unquestionably the morph triggers that act as creature removal. This mechanic opens many new doors in terms of game theory. This is the only *new* reason to block a morph, in order to get rid of a would-be two-for-one later. The triggers also make you take into account things like a possible Echo Tracer or Skinthinner lurking somewhere in the ranks of the face-downs to destroy your well-laid-plans. It doesn’t stop there though, as the triggers go far beyond the removal and bounce effects.

Effects like the one on Liege of the Axe are the ones that really throw a wrench into anyone’s system when it comes to determining what a face-down creature could be. These kinds of things are great for the game because they break all of the rules and keep it real in the long run.

Finally, we have the triggers that return creatures to their original face-down position. Yet another level of depth is added to the mechanic through the creation of these creatures and the many possible situations they bring about. You can reuse another morph trigger guy, kill a bigger guy if your opponent doesn’t have enough mana to morph it back up, or even give things like Crude Rampart a second try at the face down aspect of their existence. 

Morph hasn’t really changed. It’s just gotten more complicated and interesting than ever before.

### The New Format

*Onslaught*-only draft was characterized most often by the necessity of having removal in your deck and drafting one of the two removal colors: red or black. This was due to the extremely powerful utility creatures and enchantments available in the common slot. A lot of questions have been answered with Legions, but some things do in fact remain the same.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/9a/53/9a53e37ed33cbb058823a4732e94564a87e9ba522876d9516fb98602343acba9.jpg)Unfortunately, there are now even more annoying utility creatures. Take Timberwatch Elf as the main example and you’ll see what I’m talking about. There’s also the obvious bomb for the cleric deck: Whipgrass Entangler. Both of these cards will dominate the board if left unchecked, which again requires a call to arms in the department of drafting removal.

However, the plea has not come without reply, as *Legions* does indeed offer some answers to the annoyance of utility. There are now flexible enchantment-killing morphs that you can play in any deck without the usual drawback of running enchantment removal: having a dead card. These go a long way to dealing with powerful enchantments like Lavamancer’s Skill and Improvised Armor that would otherwise end the game in a quick fashion. 

One thing notably lacking from *Onslaught* was a good bounce spell. That has finally been printed, in the form of Echo Tracer. This changes the format a good deal, with just the threat of it being there making a number of things less impressive than before.

The final implementation is the pseudo-removal ability provoke. Excellent for removing utility guys without tap abilities, while never being as good as real removal, provoke definitely makes for more exciting attack steps. All of these things make for a more interesting environment, with many possibilities left to be explored in the coming months.
