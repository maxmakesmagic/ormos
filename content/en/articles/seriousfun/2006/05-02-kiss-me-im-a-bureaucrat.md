I live many lives – father, husband, writer, gamer, politician, dog owner. But you can know all that about me and still not know what I do during the day shift. I don't talk about it much because I take it more seriously than I take most things, and this isn't exactly a serious column (name aside).

However, in a week like Azorius week, which focuses on a color combination increasingly famous for its "bureaucrats" and "rules", it seems right to make a public admission to you all.

By night, I am the avenging multiplayer enthusiast you have all come to know and love. **But by day, I am a mild-mannered bureaucrat.**

There, it's out there. Nothing to do now but go on.

My day job is the Director of Dislocated Worker and Trade Adjustment Assistance Programs, at the Minnesota Department of Employment and Economic Development. (Rolls right off the tongue, doesn't it?) The programs I manage total about $50 million per year, and serve anywhere from 20,000 to more than 30,000 people annually, depending on how well the economy's doing. Our team's job? To get laid-off workers re-employed, as quickly as possible, in appropriate jobs with wages close to what they had before.

Now, this isn't as exciting as being a writer for *Roseanne*, I'll grant you – but on the bright side, I won't be telling you stories about it every week. This week, however, I'm going to give you some insights on the bureaucratic life. While mentioning some hot White/Blue cards, of course.

I'm particularly concerned with the public sector bureaucrat, since that's really where the pejorative use of the term started. (Of course, there are private sector bureaucrats as well. Many people fool themselves into thinking this is somehow a superior breed.) Whichever type you're talking about – and I'll generally refer to the public kind from here on in – bureaucrats are sorely misunderstood, and many people twist the word in an effort to put down other people who give up an awful lot in order to help others.

Time to learn better.

**SUBPART A: Bureaucrats assume you don't know any better. This can be a problem.**

Why do many bureaucrats think they know better than the customers they presume to serve? This is partly because large swathes of humanity have a long history of *not* knowing any better. It is also partly because of a long history of arrogance among well-educated people. Those people get into certain jobs, and try to help… and they breed resentment, because even really smart people can overreach.

For example, someone in charge may decide you don't need a firearm, or a driver's license, or access to a national park today. Instead, what you really need is birds. Lots and lots of birds.

**SUBPART B: You would live a less comfortable life, were it not for the work of a bureaucrat.**

As a fiscal conservative, I have a rather long list of government programs I wouldn't mind seeing shaved back or cut altogether, but it always gives me a kick whenever I hear someone talk about government as if they've never used it. Setting aside obvious things like police protection and schools, I want to ask: you've never driven over a bridge? Never eaten at a restaurant, or bought meat at a grocery store? Never taken medicine? 

Bureaucrats operate behind an awful lot of scenes. Their influence is often subtle. Like this card:

The public programs bureaucrats run can save your life, without you (or us) ever knowing it. That's a major frustration for some, since it's often hard to prove what might have happened if there had never been a bridge, or if there had been rats in the kitchen or bacteria in the meat one day, or if the medicine you took was nothing more than a placebo with red dye.

Sometimes, you have to take it on faith that the simple enchantment you plopped down is the thing that's keeping opponents from attacking you, or targeting your creature. You're pretty sure the Shield is why… but you can never really prove it.

The Shield doesn't mind getting less than 100 percent of the credit, but it probably wouldn't mind getting more than zero. Be nice to the Shield. Tell it how much you appreciate it. Heck, call your state senator and representative and tell them what a great job that Shield did for you! You'll get a warm and fuzzy feeling from it all.

**SUBPART C: Bureaucrats slow things down. This is usually, but not always, bad.**

Long lines at the Department of Motor Vehicles offices stink. Lots of paperwork for a simple fishing license becomes obnoxious. And government is like every large organization nowadays – really good at creating voice mail hell.

But beyond those obvious examples of inefficiency, there are lesser-recognized examples of times when you want things to go more slowly.

For example, you *want* an architect to take the time to design a building to code. You *want* a legislature to take the time to debate a law that will affect your life. You *want* inspectors to examine cargo containers before they arrive in your country's ports.

And you want enemies to think hard before attacking you.

Am I saying you should be thankful for having to wait? Heck, no. But I am saying you should take the time to question why something is going more slowly than you'd like – in **Magic**, in life, in your relationships, and anywhere else. Sometimes, there's a reason. If the reason's good, reflecting on it can ease the pain of the wait. If the reason stinks, at least thinking about can give you the approach you need to speed things up.

**SUBPART D: Bureaucrats spend a lot of time making and enforcing rules, usually because someone who looks an awful lot like you *asked* for that rule.**

How do most laws happen? Attention-grabbing lobbyist stories aside, most laws happen because a citizen (or group of citizens) calls up a legislator and demands it. The legislator draws up a bill, gets it passed, and assumes the bureaucrats will handle things from there.

How do most bureaucratic rules happen? A citizen (or group of citizens) calls up the bureaucrat – or a legislator who then calls the bureaucrat – and complains about an inflexible rule they want changed (which eventually becomes a loophole someone else wants closed).

Some law or rule you hate, is very likely something someone else loves.

My favorite rules in government are those that are clear and simple to understand. I don't even necessarily have to agree with those rules – I just admire the fact that they don't screw around with tons of words in an effort to confuse people or make the writer sound more important than they actually are.

A simple, clear rule is easier to change than a complex, twisted rule. That means the public can see its effect more clearly, and decide whether or not that rule (or the Arbiter enforcing it!) should be, shall we say, terminated.

**SUBPART E: Bureaucrats have lives outside of the office.**

Whereas I like my rules simple, I also like my bureaucrats complex. On my small team of 13 staff, there's a young woman who modified her own sports car with custom intake and exhaust systems, a septuagenarian who used to be a former Marine and an executive at a can company, a hockey mom, an entrepreneur, and dozens more stories – because they're all smart enough to avoid letting their work define them completely.

Yes, we all enjoy that we're public servants. We love that we're helping people, and we get a charge out of finding ways to save taxpayer money. But we all get a heck of a lot more out of our families and friends, our hobbies, and the other stuff that surrounds us when we get home.

Underestimate us, and we can kinda sneak up and surprise you.

When you meet a bureaucrat, are you sure you know what you're getting? Are you absolutely certain of what this person can do for you? Could this be someone who makes your life easier – or someone who's going to make you miserable?

The next time you meet a bureaucrat, put a little effort into the relationship. Some of us can have pretty high maintenance personalities, but we'll jump over tall buildings for you if we get the chance. We may even break open a few games of Magic for you.
