*“…and so, another Prerelease weekend comes to rest, and they were contented”* –Gary Wise, May 21st, 2003-05-21

Okay, okay. It's cheesy to quote yourself. It's cheesy to start an article with a quote, it's cheesy to make up a quote the day you write the article in question and it's definitely cheesy to put it all together, but I needed an opening for this article and had nothing better to offer, and you have to admit it adds panache.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/0d/5e/0d5e392537c216511eff6fe51c431ed8645f7f4235863c5aa04913fcbf8cc8f1.jpg)No? Forget you then. It's been a crazy week, full of unsuccessful furniture shopping and a bunch of other non-**Magic** related stuff, and when you throw in a Prerelease weekend, I'm pretty much spent, so if my openings “border” on cheesy, you'll have to pardon me, because at least it's an opening, and that will allow me to segue into a collection of thoughts all relating to *Scourge* in Limited.

Speaking of *Scourge* (see?), the Prerelease was abuzz as per usual here in Toronto (and yes, I'm living here for the foreseeable future), with small children screeching violently at every turn as they got their hands on multiple cards that looked like “\[proper name\], the \[impressive title\]”, marked by either dragon or legend status and a couple of rather large digits in the lower right hand corner. *Scourge* is fun. A lot of it. It's Timmy personified, with a ridiculous eighteen cards having to do with the newest and biggest of the winged, toothy varieties of tribe. You think people have whined about losing to bombs in the past? This set's going to make your ears bleed:

**Zombie Cutthroat** – It sure looks black, and it definitely costs black mana, but you don't need swamps to play with it. In a tempo-crucial format that just got even more stupidly slow cards, the Cutthroat is faster than a speeding bullet (more on this later), allowing you to flip it over with or without mana available to you. Five life might seem like a lot, but when it's used for card advantage that leaves a 3/4 body in play on turn four, the effect is devastating. Yes, you're right, if you're at low life you can't flip it over, but you know what? We don't plan to be on low life, we plan for our opponents to be, and this guy's going to take charge in a nasty way to ensure those plans come to fruition.

**Rush of Knowledge** – Remember Allied Strategies? Remember how happy we were when we'd cast it for three? Well, apparently, that card was crap, because for the same price, we don't need to build our decks completely around the concept, and we potentially get to draw a lo-ho-ho-ho-hot more cards. Unless your opponent is holding a Starstorm to nullify your permanents, Rush is going to win the game instantly as often - if not more often - than not. Keep in mind that you don't need to cast it on turn five, just trade cards with your opponent, then when you're down to 1-2 each, draw five and win!

**Lingering Death** – Though there are definitely challengers, I think this is likely the best common removal spell in the format. “I'm definitely running enchantment removal main when *Scourge* comes in” quipped Victor van den Broek, and while a card like Naturalize may not be warranted, I can see why Nantuko Vigilante would most certainly be so. Yes, the Death works its magic a little slower than we're accustomed to creature kill doing, but it does it for so little mana, killing everything from Visara to Sparky to black creatures to Zombies. No limitations in the potential victims department. That feels like a pretty big deal to me.

### I Am Superman

Rearrange those letters and that heading turns out to be an anagram of Pemmin's Aura. Isn't WotC clever? I don't remember which variety it was, but there was one kind of kryptonite that gave humans Kryptonian powers, and I guess, like the name says, this is it.

While Morphling was obviously the nuts, it didn't have many functions. Granted, its sole function was “make your opponent die in the most disgusting way possible”, but it didn't kill creatures did it? Jon Stern, he of the Top 8 near miss in Yokohama, was visiting our little berg, and while drafting at the Prerelease, he noticed you could target an opposing creature and pump it so full of +1/-1s that it'd pop. We all just rolled our eyes.

Blue got shafted by design in this block, with the constant complaining of green mages everywhere finally getting on R&D's nerves to the point where *Onslaught* was released to force the Wakefieldians to pipe down, but this is one amazing card. It's going to win a lot of games, sure, but it's also never going to be bad, kind of like its predecessor. Pemmin must be some kind of superman.

### Hate Waiting to Happen

With most National Championships and Worlds using Rochester instead of Booster this year, I can't help but look forward with dread and glee to the bloodbath that's going to occur when Scourge comes around. Don't get me wrong, we've seen worse in the past (*Invasion* block), but the land cycling cards are going to diversify this, an environment that up to this point has been almost strictly a two-color affair.

Think about it. We're talking about a series of very powerful creatures here, almost all of which are easy to cast, while at the same time also aiding your mana with regards to other splash cards. Heck, if you want to, you could even just splash three of the same land cycler with one of the lands in question, though I must admit, that could leave you in a somewhat sticky situation. Probably better to play two of the land in question.

I'm not even talking about the obviously ridiculous Eternal Dragon here. Some of the more common varieties are capable of completely dominating the game. Noble Templar is huge and qualifies for both the soldier and cleric decks; Shoreline Ranger is just a massive flyer; Twisted Abomination is obviously a ridiculous problem for anyone to deal with; Chartooth Cougar might as well be called “The Abyss”, while Elvish Aberration and Wirewood Guardian are, in a word, huge.

Gab Tsang put forth an interesting theory with regards to land cycling: It may well be better than cycling. The logic? Well, if you cycle in the early game, what are you hoping to draw? More likely than not, land, and these guys provide you with that for a very reasonable price that falls nicely into the “just-before-the-always-glutted-up” three slot. I'm not sure it is better: the knowledge you'll drawing a land in the late game has to be a dampener, but still, I think land cycling is definitely better than it appeared at first look, at least to me.
