This week I returned to Toronto to start visa proceedings, get my computer fixed and see family and friends. It's a little strange going back home for the first time after you've left, because while everything looks the same, it feels a little different, kind of the way your junior kindergarten classroom would look if you went there now. The world as I once knew it is warped, and somehow a little smaller.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>Recording our history is important. It reminds us of the good, bad and amusing, helps us learn from the mistakes of others and, maybe most importantly, it leaves a mark.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

As I've written in this space over the years, one of my great passions in this world is the second best game in the universe, baseball. Most of my European friends can't relate, but there's something poetic about baseball that other sports just can't reproduce: history. The smell of the grass, the crack of the bat, the signing of autographs... it all goes back over a hundred years, but unlike other games, every iota has been recorded in almost infinite detail. It's that recorded history that allows me to relate to those millions upon millions who have watched the professional game going back to the 1800s. I don't see myself in the past spectators of other sports.

My trip home happily coincided with the opening of the 2002 baseball season (go Jays!), and in doing so reminded me of how the grand old game has warped my approach to the following and coverage of this one. Because of all the reading I was able to do on baseball, I really enjoy recording **Magic's** history, because who knows: maybe some information-hungry, thirteen year old kid will want to know who won Pro Tour-Barcelona a hundred years from now. That's what this week's Wise Words is about.

Recording our history is important. It reminds us of the good, bad and amusing, helps us learn from the mistakes of others and, maybe most importantly, it leaves a mark. When something happens without being recorded, it can erode away with the minds of the observers. When it's put to paper though (or in this case, the computer screen), it can conceivably last forever. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/protour/images/pt_lg.gif)What follows is a reminder of Pro Tours past. For some of you this will be a happy walk down memory lane, for others it will finally put accomplishments to the names you've heard but can't identify with. Hopefully, the list will continue to grow for as long as baseball's has.

Specifically, this is a brief look at every Pro Tour of the modern age, which begins with the first professional tournament in New York (with apologies to misters Dolan and Blumke). This week looks at the tour's first three seasons, next week will bring you up to date.

### 1995-96

Back then, the world was playing **Magic** under a different set of rules. The stack hadn't been created, no one understood the timing rules and organizers could be creative with their formats because nothing had been established. These, in **Magic**, are what people are referring to when they talk about the good old days.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/69/a8/69a8fb8fb63d27f465cb0662a0241b758f4c8715ab6a20dfb8124e3e46dfcc3b.jpg"><br><i>One of the more unusual cards in Loconto's deck.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**New York** - Seven years ago, Wizards of the Coast announced they'd be holding the first Professional tournament in New York. One "qualified" by calling a hotline and leaving their name and credit card number, with the result being that a number of those players who qualified weren't really qualified professionals. Fortunately, the cream were able to rise to the top, with half the Top 8 eventually making it back to that hallowed plateau.

Playing an unusual Standard variant that required five cards from every set available, in the final, Mike Loconto's blue-white control deck beat Bertrand Lestree's green deck. The night before the tournament, Bertrand met Dennis Bentley in the player's lounge and played him, losing so horribly that he felt he had to ask the eventual U.S. Champ for a decklist. Bertrand changed one card, taking out Feldon's Cane for an Icy Manipulator, and while the change fared him well throughout the tournament, in the final, the Cane would have been the decider. Lestree, one of the Tour's most colorful characters, would go on to party a lot and punch out a judge at future tournaments.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/USNAT01/929.jpg"><br><i>The Hammer</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

**Los Angeles** - For the first few years of the Pro Tour, if there was a cover boy, it was Shawn 'Hammer' Regnier. A former professional arm wrestler, Hammer's pythons weren't his only intimidator, backing them up with some of the hardest trash talk the Pro Tour has ever seen. Playing on the historic Queen Mary in the first Limited Pro Tour, Hammer met up with then-rival-later-friend Tom Guevin, who history has inaccurately said was forced to vomit part way through the final match by Hammer's rhetoric. Put in his situation, I might have been forced to upchuck myself, with Hammer's Control Magic grabbing Tom's big bomb, Shivan Dragon, which the big man rode to victory. In case you think seven years is a long time, if you look at the Top 8, you'll see that it really isn't. Darwin Kastle came third, Scott Johns fifth.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/GPAMS01/945.jpg"><br><i>The Littlest Viking</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

**Columbus** - Playing the first ever sanctioned Block Constructed tournament, the players of the world gathered in Columbus, Ohio at Origins for a Pro Tour that would share the spotlight with U.S. Nationals. Not surprisingly, no one dominated both, with Sean Fleischman being the only American in the Top 4. Fleischman lost to a little Swedish kid playing a red-green deck full of 2/3s named Olle Rade. It was a good start for what may be the best Constructed player of all time whose name doesn't start with 'K' and end with 'ai Budde'.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/3e/4a/3e4ad8b5efad070160a7906a4d72ee7f299ab0f5b02c512565efe70a48014d8d.jpg"><br><i>My kingdom for an Adarkar Wastes!</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**World Championships** - Back before time began, World Championships was always held in Seattle, near Wizards of the Coast's home office. With the stars of the newly formed Pro Tour converging, it was an Australian unknown, Tom Chanpheng who won the tournament. Playing white weenie in the Standard portion of the tournament, Chanpheng misrecorded his decklist, failing to mark down the Adarkar Wastes he was playing in order to cast his one Sleight of Mind, and as a result he was left without blue mana and with a dead card in his maindeck. Tom beat Mark Justice, generally considered the best player in the world at the time, in a final that featured a huge error from the U.S. Champ, who cast two Demonic Consultations in response to the Aussie's Armageddon. The resolutions left Justice without the mana to cast Ishan's Shade, and eventually cost him the match.

### 1996-97

With a season under its belt, the Pro Tour was making strong headway towards the format we're accustomed to today. The Tour's second season included five Pro Tours plus World Championships, with stops in New York, Los Angeles and Europe, a pattern that would be repeated for years to come.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/13/17/13174829e640bd7d43c1f3b14ba37125d1fb867058a2e341a12abccb0c6e0c21.jpg"><br><i>The card to have.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**Atlanta** - The first and only sealed deck Pro Tour, Atlanta's format was especially unusual in that the combatants had never seen the cards before. Atlanta premiered *Mirage* block, and because of the lack of familiarity with the format, talent and luck overshadowed preparation. In the end, one of the game's least heralded champions, Frank Adler, took the title, defeating Darwin Kastle in the finals.

The most memorable and historically important moment of this tournament though, came in Darwin's quarterfinals vs. Terry Borer. With victory at hand, Borer attacked with three creatures whilst holding a Grave Servitude, knowing that Darwin couldn't stop lethal damage, but Terry, unfamiliar with the timing rules just like everyone else at the time, asked Darwin if he had any effects before his intended casting of the Servitude. The one man in the room who seemed to know those rules was Tom Wylie, and he intervened, with Terry losing the match as a result. Players have known to not relinquish priority ever since.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/USNAT01/788.jpg"><br><i>Years later, Jason Zila surprised everyone by showing up at the Masters series.</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

**Dallas** - Borer's teammate Paul McCabe made the finals of the junior division twice in the Tour's first season, but frustrated at the lack of money (junior prizes were given out in the form of scholarships), he decided it was time to graduate and play with the big boys. Turns out they weren't much competition for him either, as McCabe, playing Necro, won Dallas without losing a duel in the Top 8, finishing things off with a win over Jason Zila's Prison deck. McCabe would go on to win Player of the Year.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/WORLDS2000/images/342.jpg"><br><i>You know, it seems like more of these folks had hair in the good old days...</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

**Los Angeles** - With the most controversial Pro Tour finish of all time, 96-97 PT-LA will forever remain one for the ages. In the finals of this Rochester PT, David Mills and his blue-white deck were cruising along against Tommi Hovi's red-green when chaos ensued. For the third time in the tournament, Mills played a spell, then tapped his lands for it, at the time an illegal action. After much deliberation, Mills was disqualified with prizes and chaos ensued. Led by Mark Justice, many of the American players let their rage be known in what has come to be known as our version of the LA riots. Mills would make one more final before eventually disappearing into seclusion.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/GPKYO00/963.jpg)**Paris** - A lot of negative things have been said about this Top 8, but you can't deny its star power. At the top, Mike Long finished first, taking his only Pro Tour by defeating long time friend/foe Mark Justice while playing the first of the truly great combo decks, ProsBloom. The most memorable moment of the match came when Justice, not realizing that all of Long's win conditions had been removed, conceded when Mike started going off. It would be the closest Justice would come to winning a Pro Tour.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/15/01/1501e6d5ce015c7f8cb88e2635c0f843b3c86b40125dbbd0296e4d78e668651d.jpg)**New York** - Out to avenge his Atlanta misfortunes, Terry Borer, believed by some to be possessed of one of the top five minds for the game on this planet, nailed it in New York, storming through another star-studded Top 8, finally defeating Ivan Stanoev in the final after psyching the European out by shuffling for over half an hour prior to the finals. "I just like shuffling" Terry would recount with a wry smile on his face. "I can't help it if other people don't appreciate it". Borer would soon thereafter become the first pro player to turn down Grand Prix byes, stating his preference to play the first three rounds of GP-Toronto.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/GPPRA01/944.jpg)**World Championships** - Ask Pro Tour insiders to name the nicest guys in the game and Jakub Slemr's moniker is sure to make an appearance. Jakub made his first of his three Top 8s at Worlds '97 and made the most of it, winning with his four-color-black deck despite having gone just 4-3 in the Standard portion of the tournament. Jakub defeated Janosh Kuhn in the finals, but it was Kuhn who knocked out Gab Tsang in the quarterfinals. Tsang finished the Swiss five points ahead of the field, becoming the only player to date to finish two consecutive Pro Tours first in the Swiss.

### 1997-98

Despite American proclamations of Pro Tour supremacy, the record hadn't backed their claims until this season, when the U.S. won every Pro Tour title available. 1997-98 also marked the emergence of Jon Finkel as the world's best player, a reign that most feel only ended this year.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/USNAT01/987a.jpg"><br><i>Check out Randy's column on MagictheGathering.com!</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

**Chicago** - Every Pro Tour since Tom Chanpheng's World Championship had been won by a veteran until the first Chicago. Coming out of Carnegie Melon University, Randy Buehler shocked the world with his Extended win over David Mills in what many feel was the strongest Top 8 ever. Playing a three-color Necro deck designed by teammate Erik Lauer, Randy defeated Tongo, a blue-white-red deck featuring Frenetic Efreets, then went on to become one of the most consistent performers in the game's history. He eventually graduated from the Pro Tour to become a member of R&D, where he still is today.

![](http://www.wizards.com/international/sideboard/images/Matt_Place.gif)**Mainz** - One of the game's greatest players before the Pro Tour, Matt Place seemed to be fading from the landscape until he won PT-Mainz. Matt had only Rochestered Tempest block three times before his appearance in this tournament, but his natural ability guided him through, eventually netting him the win over Steve O'Mahoney-Schwartz in the final. Also of interest, this was Kai Budde's first masters-level Pro Tour. He drew into Day 2, where he finished 52nd at the age of sixteen.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/MI00/875.jpg"><br><i>A legend in his own time, Dave Price often qualifies for the Pro Tour through PTQs.</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

**Los Angeles** - Never has there been a more popular Pro Tour Champion then Dave Price. To this day, wherever he goes, if there are **Magic** players, there's high praise for the man who taught the world how to beat down. Playing monored in *Tempest* Block, Price captured the crown by defeating the youngest finalist in Pro Tour history, Ben Rubin. Price wouldn't find this kind of success again, but his record of longevity is matched only by Darwin Kastle as far as masters-level Pro Tours go.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/8c/79/8c790dcd10c74051fa2f0acabe8ab5ec8f131ad0fb459c58df3042fa8a656f1a.jpg"><br><i>It began here.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**New York** - Everyone knew it was coming, because it was apparent to those who saw him play that Jon Finkel was on a higher plane. Finkel made his first Top 8 in Chicago earlier in the year, but it took him three more tournaments to reach the champion's plateau. Limited has always been Jon's forte, and this tournament only added fuel to that argument's fire, with Finkel defeating rival David Bachmann, playing in his second consecutive semifinal, then Dominic Crapuchettes in the final of a tournament that featured three hometown players in the Top 4. Finkel would go on to finish third at World Championships and capture his only Player of the Year crown to date.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/21/aa/21aaa9403e982e5386af8f5efb888e40cf7d422c3aefbbc703a0d450eb9b2474.jpg"><br><i>Riding this eventually-banned card, Brian Selden cemented Ben Rubin in the second place seat for the next few years.</i>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

**World Championships** - In the same season in which Randy Buehler became the first rookie to win a Pro Tour since Tom Chanpheng, Californian Brian Selden took it a step further, winning World Championships in his first PT appearance. Playing Survival, Selden beat hard-luck kid Ben Rubin in the final of a power-packed Top 8 that included names like Finkel, Johns, Comer, Hacker, Pikula and Levy. Selden would go on to play in two more PT Sundays, showing the world his win was not the fluke it was thought to be at the time.
