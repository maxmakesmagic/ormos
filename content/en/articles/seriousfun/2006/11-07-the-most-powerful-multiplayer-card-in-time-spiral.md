A few weeks ago, I asked you folks what [The Best Multiplayer Card in *Time Spiral*](/en/articles/seriousfun/2006/10-10-the-best-multiplayer-card-in-time-spiral) was. And over two hundred of you responded with your picks for power, sending in impassioned essays by the score to fight for your right to get a free card!

The results were surprisingly varied, which is a good sign for *Time Spiral*. (If all of you had said, "Stuffy Doll!" then I would know that Wizards had printed either a *really* boring set or a ludicrously overpowered card.)

The problem is, of course, that I didn't define "Best."

![Promise_of_Power](https://i.ormos.uk/d4/25/d42546acfbcd7331565a97178c5dd08ef3bb095feadbaf5fb89183b219eef85b.jpg) See, I think of "The Best" as "The Most Powerful." But many of you thought of "The Best" card as "The Most *Fun*" card. Others, who apparently like shoving sticks into beehives to watch all the pretty yellowjackets set sail, defined "The Best Card" as "The Card That Would Cause The Most Stuff To Happen."

You know what? *All of you are right*.

That's the glory of casual, really...Because tournaments are a harsh and ugly mistress. All tournaments care about is *winning*. Did you come in first? Then it was a good tournament deck. Second place? That's a pretty good tournament deck.

Seventeenth place? "Don't talk to me," Mistress Tournament says, holding up one hand while she clasps her nostrils shut with the other. "You *disgust* my sense of being."

But if tournament is a cold hard business executive clutching a spreadsheet, then casual play is like having Randal from [Clerks](https://web.archive.org/web/20061107000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://www.imdb.com/title/tt0109445/&origin=mtgcom_daily_tf7) as your boss. "Who gives a crap about winning or losing?" he asks, a glint of mischief in his eye. "Did you have fun?"

![Goblin_Flectomancer](https://i.ormos.uk/9b/93/9b93c6079e08fbe1a9ca467d90891da8ea243bf23520f00295bf5c0a0fcb0e7b.jpg)You made a lot of cool plays and still lost? That's great! Did you fire off that once-in-a-lifetime combo? *Awesome*. As long as you're happy.

Thus, it occurred to me that I have to pick not one, but *two* winners for this crazy challenge: One for the most *powerful* card, and the other for the *most fun* card.

(Side note: My fourteen-year-old daughter has been trying to convince me for years that "Funnest" is really a word. I have told her since she was seven that in fact it is *not* a word. But I gotta say, aesthetically, "Funnest" beats the heck out of "Most Fun.")

Today, we'll pick the most powerful multiplayer card in *Time Spiral*. Next week? The funnest most fun card in *Time Spiral*.

**Ixidron (18 votes)  
 Status: Close, But No Cigar**  
 Ixy's a darned fine card.... But he's also very narrow. For Ixy to work, your local multiplayer table has to be creature-heavy - which, given that my multiplayer group is addicted to Wrath of God, may not be an option. And then you have to play him when there are enough creatures out that you won't immediately die to the onslaught when everyone attacks the guy who just turned their Akroma, Angel of Wrath into a flat piece of 2/2 brown cardboard. And *then* you have to have a morph deck that can abuse the flipped critters.

Is that good? Absolutely. But it means that Ixidron can only go in basically one type of deck, for one type of game. I'd like to think that "The Best *Time Spiral* Card In Multiplayer" would be usable in more than a single deck. Thus, I'm going to have to say "no."

**Kaervek the Merciless (16 Votes)  
 Status: In Contention**  
 Kaervek is really expensive at seven mana, but he has a real nice ability in a finisher; he hurts your competition something fierce. If they want to kill him, it's going to cost at least a mana (and most likely three or four, if you're playing with more recent cards), which means you're going to get some damage in.

Plus, he's nicely political. If some temporary allies know that you both want Player X out of the game, they can start casting big spells in the knowledge that you'll fling it all straight at your mutual enemy's head.

The only problem with Kaervek is that he's a) very expensive, and b) very vulnerable. Seven mana's still a lot to ask for in a multiplayer game, and with such a powerful effect you know everyone will be gunning for him. The question is, "How much damage can you do with him before everyone gangs up on him and sends him packing?"

**Deep-Sea Kraken (15 votes)  
 Status: Nah**  
 It's no wonder why people love the Kraken; he's huge, he's cheap, and he's unblockable! But unfortunately, the Kraken does not protect himself well. There are probably multiplayer groups where a turn-four Kraken is unstoppable, but in my group all it takes is two players with Terrors to overwhelm whatever Counterspell protection I may have.

The best creatures in multiplayer can protect themselves: Spiritmonger can regenerate for a lonely mana. Pristine Angel has native protection. Stuffy Doll is indestructible. I don't need to spend precious cards from my hand in order to ensure that they survive until the next turn; they do that pretty much all on their own.

But all Deep-Sea Kraken has is a premature arrival with big fat target on his back. And someone can take him down as easily as he arrived. (That said, if you have a beginners' group where people don't really get the concept of "be able to destroy large creatures with your deck," he could totally be the best card EVAR.)

**Stuffy Doll (15 votes)  
 Status: A Playa, To Be Sure**  
 The funny thing about the Stuffmeister is that people were initially afraid to vote for him simply because he was Anthony Alongi's final preview. Even the people who did vote for him seemed vaguely apologetic for being so trendy.

Folks, this isn't a popularity contest. (If it was, Ixidron would have taken it in a walk.) If you think Stuffy Doll is the best card, who *cares* who else agrees with you? All that matters is that Stuffy Doll is a great little card, and in fact it is.

For five mana, you can pretty much choose to destroy the non-black-packing player of your choice. Stuffy Doll dies to the slightest of -x/-x effects and is just as vulnerable to enchantments as any other creature...But aside from that, he destroys almost everyone else in style.

I had the good fortune to play with Stuffy Doll this last week, and lemme tell you...There's almost no more satisfying way to get past the "Wall of Gigantic Blockers" problem than to abuse the heck out of your Stuffy Doll. I thought Glarecaster was fun, but the guy who hides behind his army of creatures and never attacks *cannot* hide from the power of Stuffy Doll. Combine Stuffy Doll with Crater Hellion for maximum board-clearing fun!

I had so much fun with Stuffy Doll that my opponent had to point out that I was chucking Lightning Bolts and Sudden Shocks at my Stuffy Doll instead of at his equally-vulnerable head. So he's definitely powerful, and thus in contention.

(I should add that Guilty Conscience is an *awfully* fun way of amping a Stuffy Doll's power. And, as reader Joshua Powers pointed out, a Soul Foundry set to "Stuffy Doll" could be good times indeed.)

> *I choose whichever other player has the most creatures and send in Stuffy Doll, **inviting them to gang-block it**. My victim generally goes ballistic at this point."

**Curse of the Cabal (9 Votes)  
 Status: Does Not Play Well With Others**  
 Let me make a confession: I have not yet gotten the chance to play with the Curse. I own a couple, ready and raring to go - I just haven't had a chance to build a deck around it. Yet people I respect tell me that it's good.

The problem is that, though it creates political havoc when it hits the table, it's not that hard to stave off. Sacrificing one permanent for two turns - and a turn can be a *long* time in a large game - is a little too easy to accomplish.

Plus, it's usually only going to screw one player, and that's the guy immediately to your right (assuming you play clockwise). Unless they're really under the gun, everyone else will pass the buck to see whether Dude On Your Right will sacrifice anything - and if it's going to hurt him, he will. Also, this being multiplayer, the Land Where Tokens Run Free, there are going to be players who have tons of 1/1s ready to go at a moment's notice.

Now, when it hits it's going to decimate someone, no doubt. Hard-casting this is ugly, and if you play it in a mono-Black deck it's going to be hard to avoid sacrificing lands between the Mutilates and the Innocent Bloods and the Barter in Bloods. But in the end, the suspend shakes games up but probably will not *win* them.

I could be wrong. But I'm going to pass on this one.

**Lim-Dul the Necromancer (7 Votes)  
 Status: Not Consistent Enough**  
 He's impressive. And his stats are about right. But so many things have to come together all at once for him to pay off that you might be waiting forever.

To get him to steal a guy, you have to a) have a creature die when b) you have two mana open and Lim-Dul out. Considering that you'll probably pay almost all of your mana to bring him into play, Lim-Dul will most likely have to live a turn before he goes active. Now see Deep-Sea Kraken, and discuss among yourselves what happens when Lim-Dul cannot regenerate himself.

And even assuming that the Dul *does* live, you'll have to keep a boatload of mana open in the hopes that something dies, making you inherently more passive. Even if you kill it yourself, that means that you're going to get one creature, and you've invested a boatload of mana into Lim-Dul. Assuming you paid full price and didn't do any nifty Zombify-style tricks, that means that you've paid a minimum of nine mana to get *one* creature - which might not even stick if someone Sudden Deaths Lim-Dul before he can get to use his "resurrect a critter" business. And that doesn't even take into account the problem that once Lim-Dul hits the table, people will start to play *much* more conservatively to avoid handing you their best creatures.

What's going to happen most of the time is that Lim-Dul will get played, someone will kill or neuter him, and you'll have invested a boatload of mana for almost no gain. Sometimes, you'll win big with the D, snagging someone's entire board, which is great! But that's why he costs so much. In stock terms, he's a high-risk, high-gain investment.
