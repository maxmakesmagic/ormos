After a brief case study last week, we move back into our main series, bridging the gap between safety and card advantage by describing the tempo of the game. Tempo is easy to conceptualize. The person who uses the most mana over the course of the game will almost always win. Think about it. How does a game usually go? Why is going first so important? The reason is clear. It's not the fact that you get the first first turn, but the first fifth turn. This is not to be taken literally, but that the player who goes first gets the first Xth turn.

Also, think about the fact that the burden is on the player drawing to draw well. There is a lot of safety in going first, because if I play a 4 mana 3/3 on my turn, you better not play a 2/2 on your turn or things start to get ugly.

There is also the notion that going first you can overcome the card advantage lost by winning before they have the opportunity to play as many cards as they have drawn. When the game drags on to everyone having played all their spells is when going first's loss of cards can catch up with you.

Your amount of mana and the casting cost of your spells merge on one another as you approach the middle of the game. Often we will see a shift in tempo on the fifth turn of the game when one person lays a 2-drop and a 3-drop, while his opponent can only play one spell. Other major shifts are turn 6 in laying two 3 drops, or the less likely cases of 2 2-drops on turn 4 and a 3-drop and a 4-drop on turn 7. Also, a high quality 3-drop dominates having too many 2 drops in your deck. Zvi Mowshowitz once wrote about a similar concept he called the [Fundamental Turn](https://web.archive.org/web/20020730000000/http://www.wizards.com/leaving.asp?url=http://web.archive.org/web/20001204020400/thedojo.com/column2/col.991102zmo.shtml&origin=sideboard_strategy_20020730a).

In approaching Constructed and Limited **Magic**, we must choose the best cards and then win the battle of tempo. In Constructed **Magic** then, it follows that we must always choose the best cards and the best deck before we can even think about playing. In Limited **Magic**, we must make sure to use the best cards in our sealed deck and draft the best cards. This is because if we allow our opponents to just play better spells, we cannot even win a tempo battle, let alone the match. 

We will talk more about deck building and drafting later, but talking about these first four fundamentals will let you figure out most of that stuff for yourself.

It is advisable to combine your own thoughts about the game with the thoughts of other good players. Some people mock Gary Wise and Matt Vienneau as they give specific instructions about a given format. But these types of guides are a welcome addition to our repertoire because we use these articles as tools, and do not blindly follow them.

Using our reasoning about the game, we can analyze the advice they offer and not only understand the advice, but build upon it as well.

We can also use our analysis to figure out a given Constructed format. Take for example *Odyssey* Block Constructed. In terms of our framework, the major tempo shifting turns in the format are turn 3 in the form of Wild Mongrel and Circular Logic and turn 4 in terms of Roar of the Wurm. Our cards must be good enough to beat this card quality or we must be able to cast spells faster or we must join them and play with the fundamental tempo-breakers in order to succeed in *Odyssey* Block Constructed.

This is why certain decks succeed and others do not. The black decks can seize tempo back by casting multiple cheap removal spells in one turn. So when you are playtesting trying to break this format, think about the tempo game you must beat and save yourself a lot of time. If your deck isn't up to speed, you can more easily move onto something else, or onto tweaking the best-known deck.

We can also use our framework to guide our play. One of the toughest parts of **Magic** is the timing of spells, and the timing of spells is how we win the tempo battle.

For example, let's say you are playing a white deck in an Odyssey Block draft and you have the option of playing either turn 2 Mystic Familiar or a turn two Mystic Visionary. The right play depends on how you expect the game to play out. You must determine exactly how the tempo is going to shift.

Let's say you played first and play the Mystic Familiar. Now they match you with a Mad Dog and you play a Cephalid Looter. You are suddenly hosed because you don't want to take 2, you don't want to block with the Looter, and can't successfully block with the Familiar. So now you got sucked into a two for one damage race that you could have avoided. In this case, the Mystic Visionary route was in fact best. Consider instead if you played out the Visionary. Then on turn 3 you can merely play the Looter without attacking, and trade off the Visionary when he attacks.

On the other hand, if we expect our opponent to lay Phantom Whelp, then we would rather lead with the Mystic Familiar. We must always look forward several turns in making our decisions in games.

Here's another common decision that you can make by looking at the tempo framework. Let's say neither side plays a creature until turn 3. Each side plays a Gray Ogre, and one side attacks first. Now, the player is trying to decide if he wants to block and perhaps get Giant Growthed. He should decide to block, even if he thinks the opposition has Giant Growth, because Giant Growthing there will set his opponent back as much as it does him. Since Giant Growth would most likely stop his opponent from playing any other spells that turn, losing the creature is not as big a deal.

Because we are trying to wrest control of the tempo of the game from our opponent not just this turn but perhaps in many turns, we have to look several turns in advance. While you may not be able to predict exactly what will happen, you can get a good idea of how the game is going to go. You can look at who will draw more cards, who will be lower on life, and whose board position is gradually improving. 

By just thinking about these concepts, understanding sound play becomes easier. Just from thinking about how a game will likely play out, you can decide which two-drop to play.

Let's go back to the game at the end of the Card Advantage article. In it, our opponent has just attacked his Mystic Visionary into our Phantom Whelp, on the third turn. Now, this example is a bit simplistic, but we still reason the same way about this play as any other. We can't really race with Phantom Whelp because this would cost too much mana this early in the game. Even if we could keep recasting it, we are still going second in the damage race. So we think the Visionary is worth trading off with. We decide that even if our opponent has a trick, it's going to cost him at least 2 of his 3 mana, denying him the opportunity to lay a creature so we easily make a block. Now if he stutter-steps and doesn't play a 3-drop after his attack, perhaps we can grab hold of the tempo of the game.

### Quiz 1

![](https://i.ormos.uk/79/af/79af33945598e7b5de837df933519542b2a6983434bb98a4603653fcaea21ef2.jpg)

What two cards definitely should have been in the main deck, and what do they replace. What other card is a likely candidate and what should it replace? Take time to think through your answer and reason out the strategic considerations for these card swaps.

Swamp should have definitely replaced the Cabal Pit and Teroh's Faithful should have replaced a Mystic Visionary. It is also likely that a Floating Shield should have replaced the other Mystic Visionary. These choices are slightly better because:

The deck needs as much help as possible getting to a later (and hopefully Thresholded) game. The Cabal Pit hurts you before when you are strong and helps you when you should already be winning.  
 The Mystic Visionaries are merely the weakest two-drops in the deck and want to be replaced by cards that help the deck achieve the later game when it is stronger.

### Quiz 2

![](https://i.ormos.uk/30/85/308594cf08984c39806b071433fac48de701c3e62e204a5382509eec81c5d243.jpg)

In the above situation, I had the choice between killing the Seton's Scout on my turn, during my opponent's upkeep, or during my opponent's attack/main phase. What is the correct play and why?

In the situation presented, I ended up misplaying. The correct play is to kill the Scout on your own turn. This is reasoned out by examining possible scenarios.

First we reason is that our opponent does not have a removal spell for the Torturer to start since he would have killed it prior to the Torturer becoming active (unless his removal spell is Waste Away).

Scenario 1: Opponent has nothing. Neither decision is better.  
 Scenario 2: Opponent has Giant Growth. Killing the creature on your own turn puts the opponent to the decision when he doesn't know if he will draw a removal spell for the Torturer. Letting him look at his draw step may let him find a removal spell, thus making Giant Growthing right.

Basically, the only reason to wait is if you are assuming your opponent will misplay and Giant Growth for extra damage, which you know will not matter later. You can even get yourself into trouble here because it may end up being the right play to Giant Growth.

Now, one cannot make the argument that the opponent may Giant Growth the Scout when we try to kill it on our turn and then draw removal. This is merely a bad play he can make that will usually not work out.

In order to wait with the Torturer, we have to have a strong suspicion that our opponent will wrongly play the Giant Growth effect during his attack step. But, if we are assuming reasonably good play, we should immediately kill the creature.
