I love Boston. I’m not sure if it’s the old world architecture, the town’s rich history, the bridesmaid proximity to New York or the fact that my last time there, I came home with a fairly sizable check, but I’ve always liked the town. I love the accent, the Cheers mentality, the Red Sox ability to take the role of also-ran to uncharted heights… it’s just a great town and this weekend didn’t disappoint.

![](http://www.wizards.com/grandprix/images/NEW_GP.jpg)Usually, the goings on at the lesser-light environment of the Grand Prix aren’t enough to give me a rush. The simple fact is that even when Brian Kibler and Anton Jonsson won their respective GPs this weekend (good job boys. Kibler’s been drafting black-white for three months; it’s about time it paid off), the cash payout and pro point gains they made were barely more than the equivalent of a 32nd place finish on Tour and that means that even the big things that happen in those environments generally aren’t big things at all.

Not this weekend.

Boston came through for me again. Nick Eisel, the Pro Tour’s newest star, was disqualified for adding cards to his deck and, upon seeing my immediate reaction, Billy Jensen said I was not unlike “a hog at a buffet”. I’m friendly with most of the denizens of the Pro Tour, but the PT enthusiast and the columnist in me is always hungry for a good story. This is the biggest to hit in some time.

### That’s Game Boys

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gpphi02/911.jpg)Nick Eisel has been playing **Magic** for a long time. For eight years, he’s known the Randy Buehlers, Mike Turians and Eugene Harveys of the Pittsburgh **Magic** community. He rose through the ranks there over time, playing his first PT in London without much commotion and enjoyed a quiet existence within the international **Magic** community, up until the release of ***Magic** Online*. That’s when we all took notice.

Out of nowhere, playing under the nickname That’s Game Boys, Eisel climbed the ranks to the top of the charts, doing so in spectacular fashion, playing far fewer matches than his pursuers. Upon reaching the top spot, Nick retired his inaugural account, starting another, Soooooo, which he again carried to the heights, barely surpassing That’s Game Boys, again in relatively few matches, before retiring yet again.

Eisel’s cyber success coincided with the beginnings of a promising live game career as evidenced by his twin Top 8s at Grand Prix-Philadelphia and Grand Prix-Los Angeles, leading to his Pro Tour reemergence at Chicago where, despite a disappointing finish, he managed a 5-0 start, silencing many of his critics. The legitimacy of the ridiculously powerful sealed decks he had in those two Grand Prix, both of which he played to 7-0 finishes, is now being questioned by many of his Pro Tour peers.

Despite his disqualification, not everyone is convinced of his guilt. His staunchest defender is CMU teammate Mike Turian. “It’s just not in his character”, said Turian, who has known Eisel for eight years. “I’ve never known anyone who loves the game as much as he does. It doesn’t make sense that someone as smart as Nick would risk so much for a little success at a Grand Prix.” Turian’s optimism isn’t shared by some of his CMU teammates, though they will wait for the DCI’s final decision in the hopes that their comrade in arms will be proven innocent. Team members have voiced concerns that the differences in opinion could lead to unrest, but those questioned all seemed confident the team would survive this crisis.

That the news comes out of the CMU camp is surprising considering the team’s longstanding reputation for honest play. Team founder Randy Buehler had something of a reputation for being a rules lawyer, but always knew where the line of illegality stood and stayed on its more favorable side. 

Forums on numerous websites have exploded to an extent, with hearsay and rampant speculation sprinkled with occasional contributions from pro players attending the event apparently determining public opinion. The DCI, which has shown a pattern of careful vigilance in the past, is expected to make a more official ruling after an investigation has been concluded.

### The New School

One thing that’s struck me about the Eisel controversy was the reaction from a lot (not all) of established pros, that being a gleeful pump of the fist. Eisel’s rapid ascendance through the Pro Tour ranks has irked some, because of the sense that he hasn’t paid the dues required of a member of the pro community. He started out with an established pro team (CMU); he got recognition in Sideboard articles and Grand Prix Feature Matches before even playing in a successful Pro Tour; he practiced an unconventional strategy in his GP successes, hate drafting his peers, including teammate Ken Krouner in the Top 8 of GP-LA, despite not being able to face him until the finals and being the flashy, confident and sometimes cocky guy he is, has rubbed some the wrong way.

It’s been a while since I’ve taken a look at the up and comers of the Pro Tour in these pages, but there are some very good players out there still searching for that breakthrough performance, whose games deserve some recognition despite their failure to find it thus far. This is the next wave of Pro Tour regulars: My guess is you’ll be hearing some of their names on regular occasion over the next few years:

Louis Boileau – Might as well start with a countryman. Louis has been around for a couple of years now and while his PT credentials haven’t exploded yet, his finishes in other tournaments suggest a bright future. Making a final at GP-Montreal and Top 8 at GP-Detroit amid tough North American fields, Boileau has also managed high finish in consecutive National Championships. A longtime friend of GP-Boston finalist David Rood, Boileau is a part of the up and coming core of eastern Canadians that has started to flourish this year, including Rood, Elijah Pollock, Jurgen Hahn, Richard Hoean, Steve Wolfman, Steve Cassels and Mark Zaijdner. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gpcop02/a947.jpg)David Brucker – Something of a standout on this list, Brucker is the reigning European Champion and a former member of the German National Team, but he frighteningly has only recently started taking **Magic** seriously, with Houston was his first event outside European soil. Working consistently with longtime teammate Christoff Lippert, David has had the benefit of working with some of Germany’s better known stars as he’s started tasting success. Kai Budde himself insists his Euros success was no fluke, so expect more of the same.

Joe Crosby – If you’ve read Joe’s articles on *Sideboard* of late, you can understand why Ken Krouner has so much respect for his understanding of some of the game’s more advanced theoretical areas. Crosby’s been around for a long time, getting his education in Georgia with such professors as Rudy Edwards, Aziz Al-Doory and Andy Wolf. Crosby’s life has undergone noticeable changes of late that include a more serious approach towards the game, which proved itself important with a strong finish in Chicago. Despite the narrow miss on Top 8, Crosby’s performance there bodes well for things to come.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gpphi02/860.jpg)Morgan Douglass – I played Douglass at GP-DC years ago when he was a freckle-faced kid and he was bad. I remember the frustration I felt as my completely awful deck was only barely able to manage a draw on the match, but when I found out it was him recently, I felt a little better. Douglass, a peer of the Huey, Brock and Linde group, has finally started reaping the rewards of his hard work of late, appearing in the same two GP Top 8’s as Eisel, amidst far less fanfare. Not too surprisingly, Douglass is more a Limited player than Constructed, but his recent successes with forty-card decks suggest the potential for a lot more exposure in the near future. 

Sam Gomersall – I played a lot with Sam during my year in England and the leaps he made in that time were astounding. Gomersall, the creator of the original Wake deck seen at GP-London is in a fairly unique situation, living in a house with three other gamers while independently wealthy enough to dedicate himself to the craft full time. Sam’s had the benefit of working with Constructed masterminds John Ormerod and Ben Ronaldson from time to time and it’s started paying off with a remarkable streak of second day appearances at Grand Prix in both North America and Europe. Look for a Constructed breakthrough, perhaps in Venice, where aside from Ronaldson, England will be sending a full contingent in what has historically been their strongest format. 

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gplon02/898.jpg)Dario Minieri – When I finished 9th at GP-London, one great disappointment was that it meant I’d never get to play Dario in OBC. The reason I wanted to play him so specifically is because there was not a player in the world who posted better results with blue-green than the youthful looking Italian, who finished in the Top 8 there to cap off a season in which he went 6-0 at Worlds. Minieri’s education has come at the hands of Pro Tour regulars Antonino De Rosa, Andrea Santin and Raffaele Lo Moro among others, but he surpassed them all at PT-Houston where a final round loss to Darwin Kastle deprived him of his first Top 8 finish. 

Ben Stark – The inspiration for this list, most every player this side of the Atlantic is waiting for Ben’s day in the sun. A veteran of the drafting circuit, Stark fears no opponent and can hold his own against all comers in the Limited arena. So why, do you ask, hasn’t he proven his mettle in the Pro Tour ring? Simply put, it’s a case of nerves, which he’s slowly but surely overcoming as evidenced by his recent successes, with the Floridian receiving checks for both his Houston and Chicago finishes, not to mention qualifying for the Masters by way of the Gateway. Ben is working with CMU for Venice and has ties to Bob Maher and Neil Reeves also, associations that bode well.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/worlds02/a914.jpg)Cole Swannack – Like his American counterpart, The Baby Faced Assassin, Zach Parker, I’m not predicting Top 8 glory for Cole in the coming months, but instead, the coming years. These kids, at the tender age of fourteen, have proven with multiple qualifications and limited success that they can stand with the big boys. Both have positioned themselves well among their peers, with advice from more experienced players laying the groundwork for their respective futures. Cole’s 17th place finish at World Championships made headlines, but more impressive than the placing is the long list of seasoned pros he beat. His detractors suggest he made the simplest deck choices, but if that’s true, it shows a maturity inherent in the ability to not try to outdo one’s self.

If there’s a lesson to be learned from these players, it’s that it is important to ally yourself with players who are more experienced than you are. Their lessons will be your lessons and, hopefully, their recipes for success will rub off. Team CMU has always above all else loved the game, playing casual and competitive alike, an example Nick Eisel followed. If however, Nick is suspended, I’d suggest it may be in part because he ignored the example of those who came before him. Tough way to learn a lesson.

Have a good week.
