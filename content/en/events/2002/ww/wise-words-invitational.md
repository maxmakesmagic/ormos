### 

It's funny. Everyone I've talked to since returning home to Toronto and returning to **Magic** Online has suggested I should be disappointed. 'Good try at the Invitational', 'sorry about the Invitational' and similar phrases seem to have become the status quo when people have started conversations with me. Am I the only one who feels like my final record, be it 9-6 or 2-13, doesn't matter one iota?

Sure I'm a gamer, but I'm a gamer for the competition, the battle of wills, and yes, the money the Pro Tour offers, and as a result, the creation of a card just isn't that big a deal for me. In the past, I've said that my chances of winning an Invitational were awful because I've always been slow to adjust to a format and the Invitational is full of new ones, but this year showed me that with a few different bounces, that theory flies out the window and it still doesn't matter: even with my missing the final, I'm ecstatic with this year's event.

Going into the weekend, there were a lot of unanswered questions: What's the Invitational without Kai? Isn't Seattle a little boring? Won't playing on **Magic** Online suck? Well, without Kai, you still have sixteen of the game's stars, and for an exhibition tournament, it doesn't matter if one great player doesn't turn up. Kai's my buddy and I love hanging out with him, but I can honestly say that this year's crop of Invitationalists was easily the best group of people I've shared the event with. Seattle was not only not boring, but also a lot of fun, giving those of us who'd never been to WotC before a chance to see the works, from R&D's barricaded little playpen to Mark Rosewater's wonderful home. The gaps in my mind with regards to the corporate process that is **Magic** have been filled, at least in part, thanks to the trip. As for **Magic** Online? Read on…

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="https://i.ormos.uk/38/1b/381b73ad207922372b69ba27a61ea286a3934cd8d159e20c014aec026805566c.jpg"><br><i>Draw five cards?</i></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

I didn't get to play in the first three Invitationals, but of the four I've attended, Seattle will go down as the most important, most lasting and most fun of the bunch, and every veteran Invitationalist in attendance seemed to agree with those sentiments. Yes, games were decided on misclicks, while others were definitely affected by them (No, my splitting Itaru Ishida's Fact of Fiction 5-0 was not a strategic decision), and in the end, even the second seed in the final was decided by Eivind Nitter's lack of experience with the interface. Winning isn't the only thing though.

What made this event so great was the audience. In the past, the Invitational has been played in front of a handful of stragglers on the first couple of days before a more public arena allowed for more of a crowd, but even then, the crowd would only linger so long as a Grand Prix round hadn't started. To fly thousands of miles and endure a schedule of activities you don't necessarily want to carry out to play in front of a dozen people is a less than rewarding experience. So much so that I considered not attending this year's event: a free trip just isn't that big a deal to someone who travels twenty times a year.

This time, it was different. Being located in the WotC corporate offices in Renton, the Invitational couldn't be watched live by non-WotC types, but the entire Wizards contingent was in attendance, which for those of us who've been around the game for a long time is a big bonus. You all know Randy and Rosewater and Donais, but getting to see people like Renee Roub and Skaff Elias and Andy Heckt in this more-personal-than-a-Pro-Tour environment is a nice change. Of course, that was nothing compared to the reception we got online.

When we got on **Magic** Online, I think we were all pretty much overwhelmed by the reception we got. Hundreds of voices, cheering for favorites and razzing their personal villains, interviews and less organized Q and A periods…as a whole there was a very good feel to the online atmosphere. See, that's when the upside of the Online invitational became apparent: instead of a few match write ups (not that there's anything wrong with that), for the first time ever, every game in a premiere-level tournament was recorded, to stay in the **Magic** Online archives for as long as they exist. As our first round games ended and eventually made their way to the replay format, people started watching… and questioning… and opining… and complaining. Suddenly, the ramifications were abundantly clear: every mistake would be studied, and mocked, forever.

I think for the Invitationalists, the importance of this fact varied. I know Tomi Walamies was a little frustrated at the collected voices questioning his play during the Finals. The fact they could see our hands added a lot of pressure, at least for the other guys. I think I was the exception.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/mi02/996.jpg)

This goes back to the whole "not caring about winning" thing. I'm pretty sure my average time between actions was less than two seconds and I definitely wasn't being careful about avoiding misclicks, and here's why: more or less, Pro Tour regulars all know one another and despite the competition between us, with certain exceptions (hey Ryan!) we more or less provide a peer group for one another. The problem is that with the bunch of us fighting over so much money and only meeting up when we do, there aren't many opportunities to just hang out. Enter the Invitational.

Seattle was my one chance this year to just hang out with these guys. None of the other fifteen are presently teammates of mine, and that means I'm always hiding something from them at PTs, as I expect they are from me. The moment I start getting competitive, that oasis in the Pro Tour desert would be gone. For a card, it just didn't seem worth it. I guess that's why I didn't mind when Jon Finkel crushed me in the last round.

I guess what I'm saying is that for those of us lucky enough to go, while there are definitely some for whom winning is important, to a man, it isn't as important as the spirit of the event. That means minimal playtesting, which in turn, combined with formats not usually played, means that mistakes will be made. For those of you who went online expecting to see Pro Tour miracles performed every day, I'm sorry if what you found was disappointing, but I do ask for a little forgiveness as you watch those replays. For that one weekend, we were just sixteen gamers, getting together, and having a lot of fun.

### Thoughts on the Invitational Rochester Draft

It doesn't matter what packs you use, the rules for successful Rochester are the same. Rochester draft was the one format I managed to 3-0 despite my having done less *Onslaught* Limited than most of my competitors, and that's solely because of the fact I saw a pattern in the first pack of the draft. Sitting in seat seven behind Alex Shvartsman and in front of Jon Finkel, the first pack was opened and looked like this:

Brightstone Ritual  
Sea's Claim  
Elvish Pioneer  
Syphon Soul  
Battering Craghorn  
Sage Aven  
Gustcloak Harrier  
Forgotten Cave  
Taunting Elf  
Fallen Cleric  
Battlefield Medic  
Crowd Favorites  
Custody Battle  
Ghosthelm Courier  
Slate of Ancestry

![](http://www.wizards.com/global/images/magic/general/Slate_of_Ancestry.jpg)

Pretty weak. Chris Pikula, sitting in seat one, picked Gustcloak Harrier, which I'm convinced was a big mistake. The big complaint that most players have with sitting in seat one, considered by most to be the weakest of the eight, is that your initial picks may end up being wasted if the player in seat eight goes into your color, forcing you to switch to another one. My experiences with Slate of Ancestry have been mixed, but I'm not convinced it's so far inferior to the Harrier that it's worth making a statement with a double-colored-casting cost spell that, while good, is far from exciting. In Chris's situation, I definitely would have taken the Slate, which would give me a card that would definitely find a place in my deck regardless of my colors, and then try to draft one of the faster tribes pretty hard, preferably Elves, Soldiers or Goblins. Instead, Chris used another pick on Doubtless One while fighting Carlos Romao on his left for black before finally taking a Krosan Tusker in the fourth pack and leaving white behind.

Back in the first pack, I had no plan to go into blue, but the six picks in front of me convinced me it was the right plan of action. Looking at the pack, Ghosthelm Courier was one of the best non-white cards in there, but Pikula, Romao, Thoren, Ruel, Davis and Shvartsman all passed on it. Being a pro table with the conventional wisdom that blue is by far the weakest color in the format, it seemed possible that no one, aside from possibly Olivier (who'd drafted blue a lot through the weekend) and Jon might want blue, and Jon wasn't about to fight for it from the left. I grabbed the Courier 7th with Finkel leaving the only other mildly playable blue card, Sage Aven for me to pick up tenth.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/e6/3f/e63f8023acdf6827c5a4ede3f01c06c9bbdbe7ac5af054d08bf5c860040693df.jpg)

The next two packs not only backed up my theory, but probably were most responsible for the success of my draft. The second had only two playable blue cards, Riptide Biologist and Disruptive Pitmage, but while both are playable, neither is a card that would make someone go into blue. I got both cards and then in the third pack, there were no playable blue cards to speak of, meaning an eighth of the way through the draft, I was the only blue player, while the other drafters had established themselves in other colors. Eventually, Olivier, who started black-white, ditched the black for blue, splitting the table between us. Here's how things ended up:

Chris - green-black  
 Carlos - black-red  
 Jens - green-red  
 Olivier - blue-white  
 Brian - black-green  
 Alex - green-white  
 Gary - blue-black  
 Jon - white-red

![](https://i.ormos.uk/92/79/9279b221a3f1b483796b2aa1f25032a33b14bd120c6df28d5099e489b30ba1db.jpg)

I've always held to the theory that your draft's strength relies as much as anything else on your position for one simple reason: you want first crack at your colors as often as possible. Jon and I each went 3-0 and if you count the number of first pick possibilities that had been established by the second pack on both counts, it seems to back that theory. Insanely, Jon had four seats between himself and the closest red player in front of him, despite red being considered by most to be the strongest color in the format, while Chris's switch out of white, left Jon three seats in front of the closest player behind him. Davis's late switch into black hurt my plans a bit, but that still left me with only one other blue player on the table (my deck ended up very heavily blue as a result) and no black player on either side of me, as Jon and Alex split white, red and green between them. For the first few packs, and this is especially true if you're sitting late in the draft, don't worry about card power so much as your draft position. It may not get you the 5th pick Future Sights and 5th pick Complicates I was fortunate enough to get, but it will increase your chance of that happening a lot.

I think that's enough patting myself on the back for co-winning one draft. Before ending things this week, I want to thank everyone who voted me into the Invitational, those who supported me throughout the weekend and the players who showed up on gunslinging day, and I apologize for not having the opportunity to play all of you. The event was a lot of fun and while I wasn't looking forward to it, in retrospect, I'm really glad I went. You guys are great.

Have a good week.
