For those readers who missed the first installment of this series, please read it [here](/en/articles/event-coverage/2002/07-02-concepts-of-magic-introduction). Before we move on to the meat of the article, I would like to respond to a couple of posts that I have seen on the Wizards [boards](https://web.archive.org/web/20020709000000/http://boards.wizards.com/tcg-bin/ultimatebb.cgi?ubb=get_topic;f=55;t=000148):

**Good article so far, but I do disagree with some of the author's opinions. Mainly the statement, "when you lose it's your fault". I do believe that there are some luck factors that may contribute.**

**But in individual games, luck is a strong factor, and luck will remain a strong factor in Magic until the concept of drawing cards from a library is abolished.**

**And, of course, this doesn't take into account the weird stuff like coin flipping, which is also a part of the game (if a minor part, but still...)**

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>Trying to analyze your own play while dismissing some losses as luck can seriously hinder anyone's progress as a player.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

While there is no denying there is some random element in Magic, since we are after all drawing cards from a deck, the role this element plays in deciding someone's success in Magic is negligible. There is no one who is affected more or less by the random element than any other, so when analyzing the game, we can safely ignore the factor. It is more interesting just to note the probabilities involved in plays and decisions. If the right play wins 75% of the time, it will win 75% of the time for everyone. If you believe you are a lucky or unlucky person, then either quickly drop that point of view or stop reading. None of what I say will make any sense.

I further hypothesize that trying to analyze your own play while dismissing some losses as luck can seriously hinder anyone's progress as a player. The fact is, "luck" is seriously misunderstood. Many people say, "he top-decked against me and won". People even say in [Kai Budde's record-breaking match against Tomi Walamies in New Orleans](about:blank), he got lucky and top-decked a Morphling. This is a misnomer however. There is a very small group of people in the world who would have won that match from Kai's side. Kai's play up until that point in the match maximized his card drawing allowing himself the most chances to draw Morphling. We'll cover this example in more depth later in the article.

**I think that it was a great article, but what does this mean:**  
**"This is all crap right? Jon Finkel loses to Gary Wendelhaven of Iowa sometimes"**

Sorry, all of you from Iowa. I just chose a place that was not generally considered a strong Magic center. My own native Texas would do just as nicely. Gary Wendelhaven is, of course, imaginary.

**I have been playing ever since Invasion and have had my share of losses. Every time I have suffered a loss, due to a mistake, I have never made that mistake again, or, learned something that I should, or should not, have done. I have earned a respect from more experienced players, that i don't make mistakes. The primary goal in playing ANY type of game, besides not thinking about losing, is not making mistakes.**

There are a couple of issues with this post. First, every time one makes a mistake, it is impossible never to make the mistake again, or even to learn something from mistakes every time. It is a good goal as a student of the game to learn and to not repeat mistakes, but to do so every time implies superhuman discipline. And everyone makes mistakes. In fact, everyone makes mistakes in almost every single match. If anyone were to claim that they had played a perfect match, I would be hard-pressed to believe it. If someone were to say they played a perfect tournament, I would dismiss that as a crazy and/or arrogant rant.

**I don't consider myself to be an expert by any stretch of the imagination, but I do believe in the bad/good shuffle.**

The point is, why should we even think about a bad shuffle when it cannot be controlled. Even more poignant is *most bad shuffles are in fact a poor choice not to Mulligan.* 

**Jon Finkel vs. Kai Budde.**  
**Both players are known as the best players ever. Then let's say they play an identical deck.**  
**And now who will win?**  
**The player who draws better – don't you agree?**

It is interesting that you should bring up matches between world-class players, especially Jon Finkel. Those of us familiar with famous matches can look at many and point to mistakes that lost the match, not luck, especially since the matches are usually 5 games. Three examples of such are: Jon Finkel vs. Bob Maher Junior, in a [Tinker mirror match](https://web.archive.org/web/20020709000000/http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/worlds2000/article.asp?xf=651finals&top=10&index=xmlBin/index_coverage.xml). The match was decided by Bob Maher's misplay of a Crumbling Sanctuary. [Bob Maher vs. Brian Davis: Finals Chicago 1999](https://web.archive.org/web/20020709000000/http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/article.asp?x=PTCHICAGO9900/finalsrecap). Brian Davis could have won all 5 games, yet Bob Maher won the match. [Myself vs. Kai Budde: Draft 2, Worlds 2001](about:blank). I could have won both games yet I played too aggressively in one and not aggressively enough in another. I use my own match in this case, because it is often hard to see when a match is lost in a subtle way unless you were actually involved. In all of these cases, the superior player won the match, whether by a minor error, sheer intimidation, or just better overall play skill.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/worlds2000/images/472.jpg)Now, in response to the debate about being on one life, I will try and crystallize the argument that life total is worth something. Let's take a situation in which a permission player is playing against a burn player. If the permission player is on five life and holds 2 Counterspells, and the burn player holds 4 cards, and the burn player plays Fireblast for its mana cost at the end of the permission players turn, the permission player should probably counter the Fireblast, All other things being equal. The reasoning here is that by allowing the spell to resolve, you make too many of their other spells too good, such as Shock, or Seal of Fire, or Mogg Fanatic. This is a good lead into card advantage. Remember our outline:

Consider the following outlook on our burn vs. control situation. Our opponent has just offered us card advantage. We can trade in four life and all of a sudden we are up a card. In fact, we should choose safety at this juncture because we can trade this advantage in later at a premium. We can trade this four life in for 2 Shocks later, but not now, because we are in jeopardy. However, were we on 17 life with the offer to go to 13, we will of course choose card advantage.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/10/61/1061310db264f2e91d0de77619e0304b04bbf7e506ed79eba2f5369e477a9c1d.jpg)Card advantage can be split into three distinct categories: getting your money's worth, pure card economy, and card selection. Examples of each might include: Jackal Pup, Battle of Wits, Repulse, or Winter Orb for getting our money's worth, card economy such as Hymn to Tourach or Concentrate, and card selection such as Think Tank,. Sometimes we trade one for another, but it is very difficult to define how many activations of a Merfolk Looter are worth drawing a card.

Without quantitatively analyzing trade-offs between the three forms of card advantage, it is helpful to notice that all three do tend to net you cards, in the long run. In fact, all winning strategies in deck-building and playing hinge on the card advantage the strategies generate. Here are three clear examples of card advantage at work:

The most straightforward example is the general game plan of a classic draw-go deck. The deck is packed with counterspells to trade one-for-one with the enemy threats. The deck also uses a minimum number of threats, such as 2 Rainbow Efreets, to minimize slots for countermagic. The deck ran on Whispers of the Muse, which would generate card advantage limited only by your (copious) mana supply. The deck had a strategy of pure attrition.

The second example of card advantage is your average red Sligh deck. This deck could be said to win purely through card advantage. This is not to say other descriptions of how the deck wins are invalid, but a card advantage framework will let us analyze any game of Magic, so it is useful.

A Sligh deck tries to use all of its cards very quickly. A draw-go deck will win when you have no cards in hand with a single creatures, while sitting on a couple of cards. A Sligh deck will win while it's opponent sits with several cards in hand, in many cases. The deck does this by denying an opponent the necessary turns to get mana out for high casting cost spells, and on top of this, it denies opponents draw phases in general. That is, it wins the game, immediately. No more life means no more turns means no more cards. Sligh, in effect, gets its money's worth from its cards, and this form of card advantage is turned into pure card economy. A card that you cannot use might as well as never been drawn in the first place. If Sligh's opponent strategy hinges on giving up safety early in hopes of a dominant late game, then Sligh can exploit this for a fast win. It doesn't take too many Necropotence activations or painland uses, or even the ignoring of creatures before the situation is critical.

Mike Flores's monoblack deck that Jon Finkel piloted to a victory in U.S. Nationals 1999 epitomizes the theory of card selection. The deck had specialized answers to deal with many situations. Once again, this can be viewed as an eventual conversion into pure card advantage. Because cards are not all equivalent, the deck hoped to negate several cards with one card. In one case, it would fetch a large creature such as a Phyrexian Negator because some decks were unequipped to deal with such a beast. In an extreme case, it could fetch a Stromgald Cabal, which effectively ended the game against the Replenish combo decks.

Of course, what is little known about the Napster deck was that the deck depended more on its pure card advantage engine (Yawgmoth's Will) than its card selection mechanism. In fact, in Constructed, decks will usually not depend on just card selection. This situation tends to arise because cards suitable for Constructed play tend to be homogenously powerful, and cheap card advantage is the norm rather than the exception. The Maher-Oath deck used a Sylvan-Abundance engine to draw good cards. Mike Turian's Squirrel-Prison deck used Deep Analysis along with its Looters. The Napster deck had Yawgmoth's Will and Persecute.

The situation changes in Limited, where the cards aren't so homogenously powerful. The power level differences between cards make Cephalid Looter as powerful as Archivist, since your first picks can often dominate your opponent's other cards.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/6e/1d/6e1d19348ebe40ecd5fa7e14437698768b0e57c9e38090136aaf5bb5cccef5b0.jpg)In essence, this means that we can formulate a plan for winning, in deck building and in playing, by generating card advantage. If we can generate card advantage, we have won. Now, casting Deep Analysis and then getting killed the following turn does not generate card advantage. The cards are still in our hand, completely unused. We have to pay attention to our safety while trying to find a winning plan. We can also try an achieve card advantage through our less direct methods than just card drawing. Perhaps we can get out money's worth from Winter Orb, and slow our opponent down, and eventually realize real card advantage when our opponent can lo longer cast all the spells they draw.

Let us place ourselves in a hypothetical Limited match. We open our hand and we should immediately draw up our plan for winning. Let us make it very easy and say we are playing OD/OD/TOR draft and we are blue-black vs. a blue-white deck. We should win if we execute our play correctly. We opt to draw first since the match will likely go for many turns and we will have no trouble casting our spells. This is dangerous though, since if our opponent gets a fast draw, he may achieve ultimate card advantage and kill us with loads of great anti-control cards in our hand.

![A keeper? Read on.](https://i.ormos.uk/78/7d/787d0fd60452060bd5e84866df15ad25102d78c3b47bc5920c12b35de94cb158.jpg)Our opening hand contains swamp, swamp, Filthy Cur, Skywing Aven, Skeletal Scrying, Dreamwinder, Aven Windreader. With this hand, our plan is all of a sudden conditional beatdown. We are going to try and play out a fast curve of creatures and win before our opponent gets going full tilt. Unfortunately, the draw has problems. If we don't hit running land, one of which must be island, our plan dissolves. We are now on the defensive with a lack of resources. We may even have to sacrifice cards just to stay alive. On top of that, we are trying full tilt offense against a deck that specializes in defense, and we are drawing first. I would council throwing this hand back in most cases, given the variables as they are.

  

![Much Better](https://i.ormos.uk/bb/f6/bbf6ded91f40547c0bf713c19f8cff51409de5adcf41a3ffb501fe820b74493e.jpg)Our next hand is more satisfying and contains island, swamp, swamp, Skywing Aven, Patriarch's Desire, and Cephalid Looter. Now are plan for winning is elegant and much harder to beat. Our draw is playing on the strength of our utility creature to achieve a long game in which we have a permanent advantage in card selection. We are playing off the weakness of the blue-white deck rather than playing into its strengths, and we now definitely have good mana. The card we traded for this new hand was well worth it. We hope to Loot our deck looking for our high picks. Now, until we have found our true card advantage, we know we want to avoid situations in which are opponent can beat our good cards with his lesser cards. We don't want to get involved in intricate combat situations where Embolden will trump our superior creatures. We don't want to get into a pure race where the great cards we have Looted go unused. We prefer to have combat when our opponent is tapped out and we prefer to conserve our life total, unless we are getting a good deal. We do not want to trade 2 damage for 2, but we would probably like to trade 2 damage for 4 damage.

Once we have achieved a dominant board position, or achieved some kind of card advantage, such as through Skeletal Scrying, then we can end the game. We are more willing to match our spells versus our opponent's, but until that time, we really just want safety and as little interaction as possible. We want to maximize the time spent in the low mana/low power early game while we have a good advantage. Then, when we approach a point where our opponent might start to catch up, we try and end the game.

Here's a small example from our draw. Our opponent leads with an island, and we draw another island and play one. Our opponent plays a plains and a Mystic Visionary. This is a little troublesome but not particularly dangerous yet. Luckily, we rip a Phantom Whelp, and play it and a swamp out (we have a Cabal Torturer in our deck so we shouldn't feign mana screw here). Our opponent swings in with the Visionary, and we happily trade for his creature. We don't really want to get into big combat situations yet, so we trade off. Plus, if our opponent has a trick, he will have to waste his whole turn killing our Phantom Whelp. We need to keep the tempo...
