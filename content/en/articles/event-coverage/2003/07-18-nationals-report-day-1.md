Seven years ago, I was a freshman at Phillips Academy in Andover, Massachusetts. Since Phillips is a boarding school, I spent all of my time during the school year on campus, more or less. School policy didn't allow students to hold jobs while classes were in session, so the first time I ever really worked was the summer between freshman and sophomore years.

A family friend was a supervisor of the catering division at a local amusement park called Canobie Lark Park, so my mother – insisting that I find some way of making some extra money over the summer – got me a job working there. In total, I worked two days at Canobie Lake, one manning a concession stand in the park itself and another catering a business function, and refused to go back. I hated it. I hated standing around wearing an awkwardly fitting hat and brightly colored polo shirt waiting for people to come by and demand I serve them. I hated people telling me to do things in absurdly inefficient ways and not even bothering to explain why. More than anything, I think, I just hated doing something that no one noticed unless it didn't get done. When I started winning local tournaments for a hundred dollars here and there, as well as selling the cards I won in prizes at smaller events, my mother agreed that I could get away without having a "real job" for the time being.

When I was in middle school, I performed in a musical called "Krazy Kamp" – one of those made-for-student-production plays which are blatantly awful and yet amusing because of it. My character was Willard, the assistant director of a summer camp for boys. I had a duet with the other male lead in the play called "Real Job" with a chorus that went like this; "I don't want no real job, nuh uh! Ain't got no time to work! I'm a little bit crazy, whole lot lazy, guess I've got this quirk! But it don't matter what they say, I'm gonna do what I want anyway! This boy will never hold a real job!" Perhaps it was a sort of prophecy. 

The Wednesday before this year's U.S. Nationals was my final day of college. Due to my hectic travel schedule over the past few years, I hadn't fulfilled all of my requirements by the time graduation came around – not to mention the fact that I was on a plane coming back from Yokohama during the actual ceremony. I took a session of summer classes and, at last, I am done. Once I get the paperwork completed, I will officially be a graduate of Emory University with a BA in Philosophy and Religion. So I decided to do what any sensible person with a degree in such a high-demand field would do – play Magic.

So as of now, I'm a full time gamer – and writer, of course. Expect to see a lot more from me these days now that I'm not trying to tackle papers in Existentialism and Ethics and Conceptions of Human Nature at the same time as articles on the Standard metagame. I've many times in the past lamented the lack of tournament reports from high level events, and I intend on rectifying this problem through leading by example – I plan on writing a report for every Pro Tour and Grand Prix event I compete in, regardless of how well or how abysmally I may finish. Hopefully I'll be doing more of the former than the latter from now on, though – and Nationals seems to have been a fine start to that plan. 

Ah, Nationals. The reason I subjected you to all that drivel above in the first place. I've always liked the tournament, back from the first time I ever attended in 1996. Back then Nationals was at Origins, a situation about which many "old timers" wax nostalgically. Nationals at Origins was a gamer's dream. Huge halls packed with people playing and trading into the wee hours of the morning. The games often spilled over into the hallways, and it was not uncommon to see dozens upon dozens of players hunched on the floor together outside the main tournament rooms playing for ante.

My first Nationals experience was 1996. My brother qualified at Regionals that year with a deck I'd built for him – apparently the phenomenon of other people doing well with my decks had its roots early on. I was not qualified, but played in the grinders with a g-w-r Land Tax/Stormbind deck only to fall just short of making it. In 1997, I missed qualifying at Regionals despite playing the original 5CG, which Matt Place, Nate Clarke, and myself designed. I went anyway and won the very last grinder, drafted after napping for an hour on the floor outside the tournament area, and then lost a heartbreaker in the final round playing for Top 8 against Jeff Butz – to this day I constant refer to him as my "arch-nemesis".

In 1998 and 1999, I wasn't really playing **Magic** seriously because of wrestling, so in ‘98 I went just to hang out with friends and in ‘99 I skipped the tournament entirely. Nationals in 2000 saw me qualified on rating, since I'd just started to play again, but I forwent playing to ensure my qualification for Worlds. There were no more ratings scares after that, however, as fall of 2000 saw my Top 4 in Chicago and I've been on the gravy train ever since. In 2001, I had an abysmal tournament, and 2002 I missed the Top 8 on tiebreakers, finishing 12th.

With two near misses already coming into this year, then, I really wanted to take that next step and make the team. While playing on the U.S. Team might just mean extra dollar signs to some players, to me it seems really exciting. I've joked around about buying an entire wardrobe of G.I. Joe and Captain America shirts to wear during Worlds if I ever make the team, and the thing is I'm only half joking. I'm no more a flag waving patriot than the next guy, but I just think the idea of playing in Worlds with the entire **Magic** playing population of the U.S. rooting me on is exhilarating – and it certainly wouldn't hurt to get my name engraved on that cool team champion trophy.

Despite how excited I was for Nationals this year and how much I wanted to win, however, I had a difficult time preparing for the tournament. I've been testing with Your Move Games since the Yokohama Masters Series, and what testing I've been able to do with them has been quite productive, but between my hectic summer session class schedule and a general schism between the schedules of most members of the team and my own – Zvi tends to be awake during "daylight", which is something I can't quite seem to pull off – I had a hard time playtesting as much as I wanted to.

This point is probably worthy of an aside. When it comes to me and playtesting, "as much I wanted to" is a rather dangerous phrase. I can be rather obsessive when I'm focused on something. As many of my new playtest partners find when they first start gaming with me, I am a virtual machine. Some people play ten game sets – I'd play fifty or a hundred if I could find someone willing to play against me. I hate hearing people say one deck beats another and then tell me they played six games and it was 4-2. One day during PT-Venice testing, I played against Eric Froelich (EFro) and then Ben Stark from around noon until five in the morning, stopping pretty much only to eat and use the bathroom. Each of them eventually had to give up and do something else. I am the strongest link.

I suppose, then, that I can't fault the members of YMG for not hooking themselves up to IVs and having their eyelids held open so they could play against me for seventy-two hours straight. I did manage to borrow the cards on ***Magic** Online* for the deck I thought seemed the best – zombies – from Dave Humphreys, so I managed to get a good deal of practice in despite everything. It's always amusing to play on ***Magic** Online* under one of my "stealth" accounts – I find people are much more likely to whine about how lucky I'm clearly getting to beat them when they don't know who I am. In any case, between ***Magic** Online* tournaments, IRC and AIM discussions, I managed to work out enough of my deck that I was confident in my decision heading to Nationals.

I arrived in San Diego in the early evening on Wednesday and, after a few brief phone calls to find out who was in town and where, headed for my hotel. I spent most of the evening discussing *Scourge* draft with EFro in his hotel room, where he'd spent the day playing in the ***Magic** Online* Prerelease, since he'd arrived before anyone else we knew. We decided the final cards for the zombie deck he'd be running in the grinders the following morning and called it an early night, him to get some sleep before the 9 AM open and me to read some Harry Potter. I get back to my room around the same time Rubin and Hegstad arrive, and I talk to Ben about his bizarre no-Wish Grizzly Fate Mirari's Wake deck he plans on playing in Standard until my head starts to hurt, so I decide to just head to bed and let the adventures of my friends at Hogwarts help clear my mind. 

I wake up Thursday morning to a phone call from my mother, who – despite remembering there's a three hour time difference between New Hampshire and California – decides that it's a good idea to call me at noon her time. I decide to stay up and head over to the site rather than try to get back to sleep and spend much of the day watching the grinders and getting my draft on. Scouting the field makes me happy with my deck choice, as I see loads of Wake, monoblack control and madness, precisely what my deck was built to beat. Bryn Kenney wins the first grinder with a zombie deck with blue for Aether Burst and Infiltrator, changes which seemed to only weaken the deck in testing by reducing the sheer power of Corrupt. Regardless, since it seems like people might look to the grinders for tech, I decide to make sure I have cards for the mirror match, and add a Gempalm Polluter to the main deck to go with the one in the sideboard, with the added bonus that the Polluter happens to get around Circle of Protection: Black as well. The remainder of the day is largely uneventful, and after dinner with the remnants of Team Deadguy (Dave Price, Tony Tsai, and Jon Finkel) at a nearby Mexican joint, I decide to call it a night.

Friday morning, I arrive on site well-rested and ready to game. Word on the street tells me that there will be more g-r than anticipated, so I toy with the idea of adding Shambling Swarms to the main deck, but content myself with simply playing the fourth Corrupt in the sideboard. I'm not thrilled by the prospect of a g-r-heavy field, but with the full complement of Corrupts, the matchup is at least winnable. Had I realized just how much g-r there would be – and how little g-u, comparatively, I certainly would have played Mutilates and more Corrupts main deck and made room for Shambling Swarms in the sideboard. As it was, I expected the field to resemble Regionals far more closely than it actually did, so my "crush g-u, Wake, ‘Tog, and black" plan – which my deck accomplished quite nicely – wasn't necessarily the best call for the tournament.

It worked out, at least, for the first few rounds. Pairings go up and I'm playing against a rather quiet fellow playing Wake. None of the games are particularly interesting, with game one going completely according to plan as I pick apart his hand and bash with some zombies. He manages to come close to stabilizing, but Corrupt finishes him off. Game two sees him play a turn three Exalted Angel and I have no answer – which is reasonable, considering after sideboarding I only have Chainer's Edict to deal with it before Corrupt goes online. Five turns later, his Angel is still alive and thus I am not. Game three is closer, as he manages to Wrath my early offensive, but I come back with Unholy Grotto, Rotlung Reanimator, and Gempalm Polluter. He looks to gain control with Moment's Peace, and manages to get Mirari into play, but passes the turn to me when I have a lethal number of zombies to Polluter him with. Immediately he says "I made a mistake", and when he tries to Renewed Faith and Mirari it when I attack, so I respond to the copy with Polluter for the game.

Matches 1-0 Games 2-1

Round two is quite a bit more stressful, as instead of a turn one Krosan Verge, my opponent plays turn one Wooded Foothills. My heart sinks momentarily, but when he sacrifices the Foothills and plays a Birds of Paradise, I'm quite relieved. While beasts is not a great matchup by any means, it's certainly much better than g-r, as they're far worse equipped to deal with my creatures without tons of burn spells, so often I can get in a good amount of damage and just win with Corrupts. Game one goes exactly according to plan, as Contested Cliffs never shows up on the other side of the board. I'm able to strip a few key spells with Duress and Cabal Therapy, and Withered Wretch takes care of the flashback on Beast Attack before it can become a problem. Zombies chip away at his life total, and Gempalm Polluter slowly whittles him down when we reach a stalemate. I'm never in any real danger and Corrupt finishes it.

Matches 3-0 Games 6-1

Between the end of my match and the start of the first draft, EFro and I make a trip to a nearby sushi restaurant – the Octopus Garden – to get some lunch. I'm a sushi fanatic, and I'm thrilled that there's a place near the site where I can indulge in my obsession. At PT-Yokahama, I ate at a sushi bar a few blocks from the site almost every day I was there. It was the sort of place with a little conveyor belt that brings around little plates with either small rolls or two pieces of sushi on each, color coded according to price. All in all, I ate forty-seven plates of sushi while I was there. I didn't reach quite that amount while in San Diego, but by the time I left town I'd become a regular – the chefs knew my order (the Sushi B special) and the host knew to seat me at the bar and bring me a water and EFro a Diet Coke. I suppose that can happen when you go four times in a weekend.

Before the draft starts, I look at the standings to find what pod I'll be in – and find, to my amusement and frustration, that I have the lowest tiebreakers of all of the 3-0's. Apparently, my first round opponent failed to win a match, my second round opponent lost again in the third round, and I'd been paired down against Bill, who was now 1-1-1 – leaving me with pretty much the mathematically lowest possible tiebreakers one can have at 3-0. I started to get visions of Nationals and Worlds last year, where I finished 12th and 11th, both times missing Top 8 on tiebreakers, and resolved that I'd just have to keep winning so my tiebreakers wouldn't matter. With that in mind, it was time to draft…

I was excited to be in first place at the end of the day, but wary of getting overconfident. I'd seen too many Day 1 undefeated players miss the elimination rounds to think I was anything close to a lock for Top 8, so despite EFro's requests, I wasn't buying any dinners just yet – or, at least, I didn't plan on it. I head to a nearby Thai restaurant for dinner with EFro, Finkel, Zev, and Ben Stark, and manage to lose the credit card game when the check comes. For those who are unaware, the credit card game is when at the end of the meal, everyone shuffles up their credit cards and one person's card is picked at random to pay for the entire meal. It sounds silly – and if you're the sort who's a light eater, it probably is – but for us gamer types, it adds a bit of excitement even to the simple act of paying for dinner. Some people take the game more seriously than others, making sure to order something really expensive in order to increase their "pot odds". I'm pretty sure I had pot odds on this particular meal, having had both an appetizer and an entrée as well as two drinks – one of which, by the way, was called a "Bird of Paradise", which I of course ordered simply due to the name. It was good, but not fantastic. Thankfully, it wasn't as expensive as the card is nowadays, since I was the one stuck with the bill.

After dinner I stayed up for a little while, going online in my hotel to check my email and talk to some friends from home to let them know how I was doing. A lot of my friends who don't even play **Magic** keep up with the *Sideboard* coverage of tournaments, and some of them even follow it close enough that they recognize the top players by name. Some of them haven't quite figured out the whole nationality thing, though, so I have to explain to them that no, I didn't play against Kai Budde at U.S. Nationals. Still, it's always nice to have fans - even beyond my mom. 

I drifted off to sleep that night somewhere between nervous and excited – after, of course, tearing through a few more chapters in Harry Potter.
