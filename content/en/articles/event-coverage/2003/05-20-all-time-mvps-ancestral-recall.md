Welcome to the newest feature of Sideboard.com – All-Time MVPs. As Gary Wise is fond of pointing out, **Magic** is almost a decade old and carries with it a rich and interesting history – a history worth exploring and documenting. Most of the people playing nowadays were not playing in the “good old days”, when the game wasn’t quite as well organized or understood by its players. In those days – just as today – the game was defined by the cards. That’s what we’ll be looking at here.

Most players today have never dropped six cards on the first turn, Time Walked, attacked for five and then Timetwistered. Some players have never felt the helpless frustration of an Icy Manipulator/Winter Orb lock. They have never been the victim of Political Trickery while playing a Kjeldoran Outpost deck. There are some players who, believe it or not, have never seen Tolarian Academy wreaking havoc on turn one. For these players, All-Time MVPs will be a chance to learn some of **Magic’s** history. For those who do remember such things, All-Time MVPs will be a trip down memory lane. Better still, it will be a trip down *your* memory lane. Tell me why your favorite cards should be All-Time MVPs. I’ll be using your input to write future articles. 

All-Time MVPs will highlight cards from **Magic’s** past that have had a dramatic influence on the game. Each week, we’ll be looking at one of **Magic’s** most powerful cards, what impact it had on its metagame(s), what makes it stand out from similar cards and how the card influenced the game, overall. All-Time MVPs’s first subject is, appropriately, Ancestral Recall. For my money, this is the most powerful card ever printed. It certainly gets you the most bang for your buck.

### What’s an Ancestral Recall?

Printed in Alpha, Beta and Unlimited, Ancestral Recall is one of five cards in those sets that gives its caster three of “its color’s mechanic” for one mana. In addition to blue’s Ancestral (draw cards), black got Dark Ritual (quick burst of mana), red got Lightning Bolt (direct damage), green got Giant Growth (big creatures), and white got the seriously outclassed Healing Salve (gain life/prevent damage). Four of these cards are common – and four of them survived many sets past Unlimited. Ancestral Recall was not one of them. Ancestral is the only one of these cards that is rare – made so because it was recognized to be so powerful – and it is clearly superior to the other four. Incidentally, Giant Growth and Healing Salve are the only two of the five still in the basic set, as Lightning Bolt and Dark Ritual have also been deemed too powerful for Standard. 

“Draw three cards” are the three sweetest words in **Magic**. Card advantage is one of the most powerful forces driving the game. If I have more cards than my opponent, I have more options. If I have more options, I should win. Blue is rife with cards that allow a player to snag some extra cards – but they all trace their ancestry back to the powerhouse in Alpha. There is nothing to Ancestral Recall: pay ![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif), get three cards, any time you want them. It’s this simplicity, this sheer ruthless effectiveness, that makes Ancestral Recall so powerful. No drawbacks, no conditions, no symmetrical effects… Ancestral has no checks on it whatsoever. Instant speed multi-card drawing for a single mana with nothing to lose. I don’t think we’ll ever see such things again. 

### When It Was Legal…

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>Ancestral Recall is in elite company as one of the earliest cards to be restricted and one of the first cards to be rotated out of the core set.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Giants roamed the earth. Ancestral Recall is in elite company as one of the earliest cards to be restricted and one of the first cards to be rotated out of the core set. By making it rare instead of common, Richard Garfield was imposing a sort of pre-emptive restriction on it. Sales of **Magic** cards were originally predicted to be much lower than they actually were. It was thought that an individual might buy a starter deck and a few booster packs, probably not more than $20-30 worth of cards. As such, by virtue of being a rare, Ancestral wouldn’t show up all the time. People bought more cards than were anticipated and it became apparent how brutal Ancestral Recall was, so it was quickly restricted. 

Ancestral is still legal (and still restricted) in Classic/Type I. It is still played in virtually every deck, by virtually every person who owns one of these pricey gems. While the raw strength of drawing three cards is apparent, it bears worth mentioning that in the format where Ancestral is legal, the best cards are significantly faster and more powerful than most modern cards. Imagine playing with cards from the current Standard format and Ancestral. The three cards you draw may be something like plains, Astral Slide and Wrath of God. Compare that to Type I, where you may draw Mox Jet, Dark Ritual and Necropotence as your three cards. In the first example, you’ve gained a clear advantage that will take a couple of turns to ripen. In the second, you’re about to crack your opponent’s head open. The difference in the speed of the formats makes the card function at different power levels (though it would still be absolutely amazing in either format). The point is that, if you’ve never played with Type I cards, the effect of Ancestral Recall is more powerful than you might imagine. 

### Why Was It Great?

Ancestral Recall is amazing at recharging a spent hand. All of a sudden, there are three new options which, since they are in the deck, are probably the sort of thing you would want to be drawing. Even better is that the low cost of Ancestral compliments this effect nicely. Tapping only a single land for this much power is a trade worth making in all but the most desperate circumstances. It also means that you can probably play one or two of the extra cards immediately, rather than being forced to wait and untap. Ancestral shines, especially in the face of black’s powerful discard effects, like Mind Twist and Hymn to Tourach. As quickly as black can pull cards out of your hand, Ancestral can put them back. This quick burst of cards can make all the difference in tight matches.

The raw power of drawing three cards is impressive. Combining it with its minimal cost makes it amazing. Compare Ancestral Recall to some of its descendants. Some versions of Psychatog decks utilize Concentrate, a card that allows its caster to draw three cards. Concentrate costs ![2 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/f9/cc/f9cc8a15b009997919d58ad79964384351495d1b6c9231f938316577453038a8.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif) and is a *sorcery*! Even at four times the casting cost of Ancestral Recall, with a reduction in flexibility, Concentrate is playable – and not a bad option for raw card-drawing in the current Standard metagame. For analogous instant-speed card drawers, we have to look at Opportunity and Inspiration. For ![4 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/36/29/3629288966daa042decf01d3752789ab6676fddd67c3679fa580cd3baecae158.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif), you get to draw four cards at instant speed. For ![3 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/7f/5d/7f5d2f1e55eaa9d19a9a23027b63bfbb5b39dbf8642160dda1a477a03df23a6a.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif), you get two. Between these two cards, it seems likely that any instant that let you draw three cards would cost ![3 Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/7f/5d/7f5d2f1e55eaa9d19a9a23027b63bfbb5b39dbf8642160dda1a477a03df23a6a.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif)![Blue Mana](https://i.ormos.uk/b7/5d/b75de4f4cec208d24e23ce112a4bb89540d55aa95fe1ef377d1c7c5d2b6bdb50.gif). That’s a lot of mana compared to Ancestral Recall. It serves to demonstrate just how amazingly unfair Ancestral Recall was.

### The Big Picture

The natural power of Ancestral Recall made it a popular spinoff card. Since drawing cards is one of blue’s main abilities, practically every new expansion features cards that can be compared to Ancestral. There have been many interesting variants of Ancestral besides the simple, more fairly costed versions of it. The strangest one is probably Library of Lat-Nam from *Alliances*, which forced an opponent to choose between allowing you to Ancestral or Tutor. This card was pretty highly costed – and usually ended up giving you a very, very expensive Tutor.

Most variants fall into one of two categories: card-drawing and search. The card-drawing spells tend to cost more, falling in the range of 4+ mana typically, with many of them changing into sorceries. Concentrate is the quintessential raw card-drawing spell. Bulky and awkward, often forcing its caster to tap out in blue, Concentrate does its job and gets some cards from your deck to your hand, at the price of economy and convenience. Occasionally, there is a good instant-speed card drawer, like Whispers of the Muse, but they tend to be extremely powerful and so are seldom seen. Search cards tend to be cheap instants, facilitating consistent draws. They don’t often gain their casters any card advantage. In fact, good search cards sometimes cause their casters card disadvantage, as in the case of Careful Study. In *Ice Age*, the card Brainstorm is almost *exactly* Ancestral Recall, except that you have to put two of the cards back. Smart deckbuilders have minimized this disadvantage by using cards with shuffle mechanics to compliment it. In decks with a wide selection of cards, like Oath decks, putting two dead cards back and shuffling them in is practically Ancestral.

Ancestral Recall is the basis of blue: fast card-drawing, the ability to dig deeper looking for threats and the versatility to be used practically any time in any deck. It’s an enabler – it almost never wins the game on its own, but you can always count on it for the assist… or the occasional save.
