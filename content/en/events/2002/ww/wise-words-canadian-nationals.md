I’ve never felt quite this helpless heading into a tournament. It’s Tuesday afternoon, Pistol Pete Radonjic and The Wolf (as Steve Wolfman shall be referred to in these pages here to for) are busy testing their butts off behind me as well they should, as we’ll all be on our way to Canadian Championships in less than 24 hours. I have no deck, I have no cards, and I have no chance. Well, maybe that last part isn’t entirely true.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/0d/5e/0d5e392537c216511eff6fe51c431ed8645f7f4235863c5aa04913fcbf8cc8f1.jpg)I’ve been playing Standard 8-mans on ***Magic** Online* the last few weeks, and yesterday I did my first Scourge drafting since the quarantine, going 5-0 over the two, so maybe I’m not as lacking in preparation as feared, but I’m still reading *Scourge* uncommons and rares, and a decklist for the other half of the tournament would be nice, but instead of working on such trivialities, I’m writing Wise Words while they game. Why? Because I love you, gentle literate, that’s why! Oh yeah, and because starting to test now would be a lot like starting my studying the morning of an exam: If I don’t know my (expletive deleted) by now, I’m not going to. I haven’t given up, I know I can use superior tournament experience to my advantage and despite ***Magic** Online*, I’m sure I’ll still get a couple of “byes” in the Limited portion of the tournament, but I may not be the right choice if there’s a Fantasy tournament this time around. At least not at that cost.

Wake, red-green, ‘Tog, Reanimator, blue-green, monoblack control, Slide, elves, goblins, zombies, hippos, and she told two friends, and they told two friends, and so on, and so on. There are a lot of options in Standard. My bet is that the players who succeed will be the ones who know their decks best, as everything seems to be more or less equal and match up knowledge should be huge. I’ve always felt that the first rule of choosing a deck was to try and be the best player in any given tournament at playing that particular deck, and that philosophy should hold truer now than ever before. In Limited, blue-white and red-black are both feeling really strong to me, the former because of the synergy *Scourge* provides and the reduction of Sparksmith quantities to one pack’s worth, the latter because the lack of ‘Smiths means people are drafting decks in the blue-white-green realms for the first time in eight months, but really, with Nationals using Rochester draft, I’ll likely just draft what everyone else doesn’t. Some rules never change.

With Canadian Nats happening on this side of the Atlantic and responsible for the first (and only, until last year) team to unseat the Americans at World Championships, it often warrants web coverage and a closer look than most other National Championships, especially when you consider its temporal proximity to U.S. Nats and Euros. Here then is a brief look at the tournament’s history and a few of the names you should be following in the coverage:

### 1995

![](https://i.ormos.uk/83/00/8300871e26c75052f9a0111ee42adaa645157f5ace2698e679878180fa1bca2e.gif)The first Canadian Nationals coincided with the first-ever Prerelease, held for *Ice Age* in Toronto. The tournament, taking place before the DCI had established a more finalized code, saw players faced with a choice of building decks with either Revised or Fourth Edition, but not both. In the end, Canada’s first **Magic** star Eric Tam took the honors, defeating Ottawa’s Shawn Davies in the final.

### 1996

Held in the country’s center in Winnipeg, the 1996 Nationals produced Canada’s first great team, with Gary Krakower defeating Vancouver’s Terry Lau in the Finals. Lau’s long time teammate, Pete Radonjic, who’d just finished third at Pro Tour-Columbus added some firepower, as did defending champion Tam. The team had a disappointing World Championships, coming 7th, but this team, split evenly between Vancouver and Toronto, set the tone for the Canadian competitive scene for the rest of the decade.

### 1997

![](https://i.ormos.uk/e1/83/e183e8a7273bbf398ce5f7690fa4bd878c402278dc5cf7ab725e5f5cdb3d37e6.jpg)Back in Toronto, the ’97 Championships produced the eventual World Champions. Krakower repeated as National Champion, defeating Wolfman in the Quarterfinals en route to the Finals, where he defeated the then-eligible Mike Donais. Joining them were Gab Tsang and Ed Ito, and the foursome, who Radonjic still refers to as Team Toronto went to Worlds where Tsang led them to Canada’s lone World Championship on the strength of his first-place finish in the Swiss by an astounding five points.

### 1998

Thought to be past his best days, Radonjic turned on the jets for one last victory lap, defeating upstart Nick Chen in the Finals. Terry Tsang and Ryan Kirk joined them on the team, with the foursome fighting valiantly to defend the championship after a poor start, finishing fourth.

While most Canadians will refuse to acknowledge that this tournament ever happened, objectivity insists I acknowledge. Playing a luck-filled Hatred deck, Albertan Mark Rajotte, who had no clue as far as Limited went, managed to draw the Hatred-Ritual combination enough times to win the tournament, then added insult to injury by writing a tournament report insisting that this was the proof that **Magic** is primarily a game of luck and anyone can win on any given day. Reality caught up at World Championships, where he may have actually failed to win a game. I’m not sure if that’s actually true, but every time I try to look up information on Rajotte, I start involuntarily spitting and swearing, and frankly, my computer screen is getting fairly disgusting.

### 2000

Returning from a year in Amsterdam, Ryan Fuller turned up with a Stompy deck that saw him run all over the competition, leaving him as the last man standing when all was said and done. Fuller defeated 13-year-old Murray “The Mauler” Evans in the Finals, with the two green men being joined by Gab Tsang and Sam Lau. The team went on to lose in the finals at World Championships.

### 2001

Ottawa’s only Nationals to date saw an old face make a triumphant return. Terry Tsang, a former team member who’d seen a decline in the quantity of **Magic** he’d been playing, made one last leap at the brass ring with his win, defeating Elijah Pollock in the finals. The two were joined on the National team by the Midwest’s Sam Lount.

### 2002

After being stung by the National Team’s poor showing in 1999, it wasn’t too shocking that Jurgen Hahn became the first Canadian National Champion to not attend Worlds. Hahn defeated now-Pro Tour regular Jon Stern in the final, with Stern inheriting the team with Jurgen’s absence, being joined by Ray Weiss and 4th place finisher Dave Rood.
