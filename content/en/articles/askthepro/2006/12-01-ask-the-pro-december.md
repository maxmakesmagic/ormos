Wizards of the Coast welcomes Hall of Famer Raphaël Lévy as the featured player here at "Ask the Pro." A fixture on the Pro Tour for the last eight years, Raphaël is uniquely positioned to answer your questions about the life of a professional **Magic** player, give a historical perspective on the game and high-level tournament scene, share stories about travelling the world, and talk about the role **Magic** plays in his life.

Seventh on the lifetime Pro Points list, Raphaël began his Pro Tour career back at Pro Tour-Paris in 1997. He became a regular on the tour starting at the 1998 World Championships. Since then he hasn't missed a Pro Tour, an astounding streak of 49 consecutive events. He was recently honored as a member of the [**Magic** Pro Tour Hall of Fame](/en/articles/event-coverage/undated/tournaments-events-trading-card-game-magic-the-gathering-2)'s 2006 class and was inducted at the 2006 World Championships in Paris. Send your question, along with your name and location, via this email form. Answers will be posted every Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday. 

<table><tbody><tr>&#10;<td>&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td> <b>December 30, 2006</b></td>&#10;<td> </td>&#10;<td><img alt="" src="https://i.ormos.uk/7d/ea/7deab367d2187ba6dc0e8758afb21d975578affe86883947e65fa45de83cb2e0.jpg"></td>&#10;</tr>&#10;<tr>&#10;<td colspan="3">&#10;<table>&#10;<tbody><tr>&#10;<td><p><b>Q</b>: In your October 28 question, you mentioned that a player asked around on an IRC channel for advice on what to play. What sort of areas do you look around to get a good feel of the professional metagame, especially on the Internet? My local area has a weird metagame that doesn't fit in much at all and we always seem to have problems when we head to PTQs or Champs as a result. How can we improve our view of the metagame?<br> -Antonio Villarreal<br></p>&#10;<p><b>A:</b> Hello Antonio,</p>&#10;<p>When you playtest for a tournament, it's better to have a good feeling about what the decks you're going to play against. The metagame in a given area will depend a lot on the level of the players in that area. The more experienced the players are, the more chances you'll have to face a metagame you expect. In an area where players play more casually, they won't have access to or be looking for the latest tech on the Internet. They will bring their own decks to the tournaments, and generally those tournaments aren't very representative nor will they tell you if the deck you chose to play is good enough for you to bring it to a more important tournament. If you win, that means your deck is probably good enough to beat some random or rogue decks, and not necessarily mean that it's ready for the next level.</p>&#10;<p>For the players who want to seriously prepare for a tournament, they browse for the latest updates of Constructed decks on strategy sites and other forums where players around the world discuss the metagame. For this year's <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20070126094216/http://www.wizards.com/default.asp?x=events/magic/worlds">World Championships</a>, for example, it was hard and mostly useless to start playtesting before the results of <a href="/en/events/2006/champsmap06/2006-champs-standard-map">Champs</a>. The reason is simple: as long as you don't know what your opponents are going to play, it's a waste of time trying out your deck. Testing your deck for weeks and figuring out that it loses to the most popular decks that weren't in your playtesting field is a very frustrating feeling. Champs gives a good basis for a Standard metagame for Worlds and we can start working from there. </p>&#10;<p>The Internet changed the game entirely. Ten years ago, there was no &quot;world metagame&quot; like today. Every group of players had its own tech, and when the tournaments came, they would test against the rest of the field. They guess what you are holding by the way you play your cards, and they represent what they are holding and what you should play around knowing that you're going to fall for it. When you're paired against someone from your black list, whatever your matchup is, you have extra pressure on your shoulders. It's a vicious circle: the more you play against someone you often lose to, the less confident you are in your match, the less tight your play will be, and the more you are going to lose. </p>&#10;<p>There are a few players I hate to play against. The first name that comes to mind is Kai Budde. I think I played against him more than against anyone one else on the Pro Tour (may be tied with Antoine and Olivier). It took me six years and a total of 10 matches to actually have my first win over him, and that was in Kobe in 2004. He is definitely a better player, but winning nine out of 10 matches, in different formats? That's unreal. Another player is Frank Canu. We used to play together in team tournaments and he knows all about the way I play. He always has a good read on me, and knows how to take advantage of it. </p>&#10;<p>When you're facing someone from your black list, just have in mind that he's just a player, and that you're probably better than him (that's what you have to tell yourself before each match, anyway!). Stay focused on the cards themselves, and put away all the instances of your opponent's name from your mind. </p>&#10;<p>When you feel like you have the advantage over your opponent, that you are on his black list, don't hesitate to take advantage of it as long as you don't fall for your own trap (that you don't make mistakes because you lose focus).
