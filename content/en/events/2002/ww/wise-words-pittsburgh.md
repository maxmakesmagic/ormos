I always have a good time when I’m in Pittsburgh. Upon the completion of GP-Detroit, I hopped in my “classic” 1989 Nissan Maxima with Eugene Harvey and Paul Sottosanti in tow and proceeded to race Nate Heiss’s less vintage automobile for the vaunted spot behind Mike Turian’s swervy lead for five hours before landing in the infamous Hobart House, whose living room couch hosted my nocturnal stay for around five hundred hours. The official explanation for this detour is Worlds work. In four weeks or so, CMU-TOGIT’s finest will be making our way to Berlin with three formats on the agenda, so it’s time to get on track. Unofficially of course, any excuse to hang out with the mighty Potato, his lady friend Rachel, Eu-broken, The Heiss and Sotto is a good one. This is one great tax write off.

Poker, Magic, baseball games, movies… good times and good chats with good friends. Last night we went to the world famous Original Hot Dog Shop, a.k.a. The O and gamed while snacking on an order of fries large enough to feed twelve gamers and still have leftovers go to waste. Complex or simple, clever or not, how, for any man, could these not be good times. This is what gaming’s all about: camaraderie. “All you need is a deck and a friend.” 

Thinking constantly about **Magic**, games and life in general, a lot of topics have come up for discussion that need to be shared, some strategic, some philosophical, and what better place to do it but here in Wise Words, which is really one big binary Hobart House anyways. Wow, I’m sappy in print. Be that as it may though, this man’s got a job to do. Let’s get to it.

### Detroit

I have to admit, I didn’t care too much about GP-Detroit. With three other formats to work on for Worlds and this being the only event I’d be playing Block in, I figured my time could be better spent elsewhere, and that’s before accounting for an environment that I felt was awful for the first twelve rounds of the tournament.

Think about it: *Onslaught* Block’s defining deck was goblins, with mirror matches a plenty meaning that many fates would be decided by who could draw better with a highly draw dependent deck. Doesn’t sound too skill intensive, does it? The thing is though, while that was the direction the metagame was heading in, with Detroit on the books, the format has flip-flopped. The most successful archetypes in the tournament were all control-style decks designed to asphyxiate goblins, from Bob Maher’s red-white control to CMU- TOGIT’s Slide to the monowhite decks to Nate Heiss’s black-white concoction to both Form of the Dragon variants, leaving those decks to fight it out with one another. Ladies and gentlemen, we have a healthy, skill-intensive format again. For that, I rejoice.

What happens next though is what’s going to make the PTQ season really interesting: With the Detroit results in the books, the control decks are now going to have to soften their anti-goblin stance, meaning the beatdown players of the world will still have their favorite red men to run with as a viable deck choice, and now they won’t have to build their decks with the understanding they must thrive in the mirror. In other words, the goblin decks will be back to full power, while in the GP, those decks built that way lost the ‘mirror’ to teched-out counterparts that were apparently weaker against control. It’s the endless cycle we love to call the metagame. It’s complex, it’s frustrating, and while I have to admit I’m kind of glad I’m not going to get entirely swept up by that particular twister, it’s going to make for some really interesting tournaments.

I guess time heals all wounds. A couple of years ago, I fought a fruitless battle in these pages against the infamous PTR (Peter Szigeti), not to mention my editor, but since then, we’ve gotten over some of our differences, and I for one have come to appreciate that in his own way, the man does do what he can to help nurture the game, a lot more than most, and for less personal gain than many who do their part.

That said, just making a contribution isn’t enough to get the special mention in these hallowed pages. One’s work must stand out above that of others, and know what? Peter’s done it. Great stuff. Yes, the man stole a prosthetic leg and emptied the contents of his nose on Antonio DeRosa at GP-LA, yes, he got a game loss for the aggressive application of his posterior to a top-decked Searing Flesh in Yokohama and yes he’s pulled the wings and legs off of numerous pro and amateur gamers throughout the years but hey, what can you say? The man’s got personality and his articles are nuts. Check them out.

### Goblin Bidding

Randy, if you’re reading this, I KNOW you were squealing with delight as the deck lists from Bangkok started rolling in. When *Onslaught* came out, everyone knew that tribal, the set’s primary focus, would have some kind of effect on Constructed play, but really, can any of you say you figured it would go this far?

Sure, it looked like barring Slice and Dice, there might be a wizard deck out there (led of course by Kai Budde himself, as depicted in not one but two excellent and flattering pictures of the German), and when Timberwatch Elf made its presence known to the world, elves were the next logical step, but this is crazy. For years, when talking about drafting good decks instead of good cards, the example I’ve used is that you don’t play Fact or Fiction in Sligh, but apparently, Living Death, or at least the next best thing, is a good match.

Three players, led by eventual champ Tsuyoshi Fujita, played the deck, and all three made Top 8. I’m pretty sure that if you’d asked me in the past to bet my life on Goblin Matron being a good Constructed card I’d be dead by now, but she acts as a Wirewood Herald of sorts, one that can be tossed at a target of choice for two damage a lot of the time. Sick, sick, sick. What gave these guys the idea to build this monstrosity is beyond me, but building a new, successful archetype this late in the year is next to impossible, so be it Bangkok, Beijing, Brussels or Boston, the effort and results must be applauded.

### Fun Format

Eugene introduced me to a great alternative to serious M:tG-ing a couple of days ago that I thought share-worthy. I’ve talked about DC10, the infinite mana top-deck variant, in these pages before, but Harway, as Patrick Mello has taught the Pittsburghers to call him, added an interesting twist that actually gives the game something of a strategic function.

Taking a large stack of cards, the winner of any one game takes all the cards drawn or used in that game, with the goal being to have the most cards when the stack has been used in its entirety, with either player allowed to concede at any time to cut losses. We played this with Heiss’s 5-Color deck, and it quickly became apparent that there was actually a lot of strategy involved in knowing when to scoop (Fact or Fiction and Rush of Knowledge were good examples of spells one should concede to before resolution to deprive one’s opponent, the very likely victor, extra cards for their pile), when to slow play a card (not much point in Disintegrating for a million on turn one and getting only one card. Instead, hold onto it while a few more cards are drawn) and so on. We used a shared graveyard and library and a lot of powerful cards, but the game can just as easily work with and handful of commons. Give it a try next time you’ve got nothing better to do between rounds.

If this were a Booster Draft, I think the pick would be an easy one, with black providing the best card (albeit a mediocre one) in Gluttonous Zombie as well as the brightest prospects for *Legions* and *Scourge*, but the fact it was Rochester complicated things. Maybe too much.

Thing is, while I wanted to take the Zombie, with the pack so heavily geared towards black it seemed like Sottosanti, sitting in seat eight, would almost definitely get the black wheel of Severed Legion and Wretched Anurid, making my first pick pointless. At the same time, while taking the Defender was tempting, it would make me want to go the cleric route, which seemed kind of impossible when there would obviously end up being so many black players. The Rampart, at least to me, would have been a clear signal towards either white-blue or white-red, not a bad thing when this pack would send so many in other directions, but at the same time felt like the weaker of the two white cards in the pack, and picking it assumes everyone is going out of their way to be cooperative, which is beyond a little presumptuous, so I took the Charm, though in retrospect I think it was wrong. I’m just sure what would have been right.

My hope in taking the Charm was that everyone would stay away from green, but there ended up being two other green drafters, one of whom, two seats in front of me, switched from black to my eventual second color red thanks to the black sandwiching he was getting from Sotto and seat six. My deck ended up fairly awful, I went 0-2, and in an attempt at self-inflicted mercy, disassembled the deck before anyone had the chance to ask me to play a third match with it.

Turian says he would have just taken the black card and hoped the rest settled into seats 3, 5, and 7. Harvey says he’d want a stronger green card to commit to what has turned into the format’s weakest color, but everyone agreed the pick wasn’t clear cut and that Zombie, Goliath, Defender, Rampart and Charm could each have a case made for them, but in the end, I think black is the color you really want to get into if the packs provide, so in retrospect, I’d probably take the zombie. Makes for some fun conversation regardless.

That said, there gaming to be done and fun to be had, and I for one don’t want to spend the rest of this great day at the keyboard, so I’m out of here. 

Have a good week. I know I will.
