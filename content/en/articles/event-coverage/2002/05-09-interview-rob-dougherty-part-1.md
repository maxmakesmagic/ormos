Rob Dougherty is one of the most successful men in **Magic**, both as a player and as a businessman. Where his **Magic** game is concerned, Dougherty has proven himself to be one of the best around, racking up plenty of Top 8 finishes in Pro Tours, in just this season alone. Of course, he is also the owner of the highly successful Your Move Games stores. How does one go about achieving such success? What does it take to become a mainstay on the Pro Tour? What does it take to run one of the world's top game stores?

And what's up with that whole Star Trek wedding thing?

I sat down with Rob at Pro Tour-Nice to get his answers to these questions, and more.

**Wachter:** How did you first get into **Magic**?

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/Worlds2001/814.jpg)**Dougherty:** I got into **Magic** when the game first came out, or slightly afterwards... it was during Alpha. I was at the GenCon where the game was released, I saw it, and I heard about the random card mechanism and playing for ante. I thought, "Wow, what an incredible scam, I'll never play that game." Then a friend of mine, Jeff Spear, had a friend who was a connoisseur of games, he wrote for a game magazine. This person who knew games really well told Jeff and I "You should play this game, it's amazing" like a month after Gencon, and we said "Oh we heard about it, sounds like a horrible scam, no way." He said, "No, really it's amazing. You should pick it up." So, we each bought a starter and two booster packs, and became completely addicted, and spent thousands and thousands of dollars on cards.

Fortunately, that was during Alpha, Beta and Unlimited/*Arabian Nights* time, so that was actually a pretty good investment. I was a poor college student at the time so I couldn't afford cards, and I first got into selling cards during Beta. I was playing a game with Jeff Spear, and he was going to order pizza, and I was going to have ramen noodles because I was a college student. Jeff said, "Hey you know, I'm going to order from Uno's, and I'll give you half of a large pizza if you give me that Time Walk." I was like, "Ah! One card from a pack that I paid $2.50 for? Sure, it's the rare one, but less than $2.50 for half of a $15 pizza? Sounds like a pretty good deal to me!" So I gave him that card and I got my half a pizza, and I thought "Gosh, if he's willing to do that, I bet other people would be willing to give things for cards as well" so I started selling cards online. I believe it was... I can't really remember the name of the newsgroup, but there was some tiny newsgroup that started up for **Magic** at the time. As far as I know, there was no one else selling cards online.

**Wachter:** So you were the first?

**Dougherty:** I was either the first or one of the first. Like, I remember a month or two into it when I saw the first post from someone else selling cards. If other people were selling cards, they weren't doing it very openly. This was wicked early, this was like Beta. The thing is, people had a completely different mentality about cards. They basically were like "Oh, it's a cool game, maybe I'll spend $20 on it" and they'd get to the point where they had spent $15 or $18, and their friends would have this cool Black Knight card or this cool Giant Spider card, or this cool Northern Paladin card which they didn't have, and they felt that they had no chance of getting it by buying packs, because they were only going to buy a couple more. So, if they could buy a Black Knight from me for a dollar they were really excited, and of course I was selling an uncommon for a dollar, and the rares for five dollars, and the commons for twenty-five cents, and the packs were $2.50, so it snowballed very quickly. 

By the time *Legends* came out, I got about a hundred boxes of it. I pre-ordered and pre-paid for them, so I actually got the boxes as opposed to the short supply situation that other people had. The money from the sales of those hundred boxes...I started selling those boxes once they got to about $10 a pack, and I used that to fund the first Your Move Games.

**Wachter:** What made you decide to open the store in the first place? Was there anything else besides the story you just told?

**Dougherty:** Well, I'd always had an interest in business. What ended up happening was I was studying electrical engineering. I wasn't much of a high school student; I spent more time outside of school than in school in high school. I went from high school to the army and was in for three years. I got out and started to go to community college and the Gulf War happened so I got called back. I got out again, and I went back to community college, trying to figure out what I wanted to do. My sister was an electrical engineer, and it sounded interesting so I decided to apply to schools for it. I applied to Northeastern University in Boston and they said "If you take these three math and science courses and get As in all of them we'll let you in" and I did.

So, I was an electrical engineering student, and I was selling cards. And it got to the point where I made enough money selling cards that my scholarships and grants which were need-based were being taken away from me, because I didn't need them. I was faced with the prospect of paying full tuition out of my pocket, and that combined with the fact that I was in my third year and I started to realize that I didn't enjoy the subject matter... I really loved the physics and the calculus, and I loved the economics courses I was taking. Unfortunately, all three of those took a PhD to do anything with. So I decided to take a break and pursue this online sales thing I was doing, assuming that I would fail and then I could get my scholarships and grants back. Well, it didn't fail, so I'm still doing it.

**Wachter:** Any interesting stories or experiences from being in the army that you'd like to share?

**Dougherty:** Well, I've got various army stories, I don't know how much time you want to spend on it...

**Wachter:** What was the general experience like?

**Dougherty:** It was really good for me in that I had a complete lack of direction. I think the biggest thing for me was that when I was a high school student, I was like many high school students. I was very absorbed in my own problems, and I had some problems with depression and being down on life, the angst of a teenager thing. I think the number one thing I got out of the military was realizing that things I thought were problems were not actually problems. If no one is shooting at you, things are pretty good. Giving me a more appropriate life perspective was probably the best thing about it.

**Wachter:** What's involved with running a **Magic** store?

**Dougherty:** Obviously, as with any business there's a lot of annoying stuff behind the scenes that people don't think about. But it's a wonderful opportunity for a gamer because you're right in the center of the community, and you get to interact with all the groups and see all the different games. It's a lot of fun. I'm doing what I would want to do, regardless of financial position, so that's a pretty nice thing to be able to say.

**Wachter:** What advice would you give to someone looking to open up their own game store? 

**Dougherty:** Make sure that you're willing to put in the time necessary, which for a start up...unless you have tons of money, you're going to be working like sixteen hour days, every day, seven days a week for a long time...

**Wachter:** So it's not all glory. Some people have this idea of "Oh, I can open a game store and play **Magic** all day" but obviously it's not like that.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/2c/41/2c4153462c0c1df20291d12d90c45aa7dce81d8eca8efb3aa575ad902e2425df.jpg)**Dougherty:** No, not unless you've got enough money that you're hiring a complete staff from the get-go, and you're just there to oversee things. If you're starting it up on any kind of reasonable budget, you're going to be the one behind the counter for the foreseeable future. Which means, you're going to be behind the counter, and you're going to be doing all the ordering, and you're going to be doing all the clean up, and you're going to be doing everything that makes the shop run. And you're going to be doing that until you can afford to hire a staff. So you're pretty much going to be working at the shop all the time, so you better love it.

**Wachter:** How does being a player affect how you run the store, and vice-versa? 

**Dougherty:** I think that it's an absolutely necessity to know what you sell. I love games and I always have. I play roleplaying games, I play the Warhammer games, I play board games, I play **Magic**, so I know what things customers want. I know what I would want to do as far as tournaments go and that sort of thing. So it's pretty easy for me to put together an event that I think people would be interested in, because I know that I would be interested in it. I know what cards are good and what cards are bad, so it helps a lot for selling cards, especially before a price list comes out. Like, I can go through a set and say "These cards are broken, these cards are lame" and I can do pricing before a list comes out.

**Wachter:** As a player, what do you like about the game?

**Dougherty:** Well I think it's the best two-player game ever designed. I'm a big fan of games that have a random element. Just like everybody else I'll curse the game when I get land screwed or that sort of thing, but games like Chess definitely bore me at a certain level because there's a predetermination before you sit down. If they're a higher rated player than you, they're pretty much going to beat you, unless they're not feeling very well that day or you just thought of a new strategy they haven't seen yet. In games without a random element, (a new strategy) is very unlikely if they've been around for a while. So I like the random element of it and the combinations of the strategies involved... the resource management, the strategies involved in the game and the fact that you can play the game without an opponent by working on a deck concept and deciding what you're going to do is just really wonderful. 

Obviously, combining that with the random cards in packs is sheer genius from a marketing perspective. I don't even mean drafting, just the base concept of the game... the cards coming in random packs, people having access to different cards and trying to do the best they can with the card pool they have access to... those are just some really wonderful, unique elements. I mean they were unique when it came out; obviously it has been copied since then. But yeah, I think it's the best two-player game ever designed. 

**Wachter:** Which Pro Tour was your first?

**Dougherty:** Pro Tour 1.

**Wachter:** From that point until Pro Tour-New Jersey which was your first Top 8, did you have any finishes that were notable?

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTNY01/950.jpg)**Dougherty:** My best finish before that Top 8 was 17th place at Pro Tour-Dallas. I had been to a lot of Pro Tours, I'm not exactly sure how many. Something like eight or nine. I was often on the Pro Tour, I went to a lot of qualifiers... it was difficult for me because I couldn't play in the Boston area because I was running the qualifiers there, but I would go to qualifiers in New York and New York City. If there was ever one in the Boston area that I wasn't running, I'd go to that. So yeah, I was often on a lot but didn't have a spectacular finish until that time.

**Wachter:** Did you change your game at all from Pro Tour 1 to then?

**Dougherty:** I definitely put more time into it now than I did early on. New format Constructed is the area where I do well, and I've always been a bit of a deck designer. It took a Block format event and a little bit of luck to get a really strong breakthrough. Basically, up until that time, I spent a lot of time on a format when I had a really exciting idea. I had really exciting ideas mainly for new formats. Small set situations like Ice Age Block and that sort of thing.

**Wachter:** Does running the store ever interfere with testing for a Pro Tour? Or maybe it did before when the store was first starting out, but now not as much?

**Dougherty:** Yeah, it interferes much less now. As a matter of fact, unless you don't have a job at all I'd say that I probably have one of the best jobs for purposes of **Magic** preparation. Initially it was a huge problem, because as you can imagine I spent almost all my time working in the shop. **Magic** players were hanging out there, so if I had some time where there weren't many customers in the shop but there was a good **Magic** player, I could discuss strategies and play games over the counter. But, in general, I had no real time to prepare. As the shops became more successful and I started hiring staff and having more free time, it became a pretty advantageous situation because I had access to a lot of good players and the time to take advantage of it.
