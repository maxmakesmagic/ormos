Some weeks, I sit down at this here computer with Wise Words all planned out. Events of the past week have made my path as clear as a yellow brick road, and all I need is the Tin Man (heart), The Cowardly Lion (courage) and the Scarecrow (if I only had a brain…) to help me traverse it. Other weeks, all I see is a great fog before me, and deadlines force me to move forward into the abyss.

This is one of those other weeks.

I’m not sure what’s going to present itself over the next 2000-some odd words, but I do have a few thoughts and comments on events of the past and future, so this should turn okay, but everything from here on in is impromptu and unplanned. Anyone notice that fog getting darker?

### Kibler

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/natus03/698.jpg)

Having not attended U.S. Nats (or Euros, for that matter) for the first time in over half a decade and ample material to put together this column a week ago, I decided to not to talk about the goings on in San Diego for lack of a personalized viewpoint, but Brian Kibler’s performance there is definitely worth looking at. Not since Mike Long dropped Cadaverous Bloom in his lap has a player so dominated from the opening of the Swiss rounds, and while the rest of the world sometimes prefers to discredit the importance of “the toughest tournament in the world”, with this kind of field, such a performance is pretty impressive.

Due to a formatting change in this year’s Nationals, Kibler had to participate in both Rochester and Standard in each of the Swiss’s two days. Playing zombies, Kibler, who has long been known for his deck building, eased his way to a 3-0 record before switching to Rochester and winning the table, refusing a split in the finals and taking the extra two points off Paul “Little Darwin” Rietzl in round 6. 

As some writers have trouble making the conversion from Day 1 to Day 2, Kibler had to be a little worried, but he started Saturday in style, beating Mike McGee (more on him later I think), Dave Humpherys and Rietzl to advance to 9-0, where, virtually assured a spot in the Top 8, Brian conceded his next two matches to Neil Reeves and Jon Finkel, both friends who happened to provide Kibler with good matchups should they play in the Top 8. Remember, with Kibler eying the Worlds team slot, he wanted to have strong players in the Top 8 to head to Berlin with. To this same end, he drew round 12 with Mike Turian.

Once in Top 8, Kibler fell quickly to a horrible match up, with zombies losing to Josh Wagener’s red-green deck, but as with Kyle Rose’s 13-win start at 1999 PT-LA, Ryan Fuller’s 14-0 at 2001 PT-Tokyo and even Long’s 13-win run leading up to the Bloom incident, Kibler’s Nats performance is one we should try not to forget.

### Malherbaud and Herzog

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/euro03/825.jpg)

Is a performance not dissimilar to Kibler’s, Pierre Malherbaud ran off ten straight wins to start off European Championships. While the feat doesn’t strike me as being quite as impressive (the best-known name Pierre faced on Day 1 was Jens Krause, and his round 10 match against Sigurd Eskeland was the first he’d had against any past or present pro), Pierre’s quiet consistency over the last couple of years hasn’t gotten the recognition it deserves. 

Winner of GP-Naples, 3rd at 1999 European Championships and twice French National Champion (1999, 2001), Malherbaud, who is approaching 100 lifetime Pro Tour points, lacks the big name of players who have done less over their respective careers, because of a lack of that one big finish in a PT event that propels so many into the spotlight. 

Malherbaud played and won at least one match in each individual Masters this year. 21st in Yokohama, 50th in Venice, 27th at the 2002 World Championships… the guy is putting up numbers, and while the Top 8 formula shines the light on less consistent, more erratic players by default (let’s face it, winning $2,000 over and again is far less exciting than $30,000 all at once), this guy keeps coming back. That’s worth a lot when you eventually start winning GPs and running off ten wins to start Euros. 

As for Nico Herzog, who ended Malherbaud’s run in the eleventh round, could there be any more just scenario than this? Herzog burst onto the PT scene in the summer of 1999 when he won Norwegian Championships and Euros before placing 8th at Worlds. It was a shocking emergence, one of the more memorable over the last half-decade, but instead of following it up with Kai-like numbers, Nico’s career stumbled a tad, with consistently solid finishes but little of the silverware that ’99 had gotten him used to. Finally, this year, Nico broke through again.

Playing more or less full-time, Nico finished second at Pro Tour-Chicago this year. Life was good. The winnings there gave him a little stability while the PT points he’d received would qualify him for the Masters for the next year. That meant **Magic** could be a full time job: a whole year in the sun. Then, just as he prepared to settle himself into the PT way of life, Wizards announced the cancellation of the Masters.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/euro03/836.jpg)

No player was hit harder. Instead of those paydays, Nico would be sent back to fighting for prize checks, and as a player whose prize winnings were not being supplemented by other means, that meant the end of the dream. The man was bitter, and who could blame him? A corporate move, designed to better the game and those involved, would hurt a handful of players in a community of millions, and his name was at the top of the list.

Now, though, everything’s gravy. With his win at Euros, where his Swiss run was interrupted only by a loss to Tomi Walamies, Nico collected $15,000, which should lessen the sting of those missing Masters checks. He also becomes the first repeat European Champion, a title not even the great Budde has held, and only the second ever repeat continental champion, following the now retired Masaya Mori. Congratulations Nico. Seems like the cosmic plan came full circle. 

### U.S. Team Shakeup?

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/natus03/658.jpg)

Not necessarily, but rumor out of San Diego was that Josh Wagener might not go to Berlin. That would free up a spot on the team for Jordan Berkowitz, no longer with CMU-TOGIT, who is also planning on skipping the tournament at present. If both players fail to make an appearance, the third slot would fall to Neil Reeves, and then to Brian Kibler, Jon Finkel and Mike Turian in order if those higher up on the list no-show. Either way, the States will go into the tournament as one of the favorites, along with Germany, France and Finland.

### The Most Underrated Card in Scourge…

…at least by ***Magic** Online* drafters has got to be Wirewood Symbiote. Everyone knows that the best green card in *Scourge* is Forgotten Ancient (By the way guys, thanks for designing a card that’s too slow for Standard whilst at the same time virtually unkillable and ridiculously broken in Limited. I haven’t had the misfortunate of having an opponent cast one against me yet, but I’m pretty sure I’ll either cast Pinpoint Avalanche or click the concession button within two turns when I do), but after that it’s a toss up. Ambush Commander is very strong in the late game, as is Root Elemental, but there’s a plausible argument that can be made for the Symbiote as number two.

Presuming the green drafter, who doesn’t have to share their card pool with nearly as many players as in the *Onslaught-Onslaught-Legions* days, has some elves in their deck, the Symbiote dominates the table as long as it manages to stay alive. The key to this card is the fact one can turn the cost of returning an elf to hand into an advantage, doing so after damage is on the stack or repeatedly utilizing coming into play abilities, while at the same time untapping a potential blocker or a creature possessed of a tap ability.

The combinations are numerous: cast my morph, flip up Tribal Forcemage, enjoy the fruits of its labors then do it all over again. Cast Fierce Empath, get a fatty, block with the Empath, return to hand, and repeat. These are extreme situations of course, but your Timberwatch just won’t ever die with this little bug in play, nor will any of your other elves if it suits your fancy. I’m more of a cooperative drafter than most, but next time you see one of these things drifting by 13th, take it: if the green player to your left was drafting properly, they’d have taken it the first time around.

This week, I was assigned an article for the 10th anniversary Sideboard in which the greatest card nicknames of all time would be listed, and while collecting data, a number of my CMU teammates mentioned Charging Slateback as “The Heiss”.

Check this out: Nate Heiss, former PT regular, CMU team member and long time internet contributor, started each of PTs -Chicago and -Yokohama and this year’s U.S. Nationals 4-3, while starting Nats and Chicago 2-2 en route to his 4-3 record, which is now being referred to as “unmorphing the Heiss”. Nate also has an unsightly hunch and can’t block.

  

### Mcgee

Mike McGee faces a dilemma at U.S. Nats that most of us would love to be forced into. Qualified for the comparatively easy JSS Championships, McGee, who insisted a 5-1 record Day 1 of Nations wouldn’t be good enough to continue with that tournament, of course went 6-0 Day 1 and was forced to choose between dropping for the JSS or running the gauntlet in search of World Championships glory. He eventually chose to play, a decision that came back to bite him as losses to Kibler, Dave Humpherys, Gabe Walls and Antonio DeRosa left him out of the Top 8 despite a final round victory over Zvi Mowshowitz, finishing 22nd.

<table><tbody><tr><td>&#10;<h3><i>The JSS is a gold mine.</i></h3>&#10;</td></tr>&#10;</tbody></table>

Did McGee make the right choice? It’s easy for me to say yes, being thirty years old and therefore half a lifetime beyond JSS eligibility, but I understand the lure of money, glory and the Pro Tour. Starting a major tournament 6-0 is hard to do: I’ve been to over 35 PTs and never started one 6-0. You have to be lucky, you have to be good and you have to have focus, but that’s only the start, with maturity, professionalism and the ability to keep your mind on the goal. Were I 15 years old and looking at the standings at the end of Day 1 with my name at the top of the list, I know I’d want to see how far I could take that start, but look at those names: Kibler, Humpherys, Mowshowitz, DeRosa… these guys are heavy hitters, heavier by far than anyone you’ll face in the JSS Championships. They have the edge in experience and poise, not to mention the collected brain trusts of their respective teams standing behind them. The odds are stacked against any usurper, but at least they don’t have much to lose by trying.

The JSS is a gold mine. Old guys like me see it happening and get jealous at the thought, wishing that **Magic** were around in our day so that free tuition could have been an option. Instead, I’m forced to play against those same heavy hitters every PT, and those aren’t the best guys to try to beat when they want that same paycheck. Kids, do yourselves a favor: if you’re in a situation where a PT field offers a long shot at riches and an end to your amateur status, don’t do it. Play the JSS, get your education paid for and be patient. If you’re good enough to stand with the giants when you’re fifteen, you’ll be even better when you’re seventeen, but the money won’t flow so freely. Grab the scholarship cash and run while you can.

Well, this impromptu stuff seems to have worked out okay. We talked a little tech, got up to date in goings on in the **Magic** world, paid homage to a few guys who deserve it and managed to take a few shots at Nate Heiss. My work here is done. Now I must pack. Detroit, here I come!

Have a good week.
