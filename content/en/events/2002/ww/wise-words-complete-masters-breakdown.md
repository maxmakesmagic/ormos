Well, this year just keeps getting weirder and weirder. Eight months ago, I got rejected at the English border. Two months ago, I discovered while at the U.S. border that I couldn’t work for WotC while in the United States and now…

![](https://i.ormos.uk/fc/e0/fce0b3f7362720d0a4a6db2bcb52f6c5723a2e944626e42badf6503296f86718.jpg)Oh man. You’re not going to believe this one. 

On the afternoon of May 23rd, I went to North York General Hospital to pick up some medication. Of course, this being Toronto and SARS being the big deal it is, they forced me to go through a rigorous screening process in order to gain entrance to the building. I passed with flying colors, no big deal, attended to my business and was out of there in twenty minutes.

Three days later, on this past Monday, I was getting ready to drive down to Pittsburgh for some Nationals Standard practice and an in-depth look at the team draft format in which I’d be sharing time with Eugene Harvey and Mike Turian over the next many months leading up to PT-Boston. I’d left my wallet at my mother’s place the night before, and went to retrieve it, but while I was there, she showed me a newspaper clipping from that’s day’s edition. Contained within were the details of a new SARS case at- you guessed it- North York General, one that couldn't be accounted for in that the patient in question had no previous contact with the SARS patients at the hospital. Referred to as “an event” by excited medical professionals who thought it possible that the disease had mutated, as a safety precaution, those same professionals thought it best to impose a ten-day quarantine on all persons who had been within North York General Hospital walls on all dates leading up to and including the 23rd. Of course.

I’m stuck for a week in my unfurnished apartment. No cable. No couch. Just me and my trusty computer. Thank goodness for ***Magic** Online*. I’m not experiencing symptoms and I’m fairly certain that I’ll be fine, but the minute chance I could develop those symptoms means I really can’t do anything for the next week. That includes going to Pittsburgh. That includes playing in the Grand Prix.

Am I upset? Sure, a little. It’s frustrating that I have to miss a big tourney that’s this close to home. It’s frustrating to let your teammates down this close to the tournament in question. It’s frustrating to leave those players depending on your lift down without and it’s frustrating to be stuck here, boxes still packed all around me, waiting for the furniture that their contents will be disposed upon, but not all is lost. Turian and Harvey have picked up Jeff Cunningham for the GP. Steve Wolfman has found an alternate method of transport and Josh Ryder, my other would-be passenger is still searching diligently as of this writing and I’ve got plenty of time on my hands. Puh-len-ty. Enough to do some number crunching and put together a Wise Words like this one.

A few weeks ago, I took a look at the lifetime pro points list. Being a bit of a statistics freak, with the conclusion of the Masters at Yokohama, I wasn’t quite content with that, and was curious to find out how the Series, as a whole, had treated the individuals who’d participated in it. That meant going through each Masters and recording each participant’s personal performance for each of the nine individual and three team events that have been called Masters since the first at 2000 PT-NY. This tabulation doesn’t include the U.S. Draft Challenge of 2000, or the Team Challenge from the first New York that same year. Keep in mind these numbers are slightly skewed by the unbalanced ridiculousness that was the Team Masters: winning three matches there was worth $20,000 to the individual variety’s $10,000, and a first round loss in teams was worth 50% more than in individual play… and that’s for each team member. ### The Winners

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/pthou02/528.jpg)Over the years, Ben Rubin has been accepted as the king of the Masters. The man won two of the things in the first year of the Masters run, sandwiching a 2-1 performance between the two of them. 12-1 in the Masters. Think about how good that must feel. Almost as good as receiving multiple $20,000 checks I’d imagine. Almost.

Rubin’s numbers are pretty impressive: 12-4 over six events, and average income of over $10,000 from them, and yet, one can’t help but notice that those numbers pale by comparison in all aspects to Ryan Fuller’s.

Now, those who are familiar with the Pro Tour’s personalities will likely point at Fuller’s numbers and scream “CHEATS!” After all, the man has been disqualified from two Pro Tours, served a suspension and hardly carries with him the most sterling of reputations, but think about what he did with those few opportunities he had: 13-3, $62,000 over five events. That’s over $12,000 (better than a Semifinal finish) per tournament. Yes, those numbers were aided by his win in the Team Masters in Tokyo, but that record came against some of the finest players in the world, most of them being experienced enough in tournament play that Fuller’s supposed tactics would definitely be handicapped, and if they lacked that experience, the abnormally high judge:player ratios of the Masters meant there was always someone watching, especially for a guy with a rep like Ryan’s. Of all the players to ever participate, he’ll be the saddest to see the Masters go.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/Worlds2001/699.jpg)Third on the all-time Masters money list was Bob Maher, he of the win-to-finish-things-up in Yokohama. Before Japan, he was still averaging a little more than the average Masters payout of $5,000 for each performance, but the win at the end sent his numbers through the roof. Fuller’s teammate Chris Benafel comes fifth on the list with $45,000, thanks largely to his New Orleans Finals appearance and ABU’s Tokyo win.

Among those with fewer appearances, it’s the members of Venice Champions PS2 who really came out of this thing shining. Katsuhiro Mori, Masahiko Morita and Masahiro Kuroda each played in exactly one Masters and walked away with $20,000 for their trouble. Rough life huh? Elijah Pollock, Steve Wolfman and Dave Rood, whose 2020 were defeated by PS2 in the finals, each came away with $10,000 apiece for their one Masters.

Most of the more explosive numbers on the list belong to those players who had Masters success despite very few appearances. Trey Van Cleave won $10,000 in his one showing, back in the first Masters in New York. Jens Thoren won over $11,000 per Masters over his three tries thanks to his win in Houston. Alex Witt went 5-1 over two Masters for a combined $27,000, while both Jason Zila and Jay Elerar made the Finals in the only Masters they played in respectively.

### The Losers (no offense guys)

It’s tough to look at anyone who made the Masters as a loser in any of this. After all, the least one could walk away with was $2,000 for their troubles, a reward very few have seen in relation to **Magic**. Really, though, with that average payout of $5,000, there have to be two kinds of losers: those who kept coming back without success, and those who made it to a few only to fail to make it past the first round.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptbos02/750.jpg"><br><i>Not a big fan of the Masters Series.</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

Despite being fourth on the Masters money list, you have to think Kai Budde will be happy to see the series go. Long has the Juggernaut moaned privately and publicly about his Masters woes, not going so far as to blame the tournaments themselves, instead just being frustrated with his apparent bad luck in them. Despite Phoenix Foundation’s win in Osaka, Kai’s lifetime record in the Masters is 6-10, which doesn’t leave many wins once the Osaka 3-0 is removed from the equation. Kai managed to lose to the only Masters competitor ever, Alan Shuldiner, to compete without a pro point in his career. He managed to lose to Zila, despite the Californian’s two-year absence from competitive play. Really, he managed in one way or another to lose to just about anyone who was put at his table, from rivals (Maher) to teammates (Patrick Mello). Yes, Kai will still be taking a likely pay cut with the new end of year payout being much more spread out than Masters money, but I think Kai will happily trade the few extra bucks for the opportunity to focus on one event per PT.

Other than Kai, only one player managed to appear in enough Masters to reach double digits, and that was Rob Dougherty. Unfortunately for Rob, his trips there were wildly unsuccessful, resulting in a 5-10 record and an average haul of just $3,250. I grant, that doesn’t sound all that bad, but when the minimum he could win was $22,000 and the maximum $240,000, his $32,500 just doesn’t measure up too well.

Joining Dougherty in the post-Masters celebration are Team Outland. Outland (Eivind Nitter, Nico Herzog and Bjorn Jocumsen), the only team to compete in all three Team Masters, had a combined winnings of $45,500 over fourteen combined appearances, thanks in large part to first round losses in all three Masters. That comes to less than $3,300 per appearance with that $3,000 minimum looming large. 

The record for the most Masters appeared in without a match win is shared by two men, Bram Snepvangers and Dan Clegg. Each player managed to participate four times without advancing beyond the $2000 minimum, with all eight appearances coming in individual play. Both players are said to be ‘very excited’ about the switch to end of year payouts.
