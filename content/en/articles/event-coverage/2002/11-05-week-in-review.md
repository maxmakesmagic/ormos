This is more like a month in review this time. I've fallen behind again, and once again I can only offer apologies and the promise to try to get back on the ball by assigning a much higher priority to my writing. A lot has happened over the last few weeks, and while the **Magic** Invitational fits chronologically in the middle of the time I am covering in this column, it was by far the most interesting event and so it will get the most coverage here.

### The *Magic Online* Invitational

![](https://i.ormos.uk/63/9d/639d641230b9245f414e0724dc0c4b1bdff5ed0323a0d6f0b187dfdb329f929c.jpg)

A year ago rumor had it that this year's Invitational would take place in either Buenos Aires or Rio de Janeiro. Finding out that we were to travel to the suburbs of Seattle instead may have been a letdown, but few of those privileged to be invited could truly be disappointed. The fact that the event would be played online was also new and exciting. Dave Humpherys and Eivind Nitter were the only invitees without much **Magic** Online experience, but even they caught on very quickly. The formats were not quite as interesting as in previous years because of the more limited card pool, but they worked out pretty well anyway.

Most of us got to Seattle on Wednesday, and spent Thursday sightseeing in Seattle. The definite highlight of the day was an hour-long tour of the Wizards of the Coast headquarters. WOTC is located in Renton, Washington (about thirty minutes' drive away from downtown Seattle) in a complex of three two-story buildings. While most of the work space is taken up by conventional cubicles, room is made for fun as well. For example, they have an actual dojo which hosts a variety of martial arts and fitness classes including - of all things - belly dancing. Perhaps the most impressive room of the building was the large R&D Library. It had all the Magic cards (under lock), pretty much every product ever produced by WOTC and plenty of games and books from other publishers. Those may be used as reference material, but more likely are there for when the staff feels like a game of Risk or Settlers of Catan. In addition to board games and books, the R&D library features a ping-pong table. That one is for research purposes only.

In order to play over the course of the weekend, we were given accounts with four of every card added to them. Of course we did not get to keep the cards afterward and the trade option on the account was disabled. Also important was the fact that other users were unable to send private messages to our accounts. Otherwise we'd get bombarded with notes, relevant or not, while playing. 

The tournament began on Friday morning, kicking off with the Two Headed Giant sealed format. We were broken up into groups of four. We played three rounds, teaming up with each of the other three players. The format was a lot more fun than we originally suspected (many of us wrote it off as the lamest of the Invitational formats, but it worked out quite well). Of course I would say that though, I was the only undefeated player after the first three rounds. Still, I think everyone liked the format pretty well.

The next format up was Online Extended - that is old Standard plus *Onslaught*. Psychatog and u-g madness were expected to be all over the format as these decks obviously remain very powerful, even without many additions from *Onslaught*. While some lazier players (myself included) did indeed fall back to our World Championships decks with only minor changes to them, others were far more original. Perhaps the best deck of the format belonged to Team Punisher whose metagame call of playing b-g produced 3-0 and 2-1 results for them. Least successful were Battle of Wits builds which left Ruel and Finkel without any wins.

The Auction of the People saw some very heavy bidding for the better decks. Ruel went all the way down to four cards to get the best deck ("O"), though few others agreed with the wisdom of his strategy. Most decks ended up selling for five or six cards with only a few going at seven.

Rochester Draft was certainly the most important event for those spectators who were hoping to improve their game by watching the pros. Not only did they get to see how pros value the cards and what picks they would make in each situation, they also had full access to replay the draft in the future and watch the games to find out how well each strategy did.

Finally, a special treat for all those who had no idea what to play in State Championships, came the New Standard. The results of this format were not quite what most folks expexted. After going 0-3 with Battle of Wits in Extended, Finkel managed a 3-0 in the New Standard. Four players managed 0-3 records with different decks also, leaving even more confusion in the wake of those three rounds.

![](https://i.ormos.uk/e4/12/e4121d41442f1c81719c0a4fce8a3d79f2be24af6275245a5e36a885fba99f56.jpg)

When smoke cleared, it came down to Team Punisher's Tomi Walamies and Jens Thoren battling it out in the finals. The Finals would be held across the three Constructed formats. It began with Thoren defeating Walamies in Online Extended. Walamies rallied in Standard, bringing the score up 1-1. It was all Thoren in the last format however, as Thoren's "V" deck at five cards and fifteen life crushed Walamies's "G" deck at five cards twenty life. 

Thoren's card is not as exciting as some of the others submitted this time around but remember this - no Invitational card has seen print exactly as submitted at the event. I am sure that by the time the next standalone comes out, we are going to see an interesting and powerful card released with Jens's face on it.

### The Grand Prix Circuit

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/GPDET01/sf2d.jpg)

Bob Maher was the only American pro to cross the pond and attend Grand Prix-Copenhagen. Having been suspended for six months, Maher was anxious to get some pro points under his belt and the weekend was no disappointment. Maher took home the gold, defeating an incredibly strong Top 8 field that featured Budde, Baberowski, and Thoren. At the same time, Rei Hashimoto won Grand Prix-Utsanomiya - an event in Japan attended by no players from outside the country. 

Jeff Cunningham continued the fine tradition of Canadian players winning American Grand Prix. He made it into the Top 8 of GP-Philly that had few other name players, leaving behind Top 16 marked by names such as Maher, Gab Tsang and Gary Wise.

With one Pro Tour and seven Grand Prix behind it, it is no surprise that Phoenix Foundation - winners of PT-Boston - are leading in the standings. Budde is ahead of teammate Dirk Baberowski by a single point - which comes from him finishing third in Copenhagen while Baberowski was eliminated only one round previously, in the Top 8. Not having picked up any more pro points from, or far as I know, attended, any Grand Prix this season, Blume stands at 24 points - which is still three points ahead of Bob Maher, who is clearly the most successful player overall this season. Maher followed up his Top 4 finish in Boston with a GP win in Copenhagen and a Top 16 finish in Philadelphia. David Rood is the only other player to break out of the 'teens in Pro standings at this time, at exactly twenty points. 

With so many points on the line, PT-Houston is going to alter the standings significantly. However, all players who are currently above the twenty point mark are likely to remain in the top 10 or very close under it.

### Bad Play of the Week

Courtesy of Gaby van Dinteren:

"I was watching a game in the new Extended format, *Onslaught* included, in a local tournament in Barcelona. A "Rock" player was down to one card (Chainer's Edict) and had no threats on the table after Wrath of God swept the board. His opponent, playing u-w control, was down to 1 life and 6 cards, including a Forbid and a Squee, Goblin Nabob.

The Rock player had Volrath's Stronghold in play and used its ability to put Spiritmonger from his graveyard on top of his library. He proceeded to play the Spiritmonger, which was Forbidden with buyback, discarding Squee and another card. The u-w player kept drawing useless cards, but could use all these cards to discard together with Squee to buyback his Forbid, as his opponent was not changing tactics and continued putting Spiritmonger on top of his library, drawing it and playing it. The u-w player was not close to being decked, so it was just a matter of time before he would draw one of his win conditions (Morphling, Exalted Angel) and win the game against his opponent's ignorant strategy.

However, the u-w player demonstrated that he did not understand the weakness of his opponent's play, because he drew a Wasteland, played it and destroyed the Volrath's Stronghold! The Rock player was disappointed that his trick no longer functioned. He proceeded to draw a Treetop Village and took the game with it. 

The moral of this story is, if your opponent is playing badly, make sure you let him continue to do so.
