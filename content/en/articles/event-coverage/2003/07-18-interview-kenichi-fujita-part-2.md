**Sideboard**: Does it frustrate you at all that despite being a veteran and a two-time Grand Prix Champion that American and European players aren't as familiar with you as they might be with say, Tsuyoshi Fujita or Katsuhiro Mori?

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/natjp03/a982.jpg)

**Fujita**: It doesn't really bother me at all. For one thing, I have absolutely no interest in the whole fame thing. I could care less what people think about me, or if some random European **Magic** player knows who I am. So what if I won two Grand Prix? They were Limited, and they were in Asia, so of course Americans and Europeans aren't going to know about them. Even if they do, they're going to say, "Okay, so he can draft. He got lucky, he got some good cards. No big deal". It doesn't really bother me at all.

**Sideboard**: Do you find that you're more respected in Japan?

**Fujita**: I don't really think that I'm particularly respected. I don't even know why Japanese players should care who I am.

**Sideboard**: Well, you've been more consistent than Tsuyoshi Fujita or Katsuhiro Mori over a longer period of time. Don't you think you should be considered on their level?

**Fujita**: No. When it all comes down to it, if you're not in first place, if you don't win, it doesn't matter. It's all the same. 

**Sideboard**: Do you feel that the reason you might not be perceived as being on their level is because you've never made the Top 8 of a Pro Tour? Is that essential to being recognized as one of the top players in the world?

**Fujita**: Yes, definitely. I've won two Grand Prix, which is all well and good, but there's a Grand Prix every week somewhere in the world. It's not a big deal, lots of people win a Grand Prix. Some people even win two. If you want to be recognized as a contender, you *have* to make Top 8 at a Pro Tour. 

**Sideboard**: Do you see that as your major goal right now, and were you disappointed that you came one match away from being the first Japanese player to make a Top 8 at Pro Tour-Los Angeles in 2000?

**Fujita**: I don't really think of myself as a pro player. I come to these tournaments and I play. From an average person's perspective, I go to the Pro Tour a lot, so that must make me a pro player. But, I don't think of myself as a pro. There are a lot more people in Japan who should be given that title over me. Of course, everyone wants to win, but I don't come to win. Even today, it's great that I was qualified for Nationals, but I just came to play.

**Sideboard**: So winning isn't as important as just playing and having a good time?

**Fujita**: Playing the game and having fun is the first priority. Winning is second, or lower to me. I was invited to Nationals, and that's great, because it was another opportunity to hang out with my friends and play some **Magic**.

**Sideboard**: With that being your philosophy, does it make coming in ninth today easier to handle? Other players who want to win very badly wouldn't take that too well.

**Fujita**: Sure, it would have been nice to get into the Top 8. It would have been exciting and everything, but it's something I can tell people over a beer and we can have a laugh over it. "Yeah, I could have been in Top 8, but I suck. I came in ninth place!" Life goes on.

**Sideboard**: You have two columns for Hobby Japan's magazine, one of which is for beginning and intermediate players. How important do you feel articles like that are to helping the **Magic** community? Does your column do a good job of that?

**Fujita**: I don't really have a philosophy when I write these columns, and I don't think about it. It's more just like Hobby Japan's editorial staff came to me and said, "Hey, we have this great idea for a column, and we thought you'd be the best person to write it." It would be great if someone who is just discovering **Magic** could read that column and become more interested because of it, or could gain a deeper understanding of the game. If that happens, then I'm happy. 

**Sideboard**: Your other column presents draft situations, and then has readers mail in their choices for picks, and has the pros chime in as well. What is it like to do that column, and how do you feel about Sideboard Online recently doing a similar column?

**Fujita**: I think it was a case of parallel evolution. I didn't know that Limited Information was actually inspired by my "What Would You Pick?" column. In all actuality, I stole the idea from a Mahjong magazine, where they show the same kind of situations. "Here's the board, here's what you have in your hand, what would you throw away and what would you take?" I can't really say anything about Sideboard "stealing" my idea, because I stole it from someone else as well.

**Sideboard**: Since the beginning of the Pro Tour, Europe was always a strong force, and even South America has seemed further ahead of Japan at times. Why do you think it took so long for Japanese players to reach that level?

**Fujita**: English. The language barrier.

**Sideboard**: Was it a case of the European and American players having The Dojo in the late ‘90's, while Japanese players didn't?

**Fujita**: Well, The Dojo was mostly decklists, so if you put a little effort into it, you could read some of the reports and descriptions. Anyone could understand the decklists. The biggest barrier was just that if you went to a Pro Tour, you couldn't talk to anyone. You can't communicate in English, so you lag behind. People don't talk to you, you're afraid to talk to them, so you're just not getting involved in that network.

**Sideboard**: Do you feel that Japan's recent success might have something to do with more socializing within the **Magic** community?

**Fujita**: The better options you have, the better chances you have to win. That goes for any game. The person who has the most information is in a superior position, and that applies to anything, really. Of course, talking and hanging out with Pro Tour players is a big factor, but also Japanese players have started to share information among themselves more. They share information with foreign players, and there are more translations of magazine and web articles. There's been an explosion in information sharing.

**Sideboard**: Why did it take so long to happen?

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gpnagoya02/a948.jpg)

**Fujita**: It's because we were too fragmented. It took us that long to get our own act together, and start exchanging information among *ourselves*. If we're not communicating with each other, we certainly weren't going to go talk to American and Swedish players. Once we started sharing information among ourselves and realized the benefits that would come from it, then we started to branch out.

**Sideboard**: Do you think the fragmentation was a cultural issue?

**Fujita**: You could argue that the Japanese have a social tendency to look inward. There was a period in our history where we closed our borders to foreign countries. There could be a tendency for that. In the beginning, it was the city you were living in, and then the region. The big division was Kanto versus Kansai. Kanto is the eastern area around Tokyo, and Kansai is the western area around Osaka. People were happy to just be the big Kanto player, or the big Kansai player. They weren't looking to make connections with other communities. So, that certainly didn't help. It took people a while to start interacting with other groups. 

**Sideboard**: Now, at least from my perspective, it seems that attitude has changed. It seems that a Japanese player would say, "I don't just want to be one of the best here, but I want to be one of the best in the world." At what point did that change happen, and why?

**Fujita**: I don't really remember when it happened, but basically it was after the Japanese put aside our differences as it were, and started to think of us as one community. Itaru Ishida has been on the Pro Tour for a long time, so it was slow at first with guys like him. Then it turned into a trickle, then a stream, and it's on its way to become a river. One or two people go abroad and do kind of well, then come back with lots of tech and information. The next time, maybe a few more people go abroad. It becomes a momentum thing where it keeps building, and it helped bring the Japanese community together because these people would go out and start sharing the information they got overseas. That raised everyone's play level a little bit more, and then they started to realize the benefits of exchanging information. 

**Sideboard**: And so that caused the goals to change?

**Fujita**: Right. Once Japan became unified, people started to say, "Well, okay. We've realized now that there's a bigger world out there." Ishida goes abroad and does well, then Nakamura goes and becomes famous, and plays in the Invitational. So this makes Japanese players more interested in other countries that are playing the game as well. And they realize, "Hey we can get there too. Our guy is in the Invitational; our guy is playing in the Pro Tour". That generates more interest, and that momentum keeps building. Then you get Tsuyoshi Fujita who breaks into the Top 8, it creates a critical momentum, and it just keeps building. Next year, we'll probably see more Japanese players doing consistently better on the Pro Tour.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/JPNAT01/233F.jpg)

**Sideboard**: As someone who has been around for a very long time, what are your thoughts on the state of Japanese **Magic**?

**Fujita**: That's kind of a hard question to answer. What do I think about **Magic** now in Japan? Well, it's a lot better than it was eight years ago, even two years ago. Lately, it seems that the number of players is getting smaller, and not as many people are playing as they used to. But, starting with Fujita, we're starting to get people into the Top 8. At Yokohama, we had two players there so it's a good feeling to know we've arrived. It's been a long, bumpy road, but we made it. It's not unusual for a Japanese player to make Top 8 of a Pro Tour anymore. That's a good thing.

**Sideboard**: What has to happen for Japanese **Magic** to continue growing?

**Fujita**: I can't really say, because that gets more into Hobby Japan and Wizards of the Coast territory. There are limits to what players can do. What really needs to happen is for stores to do more than just sell the product by putting it on their shelves. They need to actually *sell the product*. They need to try and create a community and get people interested in the game. 

**Sideboard**: I know that most players outside Japan aren't familiar with the Hobby Japan/Wizards relationship, and that was the first thing you mentioned when it comes to growth. What does that have to do with it?

**Fujita**: Probably the best thing Hobby Japan or Wizards can do to increase the number of players and make **Magic** bigger is that the price of packs has to drop lower than it is now. There's already been one price reduction this year, from 500 Yen to 420 Yen. It needs to go lower. That's the only way to reach more players. At the current exchange rate, 420 Yen works out to be about four dollars. That's what most stores sell it for. On the internet, people can buy cards from the states and have them shipped over here for a lot cheaper, but that money doesn't go into the Japanese distributor's pocket. So, it hurts things all around.

**Sideboard**: What is the future of Japanese **Magic**?

**Fujita**: Well, it would be nice to have a Pro Tour Champion by next year's Nationals. We've had two Finalists, so the next thing is to get a Champion. Hopefully that will happen in the next year.

**Sideboard**: What's in your future?

**Fujita**: No change, I'm just going to still be playing and having fun. It would be nice if I could have fun *and* win.
