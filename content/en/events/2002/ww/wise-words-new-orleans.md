It seems to be a well known fact that mine is a loud voice, so when I return from a Pro Tour without it, only able to express myself in words on a sheet of paper or computer screen, I've probably had a good time. With regards to New Orleans, this is an understatement.

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptnor01/a968.jpg)With all of the amazing places I've been, none have been as much fun or all-consuming as New Orleans. This American city unlike any other American city is obvious for the differences from any other metropolis in the world. Renowned for its love of saints, it is ironic then that (as many pro players discovered) it's become a den for good, harmless sin, merriment and good times. In fact, it seems that with its French-American flavor, New Orleans has almost nothing in common with any other city the Pro Tour has visited as of late except that it was tamed by the same man: Kai Budde. 

That's right, once again, the German Juggernaut has prevailed against all odds, showing the world yet again that this game is far from the luck-based punishment some constantly mana-screwed players would have you believe it to be. Am I saying luck isn't a factor? Of course not, but I watched Kai play for three weeks leading up to Worlds this year and sat in awe of his talent. Sure, the ball has bounced his way on more than one occasion, but he's been good enough to get to that moment where luck could strike.

Case in point: As the reporter charged with reporting the final match at New Orleans, I was sitting to Kai's right, watching every draw as it happened. In the final game, Tomi Walamies (who, by the way, has long been deserving of the recognition he'll finally get from his Top 8 finish. Same goes for Jelger Wiegersma) had a Call of the Herd token beating down as Kai gained massive card advantage. Tomi, with nothing else to do, flashed the Herd back in the hopes of putting Kai on a clock, and appeared to have done so successfully as Kai drew six lands and one Stroke of Genius off his final two Accumulated Knowledges. Tomi attacked, taking Kai to four life, and the German Juggernaut, with seven lands and no business spells in hand aside from one Counterspell, suddenly needed an amazing top deck, which he got in the form of Morphling.

Now yes, the Morph came at the perfect moment, but it was the Knowledges that got him to that point in the first place. Mathematically, he obviously should have drawn something of substance in those seven cards, so when he didn't, he was kind of due, but give the man credit: he knew to play his own deck instead of trying to play against Walamies's, holding out until the last possible moment, and it paid off in a win, perhaps the closest he's managed during his remarkable Sunday streak.

14-0. Is everyone starting to realize just how remarkable and ridiculous that really is? I mean, only once in Pro Tour history has a player gone 14-0 in the Swiss portion of a Pro Tour, and that player, Ryan Fuller, did so with the benefit of playing against Pro Tour rookies and upstarts. Think about how good Kai's opponents have been in the formats in question. The following is a list of those players along with their performances in the formats in question up to their matches with Kai:

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTNOR01/650.jpg)So combined, these guys were winning better than three quarters of their matches, drawing more than a third of the others. By my count, these fourteen competitors have between them 30 final day appearances, five Grand Prix wins, one Junior Super Series championship, eight Invitational appearances, three National Championships and over a thousand Pro Tour Points, and none of them could stop Kai. Oh, and by the way, with the exception of Kamiel, whose only follow up was at the tournament following his loss to Kai, none of them have made it back to Sunday since. When Kai beats you, he really beats you.

After New York, both Randy Buehler and I stated publicly that while Kai was the best player in the world, Jon Finkel was the greatest of all time, with his longevity and consistency garnering both our votes. After New York, Randy was asked what Kai had to do in order to pass Jon. His answer: "Win New Orleans". If you ask me to cast my vote now, I won't have any problem with putting my checkmark next to Kai's name. He's the best there ever was.

A few other notes...

*   Ben Klauser made his fourth Sunday with his eighth place finish, putting him among the game's elite. Only Jon Finkel, Kai Budde, Darwin Kastle, Olle Rade, Ben Rubin, Scott Johns, Alan Comer and Mark Justice before him have reached that elite circle four times. Klauser's arrival at this level may be the quietest yet, with no final appearances (two quarterfinal eliminations, two semifinal eliminations) and no Invitational appearances to show for it. This latest Top 8 is his third since Worlds 2000 at Belgium, where he finished third, making three in the last nine Pro Tours. Not too shabby for Austria's top player.
*   Dave Humphreys, the king of ninth place finishes and long considered New England's best player by its residents, managed to make his second individual Top 8 finish, but he had to take a tough step to do it. Playing teammate Rob Dougherty in the final round of the swiss, Dave needed a win against the YMG owner, who needed a draw to make his third individual Top 8 (the two players teamed with Darwin Kastle to win the first Teams Pro Tour). The two ended up playing one of the hardest fought matches of the tournament (prompting a watching Chris Pikula to exclaim "these guys really know how to play their deck") with Humphreys winning one game to none. The bitter irony here is that Dave knocked Rob out of his first Top 8, at 1999 PT-NY, and that both times, Humphreys won the mirror match using a deck of Rob's design.
*   While Kai's streak remained intact, Kamiel Cornelissen's was finally broken. Kamiel, who first made his way onto the Pro Tour for 2000 PT-NY (the Rebel PT), played in every PT since, making Top 32 in every one of them. Going into the final round, Kamiel sat in 44th needing a win to make Top 32. Unfortunately for him though, he lost to Trey Van Cleave right before the Dane's disqualification for an attempted bribe of American Mike Sigrist. The loss dropped Kamiel to 57th, where a win would have put him in the mid to high twenties. The biggest benefactor for the DQ was Chris Pikula, who was previously wanting for an invite to PT San Diego. With Trey's removal from the standings, Pikula moved from 33rd to 32nd, getting the invite, a Pro Tour point and a few hundred dollars for his trouble.
*   ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/MASTERSNOR01/679.jpg)Meanwhile, Mike Pustilnik made a little history on Sunday, becoming the first player to win both a Pro Tour and a Masters (That's right, Kai hasn't won one of these yet), and you can throw a Grand Prix in for good measure. Pustilnik drafted a very strong red-green-white beatdown deck featuring four Wild Mongrels, and while many players disagreed with Mike's construction (of major concern was his decision to splash white for Aven Cloudchaser and Shelter and to play the nine casting cost Volley of Boulders while his sideboard included Barbarian Lunatic) it apparently worked, with the good doctor disposing of Chris Benafel's red-black deck in the finals. Benafel who may well be the most entertaining Top 8 competitor in the game, definitely knew he was in front of the camera, putting on a performance was enjoyed by all, especially Mikey, who giggled every time Chris moaned about how badly he was getting thrashed by the Mongrels or Roar of the Wurm.

    While the tournament was a disappointing one for me, I do have one anecdote I promised to share. Playing against California's Chris DiMaci in the fourth round and playing first, I felt obliged to keep a six card opening hand consisting of Land Grant, Brainstorm, Impulse, Duress, Swords to Plowshares and Wall of Blossoms. Casting Land Grant, I could only cringe when Chris stopped me from picking up my library, informing me he was Force of Willing my only mana source. Here's how the rest of the game played out:

    **Chris:** Island, go
    **Me:** go
    **Chris:** Island, go
    **Me:** go
    **Chris:** Island, Ophidian, go.
    **Me:**Land Grant for Tropical Island, play Tropical Island, Brainstorm into one more land, go.
    **Chris:** Attack, draw, island, go
    **Me:** Land, Wall of Blossoms, draw, go
    **Chris:** Island, go
    **Me:**Wall of Blossoms (still no third land), draw Force of Will, go.
    **Chris:**Perish

    Now, at this point I felt obliged to call a judge as this was game one, but it turned out that Chris was playing a black-blue Finkel deck with four Tutors and a number of 1-per hate cards like Perish and Massacre. Unbelievably, I needed to keep the Walls around to stop Ophidian, so I Force of Willed with no response.

    **Me:** Land, Shadowmage Infiltrator, go

    And around eight turns later, Chris conceded. That game explains why I won't give up in tournament play as long as time isn't an issue.

*   With team affiliations being so prevalent of late, the winners of Pro Tours have started being judged not just by individual performance, but also by the accomplishments of entire squads of players. For example, Zvi Mowshowitz won PT-Tokyo, but with four players making Top 8, ABU had every right to claim superiority in that tournament. This time around, there were three teams that could claim the win.

    ![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/PTNOR01/714.jpg)Most obvious amongst these was Godzilla, with Kai's win being nicely complimented by Ben Klauser's Top 8 finish. Of course, with so many players and accomplishments on their resume, Godzilla is almost expected to put up these kind of numbers, so the tournament wasn't a wild success by comparison to other recent PTs for the super team.

    At the same time, New Orleans marked a huge comeback for the team many were leaving for dead: Your Move Games. Playing Rob Dougherty's Reanimator deck, Benzo, YMG placed two members in the Top 8, while Dougherty and team associate Tom Guevin finished thirteenth and twelfth respectively. YMG hadn't placed a member in a Constructed Top 8 since Dougherty's quarterfinal appearance in Chicago a year ago.

    The third team with a claim to stake here would have to be Punisher. With Walamies and Anton Jonsson making Top 8, while Johan Sodeghpour co-won the Masters Gateway (with Itaru Ishida), the team, which also includes established PT veterans Mattias Jorstedt, Mattias Kettil, Jens Thoren and Thomas Rosholm, started making headway at Euros this year where Walamies and Thoren made Top 8, but this is their first really big Pro Tour splash. Interestingly, the teams couldn't find any one deck they liked and each made different choices as a result, but in the end, they performed as well as anyone, with Walamies finishing first in the Swiss and the little-known Jonsson almost ousting Dave Humphreys in a tough five-game quarterfinal loss. Call this one a three-way tie.

One last thing before I sign off: In an article I read just this very morning (as I write this, not post it), John Rizzo referred to me as the person in history he'd most like to fight. To his tangent-ridden posterior, I bellow a harrowing BRING IT ON. Hey, if I can inspire a few more of his rants and ravings, that's good times for all. Should be fun.

That's it for this week. Next time around, I'll be taking a look at the profound effect *Odyssey* had on the Extended environment seen in New Orleans. Should be educational, not to mention interesting to see if I know the first thing about constructed. Hey, one should never rule out personal growth, am I right?
