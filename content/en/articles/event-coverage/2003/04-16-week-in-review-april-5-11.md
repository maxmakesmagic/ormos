### Grand Prix-Kyoto

![](https://i.ormos.uk/71/d2/71d2e930db6dd56e55491d43e7c2000f13c4378a9f30b79738a011651edf1498.jpg)This should have been the biggest Grand Prix of all time. A tournament held in Japan that is feeding a Pro Tour in Japan, located in a heavily populated area of Kyoto. It was quite a surprise to me that under 700 players showed up. A very solid performance for a GP anywhere in the world – even Japan – to be sure, but far from what I’d expected.

What did meet my expectations was the lack of international participation. Most Asian pros from outside of Japan had Grand Prix-Singapore keeping them busy that same weekend. Others may not have wanted to travel amid the SARS scare and the war. In either case, the only prominent pro from outside of the country to attend the tournament was Olivier Ruel, who made Day 2 but failed to finish in the money.

Top 8 had a solid mix of well-known competitors (including Satoshi Nakamura and Itaru Ishida), rising stars such as Rookie of the Year race leader Masashi Ooiso, and first-time Top 8’ers like Yoshiaki Toshiro. There were no amateur players in Top 8. The top amateur finished tenth in the tournament.

The champion title went to Akira Asahara. A virtual unknown to world community, he is a popular deck builder who is reasonably well known in Japan. This is Asahara’s first major success as a player, and he will have a chance to prove himself again in Yokohama next month.

### Grand Prix-Singapore

![](https://i.ormos.uk/e7/b3/e7b32964daffeed4759b8eac034b4f2f25879801b16ec43618d809ee69c1bf92.jpg)This is the third time the Grand Prix circuit returns to Singapore. While Japanese players did not travel to Singapore as they had a Grand Prix of their own to attend, it seemed like the rest of the world showed up, and they got a Top 8 with players from three continents of four represented at the tournament to prove it.

It was a much smaller tournament than Kyoto, at 231 players. Beside the Top 8 finishers, it featured pros such as Albertus Law, Peter Szigeti, Ben Seck and Rob Nadebaum. The Top 8 included two of the four Americans in attendance – Gary Talim and Antonino de Rosa. Local players weren’t shamed, as they earned three slots in the Top 8 – the original GP-Singapore champion Sam Lau, GP-Singapore #2 finalist Kelvin Hoon and Kuala Lumpur finalist Chang Chua. As is the case with Akira Asahara, these are players who may be below the international community’s radar, but they are very well known in their region.

It was Sweden’s Mikael Polgary who claimed the title in the end, propelled to Top 8 with an undefeated Day 1 performance. This is Polgary’s first career Top 8. In fact, he is in the Rookie of the Year race, having earned only four pro points prior to this GP. He is also the first European player to win an Asian Grand Prix. 

### Player of the Year Race Update

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/ptven03/935.jpg"><br><i>The Germans and Americans dominate the Race.</i></td></tr></tbody></table>

Surprising no one, Kai Budde is once again in significant lead in the race. He is currently at sixty nine points, while second place is held by teammate Dirk Baberwoski at only forty eight points – a Pro Tour second place finish behind Kai. Both Baberowski and Budde jump-started their season with a team Pro Tour win and have continued to earn steady points since. Their third teammate Marco Blume is currently in ninth place with thirty three points.

Trailing Baberowski by a single point is Osyp Lebedowicz, fresh off his PT-Venice victory. Just one point behind him is Houston’s champion Justin Gary. In fifth place at forty two points is Bob Maher – the top player in the standings without a PT win this season. Maher started the season off with back-to-back Top 8 finishes and some excellent Grand Prix results. He slowed down a bit later in the season, but his position remains very strong.

This may be the first time in years, if not ever, that two countries are completely dominating the top of the standings. There are six Americans and three Germans in the current top 10. PT-Chicago finalist Nicolai Herzog of Norway is the only player from outside one of these two countries near the top of the standings. Of course, a number of European players are just a few short points behind.

With only two Pro Tours and very few GP remaining in the season, we are now starting to get a pretty accurate picture of who are the likely candidates for the next year’s **Magic** Invitational. Mark Rosewater confirmed in Venice that the Invitational would once again take place on ***Magic** Online*. At the minimum, the top six players on the pro standings will be invited.

### New Magic Magazine in Northern Europe

A new **Magic** magazine called Mana Pool is being launched in Denmark. With articles in Danish, Swedish and Norwegian, it will serve all the Scandinavian countries. Among potential contributors are some well-known pros such as Sigurd Eskeland and Eivind Nitter, according to editor Alexander Dick.

A web-based portion of the magazine will use a model similar to Brainburst – where premium content will be available to paid subscribers only. There will also be a quarterly printed magazine, which will retail for approximately $3. There is no English content on the site, but if you speak any of the above-mentioned languages, check them out at www.manapool.dk

*“I was playing in a local Extended tournament, and after I had finished my match, I went over to watch a very interesting game between two very experienced players, playing questionable decks. One was playing a goblin combo deck with Goblin Lackeys, Goblin Recruiters and Goblin Ringleaders, while the other was playing a red-blue control deck. They were both 3-0. It was game two, the first having gone to the red-blue player and there was plenty of time left. Late in the game, after the red-blue player had established control, the goblin player shrugged and cast his fourth Goblin Recruiter. The red blue player looked at his hand full of counters, Rages, and one other interesting card, and let the Goblin Recruiter resolve. Excited, the goblin player stacked his deck with 8 goblins, hoping to get off his combo with relative ease. The red-blue player untapped with a smile on his face, however, and added 8 mana to his mana pool. He returned two islands to his hands to Gush and proceeded to cast Obliterate, a questionable card against the speedy goblin deck. He buried his cards and played one of his islands for the turn. The goblin player, confused, untapped, and drew his card: goblin ringleader. He frowned as he remembered the 7 goblins atop his deck, and died to a Frenetic Efreet before he ever found another mountain.“*

*“In a Magic Online draft, I had a Gustcloak Skirmisher, a morphed Willbender and a Mistform Dreamer in play. My opponent had Festering Goblin, Goblin Turncoat, random morph and Goblin Grappler. He cast Crown of Fury on his Grappler, I thought for a while before letting it resolve. He attacked with the Turncoat and Grappler and provoked my Skirmisher. I blocked there and then had my morphed Willbender block the Turncoat. As I had hoped, he sacrificed the Festering Goblin to the Turncoat to regenerate that and give my Skirmisher -1/-1. I Willbent the -1/-1 back to his Turncoat, thus resulting in me getting a 4-for-0 and eliminating all but his lone remaining morph. Needless to say, I won that game.”*
