* * *

Lorwyn is fast approaching, and with it will come not just [planeswalkers](https://web.archive.org/web/20080429041229/http://www.wizards.com/default.asp?x=magic/planeswalkers/home) busting out of their card frames but, doubtless, new archetypes for Standard... But before then, we still have the summer 2007 international Championships to complete!

This past weekend showcased two high-profile international tournaments, [Germany](/en/events/2007/gernat07/veteran-stages-triumphant-return) and [Japan](/en/events/2007/jpnat07/champs-fall-rookies-rise-in-japan), homes to former winners—and *World* Champions—like Kai Budde and Katsuhiro Mori, as well as numerous other decorated winners, from two-time PT Champion Marco Blume to the genius's genius, Tsuyoshi Fujita (no pressure, new guys!). Just as National Championships around the world rage on, the Tier Two metagame shows no sign of letting up its martinet-like enforcement of deck diversity. Formats like Standard, where there are a lot of different playable cards at very similar power levels, allow for greater flexibility of deck choice than we have seen in most previous Constructed environments; even when you pick the "wrong" deck in Standard (or even Extended) the difference between the best deck in the format and the worst of the real contenders is pretty narrow. The metagame is therefore hard to predict, and awesome deck designers can push through uniquely effective weapons, even if they are effective only for one week or one tournament.

When I first started to look at these deck lists, I thought they might be identical (on the lists I have, they both began with two copies of Golgari Rot Farm); they're not. While they try to do the same things, and actually have a lot of functional similarities, they have subtle differences. Naoki plays a single Pendelhaven to go with his Ravenous Rats. He's also got Slaughter Pact where the Champ has Terror main deck. Kitayama has fewer creatures there... but also has two copies of Damnation main!

These decks are updates to the Rat / Rack decks that have been played for some months, since the printing of *Time Spiral*. They try to empty the opponent's hand with Funeral Charms and Stupors; they can steal creatures and nip at mana with Smallpox while destroying the opponent's hand. Disruption is disruption, and effective when you are trading one-for-one, and certainly when you are getting two-for-one... but in these decks the major internal synergy is with The Rack. That card is what makes people want to play discard in Standard. It can be a fast kill that is hard to escape.

The innovation, and one that we will see across Extended, probably Legacy, and certainly Standard next year, is the inclusion of Tarmogoyf in the deck. Modern mana options make splashing Tarmogoyf very simple, and the world's most popular Lhurgoyf makes for a fine addition. One of the biggest criticisms decks based on The Rack have received this year is that their creatures are laughable. Noted aggressive deck designer Pat Sullivan doesn't like Call of the Herd in general, but considers—or at least *considered—*it a fine sideboard card against The Rack, which usually tries to play an attrition game. However, Tarmogoyf is a great all around two-drop, one of the best ever, and shores up that one glaring weakness in The Rack's strategy. With Tarmogoyf, this deck can win quickly—even more quickly—once it starts taking away the opponent's options. 

The various sorts of discard spells are all synergistic with Tarmogoyf. Stupor is a sorcery; Funeral Charm is an instant. Either or both put some other kind of card in the graveyard. Smallpox is a sorcery, and counts for a land, some other card, and usually a creature, too. All of this makes for a happy Tarmogoyf.

One of the things I like about Kitayama's deck is the inclusion of Darkblast in the sideboard. I have been working on Extended this week, and you always see Darkblast in the strangest places. In this deck it is quite clever... Can you think of a trickier or more consistently robust Tarmogoyf-on-Tarmogoyf option? Yours wins!

Solar Flare is... Solar Flare. The deck has not changed substantially in the last year or more since it rocked the world and dominated U.S. Nationals, putting Luis Scott-Vargas and Paul Cheon on center stage for the first time. Solar Flare can be considered the quintessential mid-range control deck for Standard. It does basically everything, some things quite well. 

Solar Flare can...

*   Draw cards with Aeon Chronicler, Court Hussar, and Compulsive Research (and, being a highly customizable mid-range deck, everything from Sift to Tidings)
*   Deal with creatures, lots of creatures, with Condemn, Wrath of God, Damnation, Mortify, Faith's Fetters... (or Terror, Slaughter Pact, or anything, really)
*   Counter spells, though not particularly well, using Remand and now Pact of Negation

Solar Flare is a take-on-all-comers style of deck, using its permission largely for tempo, game preservation, or to force down threats. Because it defends from so many angles, Solar Flare is often considered a premier deck choice for undefined metagames; at the same time, the strategy is often vulnerable to highly dedicated opponents... Last year Solar Flare was generally considered a dog to land-destroying Vore or the combo deck Heartbeat, for instance. It is generally considered a superb anti-creature deck, though its reliance on *Ravnica* lands and signet mana production is often exposed by cards like Cryoclasm and Shattering Spree.

This deck has two distinct plans: One is Teferi control. He has sixteen counters, more or less; he can counter most or all of the key threats in most opponents' decks, and lock the game up with Teferi, Mage of Zhalfir. The proactive plans are quite obvious, but the defensive end intrigues me... Last Gasp? Patrick Chapin credits YT with the Last Gasps—and Volcanic Hammers—in his Korlash control deck, but when I made those suggestions, the scary turn-two beaters in the metagame were Scab-Clan Mauler and sometimes Watchwolf... The thing that actually concerns me if you are looking to try this deck is that it has no card drawing at all. It has been up for debate since Yokahama in Tsuyoshi Fujita's blue deck—well, even before that, when Pat posted his Blue-Red-White Flag Burner deck for U.S. States—whether Ancestral Vision was the right call for *any* deck, or if Think Twice was better. This is quite a curious implementation of the Angelfire strategy. The German winner seems to have hybridized the two main branches of the archetype into a single deck. Rosner has lighter permission than ever, with only two Spell Snares and no Remands, and plays the same number of Firemane Angels and Demonfires!

The Firemane decks usually make a claim for Karoos on the draw or additional Compulsive Research synergy, but the Blue-Red-White decks in general take their market share with card quality. All their cards are awesome. Lightning Angel, awesome. Lightning Helix, awesome. Wrath of God, really awesome. Conventional wisdom has Blue-Red-White as the best anti-creature deck you can play... This deck is quite similar to the one Luis Scott-Vargas used to win the U.S. National Championship recently. It has a significantly more complete counterspell base than many blue-green-white decks, employing Rune Snag for more control. It is very much a bullet deck, going for specific weapons for specific situations. Willbender can counter—functionally, and *really*—a hellbent Demonfire. Brine Elemental plays nicely with Vesuvan Shapeshifter... or might just block a Tarmogoyf. Crovax, Ascendent Hero is just a man among mice. 

Court Hussar is in an interesting position in this deck. It is not the kind of card you really want to Chord up, though you can. You end up looking at some cards and throwing the Hussar away... That isn't always bad, but it isn't really what you are looking for from either Chord or Hussar in most cases. However by himself, Court Hussar is more than able to perform here: He can hold off most or all of the modern Gruul beaters (save Tarmogoyf, of course), and really makes Giant Solifuge look silly. Speaking of Tarmogoyf, Gräfensteiner has recruited a four set of his own. It's like my old buddy and current **Magic Online** Brand Manager Worth Wollpert taught me the day I won my first PTQ: "If you can't beat 'em..." You know the rest.

**Blue-White Martyr**

Martyr of Sands was, not that long ago, a firmly cemented cornerstone of the Standard metagame. Martyr was in the Top 8 of the World Championships, and many pundits had future Hall of Famer Gabriel Nassif going all the way with his UrzaTron build. Black-White Orzhov aggressive decks ran Martyr... just because it was a good drop in their curves (it helped to race attack decks, it cost Dragon storm a Dragon or a storm copy, bought a turn, &c.), and beat down like a white Mogg Fanatic. However the printing of Extirpate and Damnation took a lot of wind out of the Martyr's sails, and we haven't seen the once-dominant strategy since *Planar Chaos*.

Extirpate has functioned so far about how I thought it would. It was hyped early, scared off Martyr of Sands and Firemane Angel in Standard, but ended up pretty much just playing a bullet in the Teachings decks with little application out of the one archetype, rarely achieving main deck status (except for the small window when dredge was the deck du jour in Standard; then Extirpate followed Tormod's Crypt in sideboard graveyard-hating popularity). For sure Extirpate was and remains a high role-player, but it is not a bomb on the order of a, say, Damnation. However *both* cards *together* in the format have really warped the color choices for control decks. The existence of Damnation, breaking Wrath of God's monopoly on the best card in the format, has put a lot of blue players in the black camp where they once were stalwart blue-white mages in the tradition of Weissman; the back side of Mystical Teachings has done no favors for white-flavored blue-side control.

All in all, that metagame setup created a nice gap for Müller's deck. This is a deck that defies expectation and can catch many opponents off guard. Sacred Mesa was another card that was being played as a high finisher, but was scared off by Sulfur Elemental (kills all your tokens). Now, however, very few are aiming lots of Extirpates at just four Martyrs, and no one at all is on the lookout for Sacred Mesa. End result: A nicely positioned Top 8.

One thing that is interesting to me is that Müller doesn't actually play Proclamation of Rebirth. He has Martyr recursion from Adarkar Valkyrie (a fine finisher). In this deck, Crovax is actually more effective than usual... Instead of just killing an opponent's small dictatorship, the Ascendant Hero does all your Mesa tokans a favor. 

I love a hybrid deck, and this one has the snow engine (including Valkyries and Coldsteel Hearts) on top of the Martyr and creature action. Card advantage is not a problem for Müller; he has Foresee and Compulsive Research both, and can honestly just beat most players with his Sacred Mesa.

For YT half the fun in **Magic** is playing a deck, or contextually effective cards, that the opponent doesn't see coming. This one seems like a lot of fun.
