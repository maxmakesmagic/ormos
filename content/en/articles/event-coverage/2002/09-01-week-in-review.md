I must begin this week’s column by apologizing to my regular readers. You may have noticed that this column has been published very irregularly over the last couple of months. I was extremely busy first opening my store, and then getting it on its feet. Things are finally becoming a little more organized and the business requires slightly less of my attention (though I will still be missing a number of Grand Prix I would travel to otherwise) – and I certainly hope to bring Week In Review back to its weekly status.

### World Championship

![](http://www.wizards.com/tournaments/images/W_SM_1.jpg)It may be old news to most of you now, but World Championships was a very exciting event for those attending, so I would like to recap it briefly in the column. The tournament gathered several hundred participants – the attendance was lower than that of Worlds in Toronto because fewer people were willing to travel to Australia – which is far away from everywhere; it’s even a 12 hour flight from Japan. Somewhat lower tournament attendance was made up for by the enthusiastic locals, who flocked to the event. John Grant, who was manning the DCI booth, commented that far more people stopped by to update their information than have in Toronto.

Worlds kicked off with six rounds of Standard play. As expected, Psychatog was the dominant archetype. The key differences in deck design were how ‘Tog players would utilize the *Judgment* cards to further enhance their deck. Several builds emerged. Some were based on Burning Wish, tutoring for powerful sorceries such as Obliterate and Upheaval. Others utilized Cunning Wish to find all manner of instant tricks from Fact or Fiction to more obscure cards such as Psychotic Haze. Few people stuck to their guns, playing ‘Tog with no Wishes at all. Of these decks, “Burning ‘Tog” had the highest win percentage. Yet, when the numbers were calculated it was the blue-green tempo deck that had the best winning percentage mathematically.

In the middle of Day 1, players and staff took an hour or so to watch the presentation. It included a speech from VP in charge of the **Magic** Brand Joe Hauck, who commented on the state of the game and committed to develop an even better relationship between game designers and players, especially through programs on *Sideboard* and MagicTheGathering.com. His speech was followed by a presentation by an aboriginal art troupe that was actually quite well done – far better than what we saw last year.

Draft day had some exciting matchups. At the end of the day, a rookie player from Malaysia Sim Han How was leading the standings with an 11-1 record. Everyone’s favorite for the tournament was Cole Swannack, a thirteen year old National Champion from New Zealand. A few players were fooled by his small size early on, but quickly learned that he was a serious competitor. Swannack placed in the Top 24 of the tournament overall and appeared to have a good time socializing with the pros. On Sunday, Mike Pustilnik and Zev Gurwitz picked him up to be their third in the team competition, and their team finished in the money. It remains to be seen whether Swannack’s parents will allow him to make the long trip to Pro Tour-Houston. It would be very nice if they did – if Swannack is able to play this well at his young age, could he become the next Finkel or Budde by the time he goes to high school?

*Odyssey* Block Constructed held less surprises than one might have suspected. There were only three distinct archetypes that got commonly played – blue-green (several variants of it in fact), monoblack control, and the blue-white metagame deck played mostly by the Scandinavian contingent. 

When the smoke cleared, we had a diverse Top 8, with four continents and seven countries represented. South America earned its first – and second – Top 8 slot in a Pro level event. Malaysia joined Singapore and Japan as the third Asian country to earn a top 8 slot. In the end, Carlos Romano became the first World Champion from South America, defeating Germany’s Mark Ziegner in the finals. 

![](https://i.ormos.uk/a7/d9/a7d9383c807864b03906a97090b260137c7b7dd92f6ee2b213b965cdbfc9fba6.gif)Despite his last-round defeat, Ziegner had a significant pay day. He and teammates Kai Budde and Felix Schneiders advanced through the ranks on the fourth day of competition to earn the team title, defeating Team U.S.A. in the finals. The Americans surprised the spectators by making a comeback similar to that of last year and fighting their way to the finals – but ran headfirst into a team with Kai in it. On a Sunday. Netherlands – the team most Predicted to win the competition – finished in sixth place. 

There was a number of interesting side events, too. Most notable of them was a $10,000 Australian Open (that’s $5,000 U.S.) – a Standard tournament held during Team Competition day that saw the likes of William Jensen, Katsuhiro Mori, and Antonino de Rosa in the Top 8. 

### Player of the Year Results

![](http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/worlds02/863.jpg)Surprising no one, Kai Budde received his third Player of the Year title. Farid Meraghni became Rookie of the Year despite the fact that he skipped both PT-Osaka and Worlds. Amiel Tenenbaum had a chance to catch up to his countryman, but lost out on tiebreakers to finish ninth at Worlds.

### Grand Prix: Sapporo

An OBC Grand Prix on the northern island of Japan kicked off the 2002-2003 tournament season. Just under 470 players attended. Although most Japanese pro players showed up, there was no international competition for them. Most players were too exhausted after a trip to Australia for another long-distance trip so soon afterward.

<table><tbody><tr><td><img src="http://www.wizards.com/sideboard/images/gpsap02/931.jpg"><br><i> Shinichi Kumagai (right) shaking with Masahiko Morita </i></td></tr></tbody></table>

U-g and monoblack were naturally the two most commonly played decks, yet monoblack was not represented in the Top 8. Instead, two blue-white team Punisher decks made it in. U-g carried the day though, allowing rookie player Shinichi Kumagai to walk away with the trophy. Overall, it was a solid Top 8 featuring Japanese GP staples Masahiko Morita, Satoshi Nakamura and Itaru Ishida. Last year’s Rookie of the Year Katsuhiro Mori finished tenth.

OBC Grand Prix will hit two more continents over the next two weeks. Grand Prix-London will be held at Gen Con UK as it was last year, and then the circuit will travel back to United States with a stop at Cleveland.

“I was playing in a Friday Night **Magic** tournament in the 3rd round, using a ‘Tog deck. My opponent played a rogue r-g beats deck. I was at 4 life and the board looked something like this:

Opponent: Mongrel, Rootwalla, 2x Yavimaya Barbarian, and Anger in the graveyard. 

Me: Familiar, Tog, full graveyard. 

He could have easily swung for the game but he decided to try to add insult to injury, and tapped out to play, of all things, a WORLDGORGER DRAGON. I had Repulse! I let the Dragon’s come into play ability go on the stack, then cast Repulse to bounce it. All of my opponent’s permanents were now removed from the game forever and I swung with Psychatog for the win.”

“In the final round of a Swedish PTQ, two players playing for Top 8 were deck checked. The matchup was u-w-g Solitary Confinement vs. u-g madness, and it turned out the player with the Confinement deck forgot to change his decklist after he made a last-minute change to his sideboard. His penalty was a game loss and he was not allowed to use his sideboard any more. This was particularly bad, since his deck contained 3 Cunning Wish but even worse - 3 Living Wish that he used to get Genesis from his sideboard.

Without them, his ability to lock his opponent with Solitary Confinement was limited to a single Genesis in the entire deck.”

#2 - Courtesy of Yoon Lee:

“This has got to be one of the funniest games I ever played. It took place in a small sealed deck event in Costa Mesa, California. The next round was starting. I checked the listings for my opponent, sat down, but he wasn't there. I called his name and he did not respond, and told the judges. A few minutes later, my opponent I was to play walks in, saying he had a phone call to make. The judges give him a game loss and we go into game two. 

While shuffling I noticed that I did not de-sideboard from the previous round and received a game loss. On to game three. 

So the scenario is that we didn't play each other, both of us cannot sideboard and are in the last game while other people were just starting their first. That is not all. After about 5 turns, he notices he didn't take two of the cards from his card box, so he had an illegal deck of 38 cards. So the game ended with me winning 2-1, without a single point of damage done. At least we both were honest!”
